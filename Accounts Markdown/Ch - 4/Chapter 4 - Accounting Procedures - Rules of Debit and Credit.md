# Class 11 Accountancy – Chapter 4: Rules of Debit and Credit

> **In one line:** Debit is the left side of an account and credit is the right side; to know which side an amount goes on, first place the account in one of five categories, then read off the rule.

---

## 1. Debit and Credit — What the Words Mean

> "In accounting, the terms — debit and credit indicate whether the transactions are to be recorded on the left hand side or right hand side of the account."

- **Debit (Dr.)** = the **left** side of the account.
- **Credit (Cr.)** = the **right** side of the account.
- To put an amount on the left side is to **debit** the account. To put it on the right side is to **credit** it.

The reason the words mean "left" and "right" is the shape of the account. Draw a **T** and you have the two sides of every account. This is called a **T-account**.

```
            Cash Account                       
-----------------------+-----------------------
 Dr. (Left)            | Cr. (Right)           
-----------------------+-----------------------
 Capital  50,000       |     Purchases 10,000  
 Sales    9,000        |     Salary    5,000   
-----------------------+-----------------------
 59,000                |     15,000            
 By Balance c/d 44,000 |                       
-----------------------+-----------------------
```

*Short example: capital introduced ₹50,000, goods bought for cash ₹10,000, salary paid ₹5,000, cash sales ₹9,000. Only the account name is written, as in the NCERT format.*

**Nothing in the words debit or credit tells you whether an amount is an increase or a decrease.** Whether it is depends on the *type* of account. That is what the rest of this chapter is about.

**Exam trap — a direct NCERT comprehension question:**

> *Does debit always mean increase and credit always mean decrease?*

**No.** The answer is no. An increase in a liability is a credit, and an increase in capital is a credit. Debit does not mean increase, and credit does not mean decrease — they only mean left and right.

---

## 2. The Five Account Categories

Before any entry can be written, the account must be placed in one of five groups. NCERT states:

> "All accounts are divided into five categories for the purposes of recording the transactions: (a) Asset (b) Liability (c) Capital (d) Expenses/Losses, and (e) Revenues/Gains."

| Category | What it covers | Normal balance |
|----------|----------------|----------------|
| **Asset** | Cash, bank, stock, debtors, machinery, building | Debit |
| **Liability** | Creditors, bank loan, outstanding salary, amounts received in advance from customers | Credit |
| **Capital** | Owner's capital introduced, profit earned | Credit |
| **Expenses / Losses** | Purchases, salary, rent, depreciation, bad debts | Debit |
| **Revenues / Gains** | Sales, interest received, discount received, gain on sale | Credit |

Most students lose marks here by deciding from the *name* of the account. "Machinery" sounds like a cost but it is an asset. "Purchase of a machine" sounds like purchases but it is a machine. Name the category first, then use the rule.

---

## 3. The Two Fundamental Rules

NCERT groups the five categories into two blocks and states the rules as follows.

### Rule Group 1 — Assets and Expenses/Losses

> "For recording changes in Assets/Expenses (Losses):
> **(i)** Increase in asset is debited, and decrease in asset is credited.
> **(ii)** Increase in expenses/losses is debited, and decrease in expenses/losses is credited."

### Rule Group 2 — Liabilities, Capital and Revenues/Gains

> "For recording changes in Liabilities and Capital/Revenues (Gains):
> **(i)** Increase in liabilities is credited and decrease in liabilities is debited.
> **(ii)** Increase in capital is credited and decrease in capital is debited.
> **(iii)** Increase in revenue/gain is credited and decrease in revenue/gain is debited."

### The complete rule chart

| Account type | Debit when it | Credit when it |
|--------------|---------------|----------------|
| **Asset** | Increases | Decreases |
| **Expense / Loss** | Increases | Decreases |
| **Liability** | Decreases | Increases |
| **Capital** | Decreases | Increases |
| **Revenue / Gain** | Decreases | Increases |

**One sentence to remember the whole chart:** assets and expenses are debited when they go **up**; liabilities, capital and revenue are credited when they go **up**.

## 4. Where the Rules Come From — the Accounting Equation

The rules are not arbitrary. They fall out of one statement, which you met in Chapter 3.

> "Accounting equation signifies that the assets of a business are always equal to the total of its liabilities and capital (owner's equity)."

```
A = L + C

Derivatives used to find a missing figure:
A – L = C        (Capital)
A – C = L        (Liabilities)
```

Here **A** = Assets, **L** = Liabilities, **C** = Capital. Every transaction changes the equation, and those changes are exactly the rules in Section 3.

| What the transaction does | Rule that follows |
|---------------------------|-------------------|
| Asset ↑ and Capital ↑ (owner brings in cash) | Asset debited, Capital credited |
| Expense ↑ and Liability ↑ (goods bought on credit) | Purchases debited, Creditor credited |
| Asset ↑ and Asset ↑ (cash exchanged for a machine) | Machine debited, Cash credited |
| Liability ↓ and Asset ↓ (paid a creditor) | Creditor debited, Cash credited |
| Revenue ↑ and Asset ↑ (goods sold on credit) | Debtor debited, Sales credited |
| Expense ↑ and Asset ↓ (paid salary, rent, freight) | Expense debited, Cash credited |
| Revenue ↑ and Asset ↓ (goods sold for cash) | Cash debited, Sales credited |
| Drawings ↑ and Asset ↓ (owner withdrew goods) | Drawings debited, Purchases credited |

**Worked example.** Ramesh starts a shop, buys goods on credit, then pays a salary.

| Step | Transaction | Assets | Liabilities | Capital | Entry |
|------|-------------|--------|-------------|---------|-------|
| 1 | Cash ₹50,000 introduced | 50,000 | – | 50,000 | Cash Dr, To Capital |
| 2 | Goods ₹10,000 bought on credit | 60,000 | 10,000 | 50,000 | Purchases Dr, To Creditor |
| 3 | Salary ₹5,000 paid in cash | 55,000 | 10,000 | 45,000 | Salary Dr, To Cash |

The equation holds after every step: 60,000 = 10,000 + 50,000, and 55,000 = 10,000 + 45,000.

**Note the second step.** Buying goods on credit is listed as **Expense ↑ and Liability ↑**, because the account debited is *Purchases*, which NCERT treats as an expense. The same rule applies to machinery, but there the account debited is the *Machine* account, an asset — which is why the two are kept as separate rows above.

**The 4-question test that always gives the right answer:**

1. Which **accounts** are affected?
2. Which of the **five categories** does each belong to?
3. Is each going **up** or **down**?
4. Apply the rule from Section 3.

## 5. Practical Rule Table — Including Drawings

In day-to-day work a sixth head is also used, because goods or cash taken out by the owner do not belong to any of the five categories. That head is **Drawings**.

| Account | On increase | On decrease | Normal balance |
|---------|-------------|-------------|----------------|
| Asset | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Expense / Loss | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| **Drawings** | **Debit** | Credit | Debit |
| Liability | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Capital | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Revenue / Gain | Credit | Debit | Credit |

**Memory line:**
> **Dr = A + E + D** (Asset, Expense, Drawings) — **Cr = L + R + C** (Liability, Revenue, Capital).

**Why Drawings behaves like an expense:** taking goods or cash out of the business reduces the owner's claim by exactly that amount, in the same way an expense does. NCERT records the transaction as `Drawings A/c Dr, To Cash A/c` and transfers the Drawings account to Capital only at the end of the accounting year.

**Exam note:** NCERT's five categories do **not** include Drawings — the word appears in its journal entries and in a classification question, but there is no rule or chart for it. Several coaching books add Drawings as a sixth head and call the result "the six rules of debit and credit". Both describe the same table.

## 6. The Rules Applied — Worked Entries

Each row shows the category analysis first, then the entry. Learn the analysis, because marks are for the analysis.

| # | Transaction | Category analysis | Entry |
|---|-------------|-------------------|-------|
| 1 | Owner introduces cash ₹50,000 | Asset ↑, Capital ↑ | **Cash A/c Dr 50,000 To Capital A/c 50,000** |
| 2 | Goods purchased on credit ₹20,000 | Expense ↑, Liability ↑ | **Purchases A/c Dr 20,000 To Sohan A/c 20,000** |
| 3 | Goods purchased for cash ₹8,000 | Expense ↑, Asset ↓ | **Purchases A/c Dr 8,000 To Cash A/c 8,000** |
| 4 | Machine purchased for cash ₹30,000 | Asset ↑, Asset ↓ | **Machine A/c Dr 30,000 To Cash A/c 30,000** |
| 5 | Machine purchased on credit ₹50,000 | Asset ↑, Liability ↑ | **Machine A/c Dr 50,000 To Supplier A/c 50,000** |
| 6 | Goods sold on credit ₹25,000 | Asset ↑, Revenue ↑ | **Ramesh A/c Dr 25,000 To Sales A/c 25,000** |
| 7 | Full amount received from Ramesh | Asset ↑, Asset ↓ | **Cash A/c Dr 25,000 To Ramesh A/c 25,000** |
| 8 | Paid Sohan ₹20,000 in full | Liability ↓, Asset ↓ | **Sohan A/c Dr 20,000 To Cash A/c 20,000** |
| 9 | Salary paid ₹5,000 in cash | Expense ↑, Asset ↓ | **Salary A/c Dr 5,000 To Cash A/c 5,000** |
| 10 | Salary outstanding ₹2,000 | Expense ↑, Liability ↑ | **Salary A/c Dr 2,000 To Outstanding Salary A/c 2,000** |
| 11 | Freight ₹500 paid on goods purchased for resale | Expense ↑, Asset ↓ | **Freight A/c Dr 500 To Cash A/c 500** |
| 12 | Depreciation ₹3,000 charged on machine | Expense ↑, Asset ↓ | **Depreciation A/c Dr 3,000 To Machine A/c 3,000** |
| 13 | Cash deposited into bank ₹20,000 | Asset ↑, Asset ↓ | **Bank A/c Dr 20,000 To Cash A/c 20,000** |
| 14 | Interest received ₹500 | Asset ↑, Revenue ↑ | **Cash A/c Dr 500 To Interest A/c 500** |
| 15 | Bad debts ₹1,000 written off | Expense ↑, Asset ↓ | **Bad Debts A/c Dr 1,000 To Debtors A/c 1,000** |
| 16 | Owner withdrew goods ₹4,000 for home | Drawings ↑, Asset ↓ | **Drawings A/c Dr 4,000 To Purchases A/c 4,000** |
| 17 | Owner withdrew cash ₹3,000 for personal use | Drawings ↑, Asset ↓ | **Drawings A/c Dr 3,000 To Cash A/c 3,000** |

### Six of these deserve extra attention

**Row 6 — a sale.** The journal entry is one line: Debtor Dr, Sales Cr. But in the *analysis table* the same transaction has **three** effects, not two. In NCERT's own worked example, goods costing ₹25,000 sold for ₹35,000 on credit gives: **Stock −₹25,000, Debtors +₹35,000, and Capital +₹10,000** (the profit). The asset side moves in two directions at once, and the capital side moves as well. Students who write only one line in the analysis lose the marks even when the journal entry is right.

**Row 7 — money received from a debtor.** This is **not income**. The income was already recorded on the day of the credit sale in row 6. Collecting the debt only converts one asset (debtor) into another (cash), so both sides of the entry are assets.

**Row 10 — outstanding salary.** The expense belongs to this year and is debited to Salary. The unpaid part is a liability, so it is credited to Outstanding Salary. The same logic gives **Rent Dr, To Outstanding Rent** and **Insurance Premium Dr, To Outstanding Insurance Premium**.

**Row 11 — freight.** Freight on goods bought **for resale** is an expense, because it is the cost of getting the goods to market. Freight on a **fixed asset** is different — that is part of the cost of the asset, so the asset account is debited instead.

**Row 15 — bad debts.** A debtor who will never pay is no longer an asset. The loss is an expense, and the debtor account falls, so the entry is Bad Debts Dr, To Debtors.

**Row 16 — goods withdrawn.** The entry debits **Drawings** and credits **Purchases A/c**. Capital is **not** credited at the time of the transaction; the Drawings account is transferred to Capital only at the end of the year.

## 7. The Ledger Account and Balancing

### Two accounts from one set of transactions

Business started with cash ₹50,000. Goods were then bought for cash ₹10,000 and on credit ₹20,000 from Sohan. Salary ₹5,000 was paid in cash, cash sales were ₹9,000, and Sohan was then paid in full.

```
            Cash Account              +                       Sohan (Creditor) Account
-----------------------+-----------------------+-----------------------+-----------------------
Dr. (Left)             |Cr. (Right)             + Dr. (Left)             |Cr. (Right)            
-----------------------+-----------------------+-----------------------+-----------------------
 Capital  50,000       |     Purchases 10,000   + To Balance c/d 20,000  |     Purchases 20,000  
 Sales    9,000        |     Salary    5,000    +                        |                       
 Sohan   20,000        |     Sohan    20,000    +                        |                       
-----------------------+-----------------------+-----------------------+-----------------------
 59,000                |     35,000             +  20,000                |     20,000            
By Balance c/d 24,000  |                        +                        |                       
-----------------------+-----------------------+-----------------------+-----------------------
```

The Cash Account is an asset, so it carries a **debit balance** of ₹24,000. Sohan is a creditor, so that account carries a **credit balance** of ₹20,000, and its `Balance c/d` therefore sits on the debit side.

### How an account is balanced

> "Balancing of an account means that the two sides are totaled and the difference between them is shown on the side, which is shorter in order to make their totals equal."

So the balance always goes on the **lighter** side:

| Situation | Where the difference is written | Word used | Type of balance |
|-----------|---------------------------------|-----------|-----------------|
| Debit side total is **more** | On the **credit** side | `By Balance c/d` | Debit balance |
| Credit side total is **more** | On the **debit** side | `To Balance c/d` | Credit balance |

- `c/d` = carried down. The balance is written at the end of the period and **carried down to the next period** as `Balance b/d`, always on the opposite side.
- If both sides are already equal there is **no balance at all** — nothing is written, and the account is simply totalled and closed.

**Check on the two accounts above:**

| Account | Debit total | Credit total | Difference | Written as | Balance type |
|---------|-------------|--------------|------------|------------|--------------|
| **Cash** | 59,000 | 35,000 | 24,000 | By Balance c/d on credit side | Debit balance |
| **Sohan** | 20,000 | 20,000 | Nil | Nil — account closed | Nil balance |

**Nominal accounts are not balanced.** NCERT: "the accounts of expenses losses and gains/revenues are not balanced but are closed by transferring to trading and profit and loss account." These are the *temporary* accounts, against the *permanent* accounts (assets, liabilities, capital), which are all balanced and carried forward.

**Cash column always shows a debit balance,** because cash payments can never exceed what was received, added to the cash in hand at the start of the period. The **bank column is the exception** — it can show a credit balance when the bank account is overdrawn.

## 8. The Journal Format and the "To" / "By" Convention

The two words behave differently in the journal and in the ledger, and mixing them up is the most common error in this unit.

### In the Journal

NCERT prescribes a **five-column** journal:

```
Date    | Particulars                       | L.F.  | Debit   | Credit
--------+-----------------------------------+-------+---------+-----
Mar 1   | Cash A/c Dr.                      |       | 50,000  |         
        |     To Capital A/c                |       |         | 50,000  
--------+-----------------------------------+-------+---------+-----
Mar 4   | Purchases A/c Dr.                 |       | 20,000  |         
        |     To Sohan A/c                  |       |         | 20,000  
--------+-----------------------------------+-------+---------+-----
Mar 5   | Purchases A/c Dr.                 |       | 8,000   |         
        |     To Cash A/c                   |       |         | 8,000   
--------+-----------------------------------+-------+---------+-----
Mar 7   | Machine A/c Dr.                   |       | 30,000  |         
        |     To Cash A/c                   |       |         | 30,000  
--------+-----------------------------------+-------+---------+-----
```

The rules:

- The account to be **debited** is written on the **first line**, beginning from the left hand corner, and the word **`Dr.`** is written at the end of the column.
- The account to be **credited** is written on the **second line**, leaving a margin on the left, with a prefix **`To`**.
- A **Narration** in brackets follows the account titles, and a line is drawn under it to mark the end of the entry.
- **L.F. (Ledger Folio)** records the ledger page on which the account appears. It is filled in at the time of posting, not when the entry is made.
- There is **no "By" anywhere in a journal entry.**

### In the Ledger

NCERT's ledger format (Fig. 3.6) is `Date | Particulars | J.F. | Amount` on both sides. Note the folio column here is **J.F. (Journal Folio)**, the reverse of the journal — it records the page of the original book of entry.

- "Dr. means Debit side of the account that is left side and Cr. means Credit side of the account, i.e. right side."
- "Particulars: Name of the item with reference to the original book of entry is written on debit/credit side of the account."

Many Indian textbooks (DK Goel, TS Grewal) add a `To` / `By` prefix to the particulars column as a reading aid. The convention used is: **`To`** before the account name written on the **debit** side, and **`By`** before the account name written on the **credit** side. **NCERT does not state this rule, and NCERT's own ledger illustrations omit the prefixes entirely** — they write only "Cash", "Bank", "Sales", "Balance c/d". Note that the words describe the *side* of the ledger, not whether the account itself is debited or credited.

| | Journal | Ledger |
|--|---------|--------|
| Debited account written | First line, `Dr.` at end of column | On the debit (left) side |
| Credited account written | Second line, prefixed `To` | On the credit (right) side |
| "By" used? | No | Only under the DK Goel / TS Grewal convention |
| Folio column | **L.F.** — Ledger Folio | **J.F.** — Journal Folio |
| Order of entries | Chronological | Analytical, grouped by account |

## 9. Purchases or Asset? The Most-Tested Trap

A single rule decides this, and it depends on what is being bought.

| What is bought | Account debited | Reason |
|----------------|-----------------|--------|
| **Goods** for resale | **Purchases** | "Although the transaction results in an increase in stock of goods, the account debited is purchases, not goods." |
| **A machine**, furniture, building, vehicle | **The asset account** | It is fixed property, not stock |

**Why the goods account is split.** NCERT: "the goods account is divided into five accounts, viz. purchases account, sales account, purchases returns account, sales returns account, and stock account." So *purchases* is one of five separate accounts that together track the goods account — which is why a goods purchase is never entered in an account called "Goods".

**Two CBSE questions to recognise:**

- *A purchase of machine for cash should be debited to:* Cash / **Machine account** / Purchase account / None.
- *Cash withdrawn by the proprietor should be credited to:* Drawings / Capital / Profit and Loss / **Cash account**.

Both answers follow straight from the rules. In the second question, Drawings is debited because it is debit-natured, and the cash account is credited because the asset is leaving.

## 10. Common Mistakes in the Exam

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | Correct approach |
|---------|-----------------|------------------|
| Treating debit as meaning "increase" | Debit only means the left side | Find the category first, then the rule |
| Labelling a goods purchase as "Asset ↑" | The account debited is Purchases, an expense | Analysis is **Expense ↑**, entry `Purchases A/c Dr` |
| Crediting Capital when the owner withdraws goods | Capital is not touched at transaction time | **Drawings Dr**, To Cash or To Purchases |
| Collecting a debtor's amount and calling it income | Income was recorded on the credit sale | **Cash Dr, To Debtor** — both sides are assets |
| Debiting a machine purchase to Purchases | A machine is a fixed asset | **Machine A/c Dr** |
| Writing only one line for a sale in the analysis table | The equation changes in three places | Stock ↓, Debtor ↑, Capital ↑ |
| Writing "By" in a journal entry | Journals have no "By" | Use `To` for the credited account, `Dr.` at end of first line |
| Putting `L.F.` in the ledger | The ledger folio is the journal page | Journal uses **L.F.**, ledger uses **J.F.** |
| Balancing a Sales or Salary account | Nominal accounts are not balanced | Total them and close to Trading / P&L |
| Saying cash book always has a debit balance | True of the cash column only | The bank column can show a credit balance (overdraft) |
| Treating freight on a machine as an expense | It is part of the asset's cost | Debit the asset account |

**Golden rule:** when stuck, ask three questions in order — *which account, which of the five categories, up or down?* The rule follows, and the side is then fixed.

## 📝 Key Terms to Remember

| Term | Simple Meaning |
|------|----------------|
| **Debit (Dr.)** | The left side of an account |
| **Credit (Cr.)** | The right side of an account |
| **T-account** | The account drawn in the shape of the letter T |
| **Five categories** | Asset, Liability, Capital, Expense/Loss, Revenue/Gain |
| **Asset increase** | Debit |
| **Asset decrease** | Credit |
| **Expense / Loss increase** | Debit |
| **Liability increase** | Credit |
| **Liability decrease** | Debit |
| **Capital increase** | Credit |
| **Revenue / Gain increase** | Credit |
| **Drawings** | Owner's withdrawal; Drawings Dr, asset Cr |
| **Dr = A + E + D** | Asset, Expense, Drawings — debit nature |
| **Cr = L + R + C** | Liability, Revenue, Capital — credit nature |
| **Accounting equation** | A = L + C; derivatives A – L = C, A – C = L |
| **Permanent accounts** | Assets, liabilities, capital — all balanced and carried forward |
| **Temporary accounts** | Revenue and expense — closed to Trading / P&L, never balanced |
| **Balancing an account** | Difference written on the shorter side to make totals equal |
| **Balance c/d** | Closing balance, carried down to the next period |
| **Balance b/d** | That balance brought down at the start of the next period |
| **Debit balance** | Written on the credit side as `By Balance c/d` |
| **Credit balance** | Written on the debit side as `To Balance c/d` |
| **L.F. (Ledger Folio)** | Journal column — page of the ledger account |
| **J.F. (Journal Folio)** | Ledger column — page of the original book of entry |
| **Narration** | The bracket description below the accounts in a journal entry |
| **Journal** | Book of original entry; `Dr.` then `To`, no `By` |
| **Ledger** | Principal book; analytical, chronological order is for the journal |

## 🔄 Quick Recap

```
Debit = LEFT side. Credit = RIGHT side. Nothing more than that.

Debit does NOT always mean increase. Increase in liability and
increase in capital are both credits.  [NCERT comprehension question]

Five categories: Asset | Liability | Capital | Expense/Loss | Revenue/Gain

Two fundamental rules:
  Assets / Expenses (Losses):          increase = Debit, decrease = Credit
  Liabilities / Capital / Revenues:    increase = Credit, decrease = Debit

Add a sixth head for practical work:
  Drawings: increase = Debit, decrease = Credit

Memory: Dr = A + E + D.   Cr = L + R + C.

Accounting equation: A = L + C  ->  A - L = C,  A - C = L

4-question test: which account -> which category -> up or down -> apply rule.

Key entries:
  Cash from debtor      Cash Dr, To Debtor        (NOT income)
  Goods on credit       Purchases Dr, To Creditor  (Expense up, not Asset)
  Machine on credit     Machine Dr, To Supplier   (Asset up)
  Outstanding salary    Salary Dr, To Outstanding Salary
  Depreciation          Depreciation Dr, To Machine
  Owner withdraws       Drawings Dr, To Cash / To Purchases (Capital untouched)
  Bad debts             Bad Debts Dr, To Debtors
  Freight on resale     Freight Dr, To Cash  (on a machine: debit the machine)

A sale: entry is ONE line (Debtor Dr, Sales Cr), but the ANALYSIS has THREE
effects - Stock down, Debtor up, Capital up by the profit.

Balancing: difference goes on the SHORTER side.
  Debit total more  -> By Balance c/d on credit side -> debit balance
  Credit total more -> To Balance c/d on debit side  -> credit balance
  Both sides equal  -> no balance, nothing written.
  Nominal accounts are never balanced - closed to Trading / P&L.
  Cash column always debit balance; bank column can be credit (overdraft).

Journal: 5 columns Date|Particulars|L.F.|Debit|Credit.
  Dr. at end of first line, To for the credited account, NO By.
Ledger: Date|Particulars|J.F.|Amount. 'To'/'By' prefixes are a
  DK Goel / TS Grewal convention, not an NCERT rule.
```

## ❓ Important Exam Questions

**Short Questions (2-3 marks):**
1. What do debit and credit mean? Why are they written Dr. and Cr.?
2. Name the five categories of accounts used for recording transactions.
3. State the two fundamental rules of debit and credit with all their sub-rules.
4. Does debit always mean increase and credit always mean decrease? Justify your answer.
5. State the rule for debiting and crediting an asset account.
6. State the rule for debiting and crediting a liability account.
7. How is an account balanced? Where is a debit balance written?
8. Why are nominal accounts not balanced? What are they closed into?
9. When is cash received from a debtor treated as income? Answer with reasoning.
10. What is the difference between the Ledger Folio and the Journal Folio?

**Long Questions (4-6 marks):**
1. Explain the meaning of debit and credit. Does debit always mean increase? Explain with examples.
2. State and explain the two fundamental rules of debit and credit with a chart of all five categories.
3. Explain the accounting equation and show how the rules of debit and credit follow from it.
4. Prepare a table showing, for eight transactions, the accounts affected, their type, whether they increase or decrease, and the entry.
5. Explain the balancing of an account. What is written in place of balance c/d and balance b/d? Why are nominal accounts not balanced?
6. Distinguish between the journal and the ledger with reference to the format of entries, the use of Dr., Cr., To and By, and the folio column.
7. Distinguish between a purchase of goods and a purchase of a machine as entries, showing which account is debited in each case and why.
