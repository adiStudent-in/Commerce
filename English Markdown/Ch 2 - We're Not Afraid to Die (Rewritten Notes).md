## 1. The Dream & the Boat

In July 1976 the narrator — a 37-year-old businessman — set sail from Plymouth, England, with his wife Mary, six-year-old son Jonathan and seven-year-old daughter Suzanne, to duplicate the round-the-world voyage made 200 years earlier by Captain James Cook. Mary and he had dreamt of sailing in the wake of the famous explorer for the longest time, and for 16 years they had spent all their leisure time **honing their seafaring skills** in British waters. Their boat, **Wavewalker**, was a 23-metre, 30-ton wooden-hulled beauty, professionally built, and they had spent months fitting it out and testing it in the roughest weather they could find.

---

## 2. Into the Storm

The first leg of their planned three-year, 105,000-kilometre journey passed pleasantly down the west coast of Africa to Cape Town. There, before heading east, they took on two crewmen to help tackle one of the world's roughest seas, the southern Indian Ocean — **Larry Vigil**, an American, and **Herb Seigler**, a Swiss. From the second day out of Cape Town, strong gales blew continuously for weeks; the gales did not worry the captain, but the waves were alarming — up to **15 metres, as high as the main mast**. On December 25, 3,500 kilometres east of Cape Town, they still enjoyed a wonderful Christmas complete with a tree despite the atrocious weather. New Year's Day brought no improvement, but they reasoned the weather had to change soon. It did — **for the worse**.

---

## 3. January 2 — The Great Wave Strikes

At dawn on January 2 the waves were gigantic. Sailing with only a small storm jib and still making eight knots, the captain slowed the boat: he dropped the storm jib, lashed a heavy mooring rope in a loop across the stern, double-lashed everything, went through the life-raft drill, attached lifelines, donned oilskins and life jackets — and waited. At about 6 p.m. came the first indication of impending disaster: an **ominous silence**. The wind dropped, the sky grew dark, then a growing roar rose and an enormous cloud towered aft — which with horror he realised was **not a cloud but a wave**, perfectly vertical, almost twice the height of the others, with a frightful breaking crest. As the stern climbed its face, a tremendous explosion shook the deck: a torrent of green and white water broke over the ship, his head smashed into the wheel, and he flew overboard, sinking below the waves. He accepted his approaching death and, losing consciousness, felt quite peaceful. Unexpectedly his head popped out — Wavewalker was near capsizing, masts almost horizontal, until another wave hurled her upright; his lifeline jerked taut, he grabbed the guard rails, crashed into the main boom, and was tossed around the deck like a rag doll. His **left ribs cracked**, his mouth filled with blood and broken teeth — yet somehow he found the wheel, lined up the stern for the next wave and hung on.

---

## 4. Water, Water Everywhere

He could feel water below but dared not abandon the wheel — until the front hatch flew open and Mary screamed, **"We're sinking! The decks are smashed; we're full of water."** Below, Larry and Herb were pumping like madmen amid broken timbers hanging at crazy angles, the whole starboard side bulged inwards, and clothes, crockery, charts, tins and toys sloshed in deep water. The children were safe on an upper bunk — though Sue pointed to a big bump above her eyes; there was no time to worry about bumped heads. With hammer, screws and canvas the captain stretched canvas and secured waterproof hatch covers across the gaping holes, deflecting most of the water over the side. Then more trouble: the hand pumps blocked with floating debris, the electric pump short-circuited, and their two spare hand pumps had been wrenched overboard — along with the forestay sail, the jib, the dinghies and the main anchor. Only a third electric pump found under the chartroom floor saved them. The night dragged on in a bitterly cold routine of pumping, steering and radio — with **no replies to their Mayday calls** in that remote corner of the world. Through it all, Sue's head swelled alarmingly with two enormous black eyes and a deep cut on her arm; when asked why she hadn't spoken earlier, she said, **"I didn't want to worry you when you were trying to save us all."**

---

## 5. January 3–4 — Damage Report & New Hope

By the morning of January 3 the pumps had the water under control enough for two hours' rest in rotation. But inspection revealed a tremendous leak below the waterline: nearly all the main rib frames were **smashed down to the keel**, and nothing held up a whole section of the starboard hull except a few cupboard partitions. They had survived 15 hours since the wave hit, but Wavewalker would never reach Australia. Checking the charts, the captain found two small islands a few hundred kilometres east — **Ile Amsterdam**, a French scientific base. Their only hope was to reach these **pinpricks in the vast ocean**. On January 4, after **36 hours of continuous pumping**, they pumped out the last few centimetres of water; since sail on the main mast would tear the damaged hull apart, they hoisted the storm jib and headed for the islands. Mary found corned beef and cracker biscuits — their **first meal in almost two days**.

---

## 6. January 5 — "We Aren't Afraid of Dying"

The respite was short-lived: at 4 p.m. black clouds built up, within an hour the wind was back to 40 knots, and by dawn on January 5 their situation was again desperate. When the captain went to comfort the children, Jon asked, **"Daddy, are we going to die?"** — and then added the words that gave the story its title: *"But, Daddy, we aren't afraid of dying if we can all be together — you and Mummy, Sue and I."* Speechless, the captain left the cabin **determined to fight the sea with everything he had**. To shield the weakened starboard side he hove-to, keeping the undamaged port hull toward the waves with an improvised **sea anchor** of heavy nylon rope and two 22-litre paraffin barrels. That evening Mary and he sat holding hands while more and more water poured through the broken planks — both feeling the end was very near.

---

## 7. January 6 — Landfall at Ile Amsterdam

But Wavewalker rode out the storm. On the morning of January 6, with the wind easing, the captain took a sextant reading and calculated wind speeds, courses, drift and current — knowing they had lost the main compass and were using a spare uncorrected for magnetic variation, somewhere in **150,000 square kilometres of ocean looking for a 65-kilometre-wide island**. At 2 p.m. he asked Larry to steer 185 degrees, promising — with a conviction he did not feel — land by about 5 p.m. Then, with a heavy heart, he dozed off, waking at 6 p.m. sure they had missed the island. Just then a **tousled head** appeared by his bunk: *"Can I have a hug?"* asked Jonathan — *"Because you are the best daddy in the whole world — and the best captain."* And Sue said matter-of-factly, *"You found the island. It's out there in front of us, as big as a battleship."* He rushed on deck and gazed with relief at the stark outline of Ile Amsterdam — only a bleak piece of volcanic rock with little vegetation, yet **the most beautiful island in the world**. Anchored offshore for the night, they were cheered ashore next morning by all 28 inhabitants. On land, his thoughts went to Larry and Herb, cheerful and optimistic under the direst stress; to Mary, who steered through the crucial hours; and most of all to a seven-year-old girl who hid a head injury (which later needed six minor operations for a recurring blood clot) and a six-year-old boy **who was not afraid to die**.

## 8. Exam Corner 📝✅

### Long Answer (Exam-Ready)

*"Describe the hazard faced by the voyagers on board the Wavewalker and how they escaped death."*

In July 1976, the narrator, his wife Mary and their two young children set sail from Plymouth to duplicate Captain James Cook's round-the-world voyage in their specially built boat Wavewalker, aided by crewmen Larry Vigil and Herb Seigler. On January 2, in the southern Indian Ocean, they met gigantic waves; that evening an ominous silence was followed by a perfectly vertical wave, almost twice the height of the rest, which smashed over the ship. The captain was thrown overboard, cracked his ribs and lost teeth, while the decks burst open and the starboard hull caved in.

What followed was a night-and-day battle against the sea. With pumps blocking, breaking and short-circuiting, and spares wrenched overboard, the family pumped, steered and repaired in rotation — the captain stretching canvas over gaping holes and rigging a third electric pump found under the chartroom floor. Their Mayday calls went unanswered in that remote corner of the world. Yet no one panicked: Sue quietly bore a severe head injury "to not worry" her parents, and little Jon declared, "We aren't afraid of dying if we can all be together."

Hope came in the form of Ile Amsterdam, a French base a few hundred kilometres east — a pinprick in 150,000 square kilometres of ocean. After 36 hours of pumping, with a storm jib and imperfect compass readings, the captain steered 185 degrees and, guided by his children's joyful announcement, sighted the island "as big as a battleship." All 28 inhabitants cheered them ashore. Preparation, presence of mind, optimism and family unity together defeated what seemed certain death.

### Expected Questions & Pointers ✅

**Q1. List the steps taken by the captain (i) to protect the ship when rough weather began, (ii) to check the flooding.**
→ (i) Dropped the storm jib; lashed heavy mooring rope in a loop across the stern; double-lashed everything; life-raft drill; attached lifelines; donned oilskins and life jackets. Later hove-to with port hull facing waves using an improvised sea anchor (nylon rope + paraffin barrels).
→ (ii) Stretched canvas and fixed waterproof hatch covers over gaping holes; operated hand pumps; rigged the spare electric pump under the chartroom floor; pumped continuously — 36 hours non-stop.

**Q2. Describe the mental condition of the voyagers on 4 and 5 January.**
→ Jan 4: exhausted but relieved after 36 hours of pumping; ate their first meal in two days; brief hope aiming for Ile Amsterdam. Jan 5: respite gone — wind back to 40 knots, situation desperate; Jon asked "are we going to die?"; parents sat holding hands feeling the end near, yet the captain resolved to fight the sea with everything he had.

**Q3. Describe the shifts in narration in the three sections of the text; give each a subtitle.**
→ Section 1 (dream voyage → the great wave and injuries): *"A Dream Voyage Turns Nightmare"* — calm, confident tone turning to horror. Section 2 (battle against flooding, Sue's hidden injury and card): *"Pumping for Life"* — tense, breathless action. Section 3 (navigation, despair, landfall): *"Land Ho! — The Reward of Courage"* — weary, then relieved and reflective.

**Q4. What difference did you notice between the reactions of the adults and the children when faced with danger?**
→ Adults showed fear through tireless work — pumping, repairing, calculating, silently preparing for death. Children showed astonishing calm and innocence: Sue hid her injuries so as not to worry them and made a thank-you card; Jon spoke of dying without fear "if we can all be together" and hugged his father as "the best daddy and best captain."

**Q5. How does the story suggest that optimism helps to endure "the direst stress"?**
→ Larry and Herb stayed "cheerful and optimistic under the direst stress"; the captain never abandoned calculation and navigation even without conviction; Sue's card revived the family's spirit; hope of reaching the island kept every pump stroke going — optimism literally kept the boat afloat.

**Q6. Why do people undertake such adventurous expeditions in spite of the risks involved?**
→ For the thrill of adventure, to fulfil lifelong dreams (sailing in Captain Cook's wake), to test the limits of human endurance, and for the deep satisfaction of conquering nature — risks accepted because careful preparation and skill reduce them.

**Q7. Why had Sue not mentioned her injuries earlier?**
→ She had a big bump, two black eyes and a deep cut, but said, "I didn't want to worry you when you were trying to save us all" — a maturity and selflessness far beyond her seven years.

## 10. Theme & Message 💡

- **Courage and presence of mind** — calm thinking, repair work and precise navigation turn certain death into survival.
- **Optimism is fuel** — staying "cheerful and optimistic under the direst stress" kept the pumps running and hope alive.
- **Family unity defeats fear** — the title itself: "we aren't afraid of dying if we can all be together."
- **Children can be the strongest** — Sue hides her pain; Jon's innocent words give his father new determination.
- **Preparation matters** — 16 years of honing seafaring skills and a professionally built boat made survival possible.
- **Never-say-die spirit** — man may not conquer the sea, but he can refuse to surrender to it.
