## 1. The Portrait of a Lady — Khushwant Singh — 8 Questions

### Q1. Why does the narrator describe the thought of his grandmother having once been young and pretty as "almost revolting"?
**Answer:** The author had known his grandmother as old for twenty years — short, fat, slightly bent, face a criss-cross of wrinkles, silver hair scattered over a pale puckered face, always in spotless white, one hand on her waist, the other telling beads of her rosary with lips in inaudible prayer. This image was so fixed that youth felt impossible. Even the grandfather's portrait with big turban and long beard looked a hundred years old, a man who could only have grandchildren. "Revolting" does not mean dislike; it shows how memory freezes a loved person in one form, and why the author adds she could never be pretty but was always beautiful in serenity, like the winter landscape breathing peace and contentment.

### Q2. How does the move from the village temple school to the city English school become the turning point in their friendship?
**Answer:** In the village they were constantly together — she woke, bathed and dressed him, plastered his wooden slate with yellow chalk, gave an earthen ink-pot and red pen, fed him thick stale chapatti with butter and sugar, walked him to the school attached to the temple where the priest taught alphabet and morning prayer while she read scriptures, and fed village dogs on return. In the city parents sent for them, he went by motor bus to an English school, she could no longer accompany him or help with English words, law of gravity, Archimedes' Principle and the round world. She was distressed there was no teaching about God and scriptures. Shared routine broke, though they shared a room, and distance quietly grew.

### Q3. Why did music lessons disturb the grandmother more than English science?
**Answer:** She already disbelieved English science and was unhappy it lacked God, but music shocked her values. To her, music had lewd associations — monopoly of harlots and beggars, not meant for gentlefolk, and in temples only for monks to sing. For a deeply religious woman whose life was prayer, spinning and scriptures, music as a school subject meant the family was abandoning decency. She said nothing, but her silence was disapproval, and she rarely talked after that. It reveals the core clash of tradition versus modernity: she accepted change quietly but could not approve what her culture coded as low.

### Q4. How does the grandmother's life after the narrator gets his own university room show acceptance with dignity, not defeat?
**Answer:** When he went to university and got a room of his own, the common link snapped. She accepted seclusion with resignation — rarely leaving her spinning-wheel, spinning from sunrise to sunset while reciting prayers, with only an afternoon recess to feed sparrows. Hundreds of birds collected, perching on legs, shoulders and even head while she smiled and never shooed them away; it was her happiest half-hour. Instead of complaining, she built a disciplined spiritual routine. This resignation is strength: she withdraws without bitterness, finding solace in prayer and service to birds, preserving self-respect till the end.

### Q5. Justify the title "The Portrait of a Lady" — why is it a portrait, and why "Lady"?
**Answer:** It is not a painted portrait but a verbal one — precise physical and moral detailing: short, fat, bent, wrinkles everywhere, white clothes, waist-hand for stoop, rosary-hand, silent lips, winter-landscape serenity. Through three phases — village companionship, city distance, university separation and death — the author completes the likeness. "Lady" is respectful: despite poverty and illiteracy, she lives with grace, faith, kindness to dogs and sparrows, calm farewells and a peaceful death. The word lifts a simple grandmother to universal dignity, making the tribute affectionate, truthful and nostalgic, not sentimental.

### Q6. Why is the grandmother's death described as peaceful and fulfilled rather than tragic?
**Answer:** On return after his five-year abroad stay, she received him at the station, clasped him reciting prayers, and that evening for the first time since he knew her did not pray — collecting neighbourhood women, thumping a dilapidated drum and singing songs of homecoming warriors for hours till persuaded to stop for overstraining. Next morning mild fever came, the doctor said nothing to worry, but she declared her end near, refused to waste time talking, lay prostrate praying and telling beads till lips stopped moving and rosary fell from lifeless fingers, pallor spreading. Having celebrated closure, she chooses prayer over talk — a willed, dignified exit.

### Q7. What is the significance of the thousands of sparrows sitting in total silence and refusing bread after her death?
**Answer:** In the city sparrows replaced village dogs as her companions. That evening thousands sat scattered on floor and verandah up to the red-shrouded corpse, with no chirruping. The author's mother broke bread into crumbs as grandmother did, but they took no notice; when the corpse was carried off they flew away quietly, and next morning the sweeper swept crumbs into dustbin. The refusal proves their bond was personal, not food-based. Nature mourns wordlessly, mirroring human mourning. It elevates her purity: even birds loved her soul, and silence honours silence — her life of silent prayer ends in silent tribute.

### Q8. How does the contrast between village dogs and city sparrows trace the story's emotional arc?
**Answer:** Dogs belong to the phase of togetherness — they met them at the temple door, growling and fighting for stale chapattis thrown on the way back, shared activity of grandmother and grandson. Sparrows belong to city distance — no dogs existed, so she fed them alone in the courtyard while the boy was at bus-school, a solitary joy. Dogs equal companionship; sparrows equal solace in seclusion and later spiritual kinship. The arc moves from shared feeding to lonely feeding to final mourning by sparrows, showing love persisting even as human closeness snaps.

## 2. A Photograph — Shirley Toulson — 8 Questions

### Q1. Why does the poet stress the "cardboard" that shows the past, and what does it suggest about memory?
**Answer:** Cardboard is the stiff backing of the old photo frame — flimsy, cheap, perishable — yet it preserves a sea-beach moment from before the poet was born: two cousins Betty and Dolly paddling, each holding one hand of the mother, the big girl of twelve. The contrast matters: a fragile object holds an irrecoverable time. The poet can know his mother's childhood only through this picture. It introduces the poem's theme — man is terribly transient, but art/memory briefly fixes what life lets go, even as the holder himself will pass.

### Q2. How does the contrast between the sea and human feet carry the poem's philosophy?
**Answer:** All three stood still smiling through hair at the uncle with the camera; the mother had a sweet face from before the poet's birth. Then the poet notes the sea, which appears to have changed less, washed their terribly transient feet. Sea equals permanence, continuity; feet equal brief human life, quickly gone. "Terribly" stresses fear and pity at mortality. Nature outlives its visitors. This single image prepares the later loss: the holiday fades, laughter fades, the mother dies — only the larger sea-like silence remains.

### Q3. Why does the poet say the sea holiday was her past, while her laughter became his past?
**Answer:** Some twenty-thirty years later the grown mother would laugh at the snapshot — "See Betty and Dolly, look how they dressed us for the beach." For her, the beach itself was memory; for the poet, who never saw it, only her laughing recall is memory. Both are wry with laboured ease of loss — smile twisted by sorrow, grief accepted with effort. It shows memory passes in layers: event becomes story, story becomes echo. After her death nearly as many years as the girl lived, even laughter is gone, leaving silence.

### Q4. Explain "terribly transient feet" — why feet, why terribly?
**Answer:** Feet are what touched the sea, stood for the camera, walked through life — the most mortal, moving part. Calling them transient means short-lived, soon erased, unlike the sea. "Terribly" adds dread: transience is not neutral but painful. The girls who paddled grew, aged and died; the sea still washes other feet. The phrase condenses the poem: human loveliness is real yet doomed, and the photograph cruelly proves it by freezing youth beside an unchanging sea.

### Q5. How does the poem's three-part time structure deepen the sense of loss?
**Answer:** First, the captured moment — twelve-year-old mother with cousins, uncle's camera, sweet face. Second, twenty-thirty years later — adult mother laughing at dress and holiday, poet listening. Third, now — mother dead nearly as many years as the girl lived, poet speechless. Each stage removes one layer: presence becomes recollection, recollection becomes absence. Time moves forward, life shortens. The structure teaches that loss is cumulative; what remains is not the person but the silence about them.

### Q6. What does the closing line "Its silence silences" mean, and why is there "nothing to say at all"?
**Answer:** Of this circumstance — death lasting as long as childhood life — there is nothing to say. Language fails before total loss. "Its silence" is death's muteness; it "silences" the poet, stopping speech, explanation and consolation. After witty recall and wry laughter, only quiet remains. The repetition of silence shows acceptance: not forgetting, but recognising some grief has no words. The poem ends where it began — stillness — but now empty, not sweet.

### Q7. Why does the poet lovingly call it "a sweet face, my mother's, that was before I was born"?
**Answer:** Sweetness is affection, not just beauty. "Before I was born" stresses distance — he never knew that twelve-year-old, yet loves her through the photo. It also marks irony: the mother he knew as adult once needed holding, was herself held by cousins. The line bridges generations, making the later death sharper: the sweet child became the laughing mother became the dead absence. Love persists across times the poet never shared.

### Q8. How do dress, laughter and silence trace changing attitudes to the same photograph?
**Answer:** First, dress is fact — how Betty, Dolly and mother were dressed for beach, smiled at. Later, dress becomes joke — mother teases past fashion, laughing with laboured ease. Finally, photo becomes unspeakable — after death, looking invites only silence. Same cardboard moves from record to play to relic. It shows how objects linked to loved lives change value with time, much like memory itself: joy thins into wryness, wryness into quiet.

## 3. We're Not Afraid to Die... if We Can All Be Together — Gordon Cook / Alan East — 8 Questions

### Q1. Why is the story titled from a six-year-old's words rather than the storm itself?
**Answer:** Jonathan, six, asks "Daddy, are we going to die?" then adds "But we aren't afraid of dying if we can all be together — you and Mummy, Sue and I." The captain, speechless, leaves determined to fight the sea with everything he had. The line shifts focus from adventure to values: courage is not fearlessness but togetherness. It explains survival — family unity, not just seamanship. The seven-year-old Sue's quiet endurance and Mary's steering echo it. The title honours innocent wisdom that revives adults at their lowest on January 5.

### Q2. How does the January 2 wave episode show both horror and the captain's presence of mind?
**Answer:** At dawn waves were gigantic; with small storm jib at eight knots he slowed, dropped jib, lashed mooring rope loop across stern, double-lashed everything, drilled life-raft, fixed lifelines, wore oilskins and jackets. At 6 p.m. ominous silence, wind dropped, sky dark, roar rose, a cloud towered aft — not cloud but vertical wave twice others with breaking crest. Explosion, green-white torrent, head smashed into wheel, thrown overboard, sinking peaceful acceptance, popping up, Wavewalker near capsizing masts horizontal, second wave upright, lifeline jerked, grabbed rails, crashed into boom like rag doll, ribs cracked, blood-broken teeth, yet found wheel and lined stern. Terror met by training.

### Q3. Why does survival depend more on preparation and teamwork than luck?
**Answer:** The 37-year-old businessman and Mary spent 16 years honing skills, fitted 23-metre 30-ton Wavewalker for months and tested in roughest weather. They hired Larry Vigil and Herb Seigler for the southern Indian Ocean. After hit, Mary screams decks smashed full water, Larry and Herb pump amid broken timbers, starboard bulged, clothes-crockery-charts-toys sloshing, captain stretches canvas with hammer-screws, fixes hatch covers, copes with blocked hand pumps, shorted electric, lost spares overboard plus forestay, jib, dinghies, anchor, finds third electric under chartroom floor, pumps in rotation, radios Mayday with no reply. Luck helps, but drills, spares knowledge and shared labour keep them afloat 15 hours to January 3.

### Q4. How does Suzanne's hidden injury reveal courage beyond her seven years?
**Answer:** After flooding she points only to a big bump; later head swells alarmingly with two black eyes and deep cut on arm needing later six minor operations for recurring clot. Asked why silent, she says "I didn't want to worry you when you were trying to save us all." She also makes a thank-you card. While adults pump and navigate, she endures pain selflessly, protecting parents' focus. Her maturity contrasts adult fear expressed through work, proving optimism and consideration sustain morale as much as pumps sustain hull.

### Q5. How do the January 3-4 decisions show leadership under "direst stress"?
**Answer:** By January 3 morning water is controlled enough for two-hour rest, but inspection shows leak below waterline, main ribs smashed to keel, starboard hull held only by cupboard partitions — never reaching Australia. Checking charts, captain picks Ile Amsterdam, French base few hundred km east, pinpricks in vast ocean. After 36 hours continuous pumping to January 4, with main mast unusable, he hoists storm jib, heads east, Mary finds corned beef and crackers — first meal in two days. He converts despair into plan, rations rest, chooses realistic refuge over impossible goal, keeping cheerful-optimistic crew aligned.

### Q6. Why is the January 5 sea-anchor night the emotional turning point?
**Answer:** Respite ends — 4 p.m. black clouds, wind 40 knots by dawn, desperate again. Jonathan's question and answer harden resolve. To shield weakened starboard, captain heaves-to with port hull to waves using improvised sea anchor of nylon rope and two 22-litre paraffin barrels. That evening he and Mary sit holding hands as water pours through broken planks, feeling end near. Yet he has decided to fight fully. Despair is faced together, not alone. Next morning wind eases, sextant work resumes, leading to landfall — proving resolve precedes rescue.

### Q7. How does the navigation to Ile Amsterdam combine skill and hope against odds?
**Answer:** On January 6 he calculates with sextant, courses, drift, current, knowing main compass lost and spare uncorrected for magnetic variation, searching 150,000 sq km for 65-km-wide island. At 2 p.m. he asks Larry to steer 185 degrees, promising land by 5 p.m. with conviction he does not feel, dozes heavy-hearted, wakes 6 p.m. sure missed. Then Jonathan seeks hug as best daddy-captain, Sue says matter-of-factly island ahead as big as battleship. Stark volcanic rock with little vegetation looks most beautiful. Skill keeps course; children's faith restores will; hope makes calculation persist without certainty.

### Q8. What do Larry, Herb and Mary contribute that the captain alone could not?
**Answer:** Larry and Herb pump like madmen, stay cheerful and optimistic under direst stress, later steer crucial hours. Mary screams warning, steers through critical phase, finds food, holds hands at end. Without rotation, canvas work and steering fail. On land his thoughts go first to them, then to Sue hiding injury and Jon unafraid. The story argues survival is collective: technical leadership needs willing hands, calm children and steady partner — togetherness literalised in title.

## 4. Discovering Tut: The Saga Continues — A. R. Williams — 8 Questions

### Q1. Why has Tut's body faced repeated scrutiny from 1922 to 1968 to 2005?
**Answer:** Tut, last heir of a powerful eighteenth-dynasty family ruling centuries, died as teenager 3,300 years ago, laden with gold, then forgotten. Since 1922 murder is extreme speculation. Two questions linger: how did he die and how old was he? His funeral was death rattle of a dynasty, yet particulars unclear. Hence Carter's 1922 clearing, 1968 anatomy X-ray finding breast-bone and front ribs missing under chest resin, and 5 January 2005 CT scan in Valley of Kings for forensic reconstruction. Each generation revisits because premature royal death plus missing bones keeps mystery open.

### Q2. Was Howard Carter's destructive investigation justified?
**Answer:** Carter found richest royal collection ever — hastily ransacked yet complete — gold meant to guarantee resurrection plus everyday board games, bronze razor, linen, food-wine for afterlife, three nested coffins, shroud with willow-olive, wild celery, lotus, cornflowers pointing to March-April burial. But ritual resins hardened, cementing Tut to solid gold coffin; 149°F sunshine failed, so consolidated material had to be chiselled from limbs-trunk with scientific detachment. Men removed head, severed nearly every major joint, reassembled on sand in padded box. Hawass calls condition very bad. Defence: thieves would have circumvented guards and ripped mummy for gold. Justified as rescue, regretted as damage — purpose saved treasures but harmed body archaeology now seeks to protect.

### Q3. How did the 2005 CT scan improve on the 1968 X-ray?
**Answer:** X-ray gave one startling fact — missing breast-bone and ribs — but flat image. CT uses hundreds of cross-section X-rays like slices of bread into 3-D virtual body. On 5 January night, Tut left tomb first time in 80 years, carried like pallbearers up ramp-stairs through sand by hydraulic lift to trailer donated by National Geographic and Siemens. After sand clogged cooler fan — million-dollar scanner quit, guard joked curse — substitute fans finished 1,700 images, head in 0.62 mm slices with eerie detail, clear neck vertebrae, hand, rib cage, skull. Data enabled accurate forensic reconstruction of boyish pharaoh, moving beyond treasure to life-death truth under Egyptian Mummy Project counting 600 mummies.

### Q4. Why did Ray Johnson call Akhenaten "wacky", and how did Tut reverse him?
**Answer:** Amenhotep III ruled almost four decades at golden height. Son Amenhotep IV promoted Aten sun disk, renamed Akhenaten servant of Aten, moved capital Thebes to Akhetaten-Amarna, attacked major god Amun smashing images and closing temples. Johnson, Chicago Luxor director, called it horrific as centuries-old family ended and king went wacky. After Akhenaten died and mysterious Smenkhkare briefly vanished, very young Tutankhaten took throne, renamed Tutankhamun living image of Amun, restoring old ways, reigning about nine years before unexpected death. Name change from Aten to Amun signals religious restoration.

### Q5. Explain "the death rattle of a dynasty" — why was Tut's funeral a big event even by royal standards?
**Answer:** Tut was last of his family's line that ruled Egypt and empire centuries. His demise in late teens ended continuity; no heir followed. Even with lavish gold and ritual, funeral marked coughing end of power, not just person. Particulars of passing remain unclear, deepening gloom. The phrase compares dynasty to dying breath — rattling sound before silence. Hence modern speculation, curse whispers among tourists 26 feet underground gazing at murals and gilded face, and wind-dust casket-grey CT night feeling like death itself.

### Q6. Why were gilded treasures buried beside everyday objects?
**Answer:** Royals were fabulously wealthy and hoped to take riches to great beyond; gold's eternal brilliance was meant to guarantee resurrection. So stunning artefacts sat beside things Tut would want in afterlife — games, razor, undergarments, food-wine cases. Three coffins, collars, necklaces, bracelets, rings, amulets, apron, sandals, finger-toe sheaths, inner coffin and mask all pure gold show status and belief: death is journey needing provision. Carter's months recording them prove 1922 focus was treasure; later science asks life details instead.

### Q7. What does the Orion ending suggest when science meets myth?
**Answer:** After scan Tut rested where priests laid him within three hours; images bloomed — grey head spun, vertebrae clear as anatomy class; Hawass smiled "didn't sleep... now will sleep." Outside wind stopped, winter air cold still like death in valley of departed, and above entrance stood Orion, Egyptians' soul of Osiris god of afterlife watching boy king. The image grants peace: technology probes, but myth watches. It respects dead while seeking truth, closing with cosmic guardianship rather than cold data.

### Q8. Explain "King Tut is one of the first mummies to be scanned — in death, as in life, moving regally ahead."
**Answer:** In life Tut led as pharaoh; in death he again leads — among first of nearly 600 inventoried since late 2003 to be CT-scanned with portable machine. "Regally ahead of countrymen" means rank persists in research order. It honours youth: teenager who ruled briefly still pioneers. It also marks shift — archaeology now focuses less on treasure, more on life-mystery using medical tools, and Tut fronts that march.

## 5. The Laburnum Top — Ted Hughes — 8 Questions

### Q1. How does the poem move from silence to energy and back to silence?
**Answer:** The laburnum top is silent, quite still in afternoon yellow September sunlight, few leaves yellowing, all seeds fallen — approaching barrenness. Till goldfinch comes with twitching chirrup, suddenness startlement at branch end. Then machine starts — chitterings, tremor wings, trillings, whole tree trembles and thrills. After feeding, with eerie delicate whistle-chirrup whisperings she launches towards infinite, and laburnum subsides to empty. Beginning and ending both still; bird's visit is brief vibrant interruption. Circle shows life visits, not stays.

### Q2. Why is the bird's movement compared to a lizard, and on what basis?
**Answer:** "Sleek as a lizard, and alert, and abrupt, she enters the thickness." Basis is manner — smooth, quick, watchful, sudden — not looks. Lizard slips silently; goldfinch, small yellow-winged singer, darts into foliage hiding nest. Comparison captures wild alertness and grace under stillness, making suddenness believable. It also hints survival: both stay sleek and cautious to protect young.

### Q3. Why is the tree called the "engine of her family" that she "stokes"?
**Answer:** Tree-with-nest is energy source: her arrival starts it like machine — chitterings, wing tremor, trillings. She stokes it full by feeding chicks, fuelling noise-life, then flirts out showing barred face identity mask. Engine image turns nature maternal-industrial: mother powers family, tree thrums. Trembles and thrills blend fear and joy. Without her, engine stops; with her, silent wood becomes living system. It celebrates motherhood as vital force.

### Q4. What does "her barred face identity mask" mean?
**Answer:** Bars are stripe marks on goldfinch face. Called mask because partly hides, yet here reveals — identity mask shows who she is as she peeks from branch end. Paradox: markings that could conceal declare species and self. After thickness, she displays face to confirm presence before leaving. It suggests identity survives concealment; mother shows herself briefly between duties.

### Q5. Why does she launch "towards the infinite" and why does the tree "subside to empty"?
**Answer:** Infinite is open sky, limitless beyond branch — freedom after duty. With delicate eerie whisperings she leaves. Laburnum subsides — sinks — to empty, drained of sound-life. Empty is not just quiet but voided. Contrast proves interdependence: tree needs bird for meaning; bird needs tree for nest but can leave. Departure restores original stillness, now felt as loss.

### Q6. Why does yellow dominate, and what does it signify?
**Answer:** Afternoon yellow sunlight, yellowing leaves, yellow flowers of laburnum — short tree with hanging branches, poisonous seeds — and yellow feathers of goldfinch. Yellow links sun, season September, tree and bird in one glow. It signals autumn ripeness and fading — leaves yellowing, seeds fallen — yet warmth of life. Monochrome makes sudden movement stand out, and unifies nature-mother-child in single colour of energy and decay.

### Q7. How do sound words prove tree and bird depend on each other?
**Answer:** Alone tree is silent, still. With bird: twitching chirrup, chitterings, tremor wings, trillings, trembles-thrills, whistle-chirrup whisperings. Sounds start only when she enters thickness, stop when she leaves. Tree provides shelter; bird brings voice. Machine metaphor needs both frame and fuel. Their duet shows ecology: silence needs life to mean peace, life needs stillness to be heard.

### Q8. Why "eerie delicate whistle-chirrup whisperings" for farewell?
**Answer:** Eerie is strange, delicate is soft — parting is gentle yet haunting. Whisperings after loud trillings lower volume, signalling end. It mirrors arrival suddenness with departure softness. The phrase makes leaving sacred, not abrupt: mother slips away, infinite awaits, tree holds breath. Delicacy honours care; eeriness hints death-like emptiness to follow.

## 6. The Voice of the Rain — Walt Whitman — 8 Questions

### Q1. Why does the rain call itself the "Poem of Earth"?
**Answer:** Asked "And who art thou?" the soft-falling shower, strange to tell, answers in translation: "I am the Poem of Earth." Like poem, rain is earth's expression — rhythmic, beautiful, meaningful, returning to origin. It rises eternally impalpable as vapour from land and bottomless sea to heaven, vaguely form'd, changed yet same, descends to lave droughts, atomies, dust-layers, waking seeds latent unborn, forever day-night giving back life, making pure and beautifying. Both poem and rain beautify source selflessly. Title equates nature's cycle with art's purpose.

### Q2. How does the rain's description match the scientific water cycle?
**Answer:** Eternal rise impalpable from land-sea equals evaporation; upward to heaven vaguely form'd equals condensation into clouds; altogether changed yet same equals form shift water-vapour-cloud-rain; descend to lave droughts equals precipitation washing dust; seeds latent unborn revived equals growth; give back life to origin equals return to sea-earth. Whitman poetically states evaporation-cloud-rain-life without terms, proving poetry and science describe same truth — cycle purifies and sustains.

### Q3. Why are droughts, atomies and latent seeds mentioned together?
**Answer:** Droughts are large dryness, atomies tiny particles, dust-layers surface, seeds hidden unborn life — all need rain. Listing vast to minute shows universality: without rain all remain seeds only, dormant. Lave means wash-bathe. Rain does not merely wet; it activates potential. Together they prove rain as life-giver, not just weather, making pure and beautifying globe.

### Q4. What does "forever, by day and night, I give back life to my own origin" teach about selflessness?
**Answer:** Origin is land-sea that sent vapour up. Rain returns to same source, not elsewhere, giving life back, purifying it. Day-night-forever stresses ceaseless, unpaid service. Unlike human giving expecting return, rain gives to parent that gave it. It models eternal reciprocity: receive, transform, return beautified. Song parallel extends it — art too returns with love whether heeded or not.

### Q5. Why are the last two lines put in brackets?
**Answer:** "(For song, issuing from its birth-place, after fulfilment, wandering Reck'd or unreck'd, duly with love returns.)" Brackets mark aside — general truth beyond rain dialogue. Song born in poet's heart, fulfilled in world, wandering heeded or ignored, duly returns with love to origin, just as rain returns to earth. Aside universalises: rain : earth :: song : soul. Brackets signal poet's commentary, not rain's voice, linking nature to art.

### Q6. Who are the two voices, and what does "strange to tell" mean?
**Answer:** Poet's voice asks, rain's voice answers. "Strange to tell" means surprisingly, unbelievably — shower cannot speak, yet here translated reply comes. Phrase admits fantasy while inviting belief: personification lets natureexplain itself. It frames poem as imagined dialogue, making science intimate.

### Q7. How do opposites structure the poem's meaning?
**Answer:** Rise-descend, land-sea-bottomless-heaven, vaguely form'd-changed yet same, day-night, reck'd-unreck'd, form'd-formless, birth-return. Opposites show cycle holds contraries: departure needs return, change keeps identity, neglect does not stop love. They balance movement — up-down, give-take — proving eternity lives in alternation, not stasis.

### Q8. How is song like rain in origin, wandering and return?
**Answer:** Song issues from birth-place heart, after fulfilment wanders world reck'd heeded or unreck'd ignored, duly with love returns to maker. Rain rises from land-sea, fulfils as shower, wanders as clouds, returns to origin purifying. Both complete circle, enrich source, persist regardless of notice. Comparison dignifies poet: like rain, poem gives life back, beautifies birth-place selflessly.

## 7. The Summer of the Beautiful White Horse — William Saroyan — 8 Questions

### Q1. How does the Garoghlanian fame for honesty create the story's central conflict?
**Answer:** The tribe lived in most amazing comical poverty yet famous for honesty eleven centuries, even when wealthiest — proud first, honest next, believing right-wrong, none taking advantage let alone stealing. Nine-year-old Aram, woken 4 a.m. by cousin Mourad deemed crazy by all except him, sees beautiful white horse he longed to ride since earliest memories. He sees, smells, hears breathing yet refuses to believe stolen — no Garoghlanian could be thief. Longing versus honour drives plot: keep horse means betraying centuries; return means losing dream. Poverty sharpens temptation.

### Q2. How does Aram justify that riding is not stealing, and what does it reveal?
**Answer:** Mourad avoids straight question, but truth is stolen. Aram consoles: stealing horse for ride is not like stealing money — it becomes stealing only when offered to sell, which they never would. Innocent boy logic separates use from ownership, adventure from crime. It reveals child morality — intent matters — and tribal pride bending truth to protect self-image. Yet unease persists, preparing return when Byro's trust shames them. Justification is evasion, but also charm of good old days nostalgia.

### Q3. Why is Mourad called crazy yet shown to have a "way with" living things?
**Answer:** Natural descendant of crazy streak before belonging to uncle Khosrove — enormous black hair, largest moustache in San Joaquin Valley, furious irritable impatient roaring "It is no harm; pay no attention" even when son Arak ran eight blocks shouting house on fire — while father Zorab practical flesh not spirit. Mourad enjoys being alive most, roars singing, shouts "Vazire run!" making horse rear-snort-burst loveliest crossing ditch returning dripping, claiming simple honest understanding Aram will know at thirteen. He repairs hurt wing of young robin, dogs follow silently. Craziness is vitality, empathy with animals, daring honour — lying lightly "Of course not" about start yet insisting horse go back to true owner in six months.

### Q4. What does uncle Khosrove's repeated roar reveal about loss and temperament?
**Answer:** Enormous, furious, impatient, he stops talk with "It is no harm; pay no attention to it." When John Byro, Assyrian farmer learning Armenian from loneliness, reports white horse stolen last month, walking ten miles paining leg, surrey useless, costing sixty dollars, Khosrove roars loss of horse nothing against lost homeland, spits on money, stalks slamming screen. Mother explains gentle heart, simply homesick, such large man. Roar is coping — exile minimises lesser losses, irritability masks grief. Humour plus pathos: comic cure-all hides tender displacement.

### Q5. Why do Byro's words "a suspicious man would believe his eyes instead of his heart" hurt more than accusation?
**Answer:** Meeting boys, Mourad says "Let me do talking — way with farmers," naming horse "My Heart." Byro studies eagerly, looks mouth, says tooth for tooth swear my horse if didn't know parents, fame of honesty well known, yet twin — suspicious man trusts eyes over heart. He chooses heart, greeting good day. Generous faith wounds conscience: exploiting trust would make them true thieves, betraying tribe and kind farmer. Accusation invites denial; trust invites shame. Hence next morning quiet return proves conscience, strengthened by ancestral honour, beats temptation.

### Q6. Why is the quiet return, with dogs silent, proof of conscience over fear?
**Answer:** For two weeks they rode mornings, Aram always thrown over vines-trees, hiding in Fetvajian deserted vineyard with oats-alfalfa. Fear existed, but hiding was safe, no panic. After Byro's faith, early next morning they put horse in Byro's barn — dogs followed silently, "way with dogs." Afternoon Byro drives surrey amazed stronger better-tempered thank God, Khosrove shouts "Quiet man quiet returned Pay no attention." Silence — no bark, no boast — shows repentance, not escape. They restore better horse, accepting guilt privately, honouring trust.

### Q7. How do hiding details show temptation struggling with honour?
**Answer:** Mourad enjoyed rides some time before fetching Aram who longed to ride; asked since when, lies riding started morning — "if found out that's what say don't want both liars." Barn choice, oats care, Aram begging keep till learned, Mourad snapping "inviting member to steal? must go back true owner" in six months, daily dawn rides while school sleeps — all show prolonged savouring, not momentary lapse. Yet limit set, return kept. Struggle makes honesty active choice, not innocence — more meaningful.

### Q8. What is the significance of naming the horse "My Heart" and "tooth for tooth" likeness?
**Answer:** "My Heart" claims love, not ownership — Mourad loves horse as self. "Tooth for tooth" means exactly alike, twin. Byro sees exact match yet trusts parents' fame over eyes. Naming exposes affection; likeness exposes fact. Together they stage theme: heart versus eyes, love versus law. Boys love horse truly, but love cannot justify taking; returning honours both hearts — Byro's and tribe's.

## 8. The Address — Marga Minco — 8 Questions

### Q1. What does Mrs Dorling's line "Have you come back? I thought that no one had come back" reveal about the hidden history?
**Answer:** After War narrator rings Marconi Street, woman opens chink, denies knowing, hears "I'm Mrs S's daughter," holds door, murmurs line. It reveals persecution-deportation of Jews — Holocaust Holland: Dorling expected whole family dead, none returning. Mother and household gone, only daughter survives. Clue explains why belongings were "saved," why narrator first afraid, why address given urgently in first half War. Single sentence carries genocide background without naming it, making personal coldness historical guilt.

### Q2. Was Mrs Dorling saving belongings or quietly plundering them?
**Answer:** Mother in first half War noticed things missing, explained old acquaintance unseen years renewed contact, visiting regularly, each time taking something home — table-silver one go, antique plates wall, large vases "crick in back from crockery." Dorling herself suggested-insisted to save if family must leave, risking full suitcase each trip; mother looked reprovingly when doubted. After Liberation Dorling wears mother's green knitted cardigan with pale washed wooden buttons, denies recognition, pleads inconvenient, hides behind bay-window, daughter misuses antiques. Saving mask covers greed — exploiting danger to appropriate, then possessively keeping, neither understanding nor valuing.

### Q3. Why is the narrator first afraid of the belongings, then curious to see-touch-remember?
**Answer:** After Liberation she was not interested, even afraid confronting connection no longer existing, hidden in cupboards-boxes waiting in vain — trauma, fear streets memory. As life normalises — bread lighter, sleep unthreatened, familiar view — curiosity wakes. Healing allows approach: normalcy restores capacity to remember without collapse. Shift shows grief stages — avoidance then seeking — and sets second visit. Curiosity is recovery, not greed; she wants touch, not possession.

### Q4. How does the second visit prove memories live in hearts, not objects?
**Answer:** Girl fifteen lets in, mother errand. Passage iron Hanukkah candle-holder cumbersome unused. Living-room stops horrified — "room I knew and did not know" — familiar things tasteless amid ugly furniture muggy smell. She rubs woollen table-cloth finding unrepaired burn edge, tea from white pot gold border lid, spoons antique box lots more, still-life apple pewter plate loved as child, girl says wall plates once eaten off wasn't special. Same objects chill in strange surroundings. Realisation: linked objects instantly lose value severed from familiar life. Mother lived in use-love, not cutlery-vases. Without rooms-love, dead weight.

### Q5. Why does the narrator suddenly flee at the jingling of spoons and forks?
**Answer:** Recalling girlhood ignorance — never knew everyday cutlery silver till mother asked polishing help — she watches girl pull drawer to show if theirs silver too. Jingling triggers disgust: heirlooms reduced to kitchen clatter, sacred handled casually. Flight "must catch train" is moral-physical recoil, not schedule. Sound embodies severance — past tinkling in alien hands unbearable. Leaving mid-visit proves seeing is enough; taking would prolong pain.

### Q6. Justify the title and the resolve to forget the address as healing, not betrayal.
**Answer:** Address — Marconi Street Number 46, remembered from mother lifting heavy suitcase with broad back brown coat shapeless hat — is thread of hope to lost mother-past. At corner she confirms correct yet no longer wishes remembering: objects lose value strange surroundings; small rented room with black-out paper shreds and narrow drawer cannot hold past. "Resolved to forget... easiest of all things to forget" chooses inner memory over retrieval. Forgetting is survival — letting go hoarding for healing, carrying mother in heart not vases. Title is both place and decision.

### Q7. How does Mrs Dorling's character contrast with the narrator's mother?
**Answer:** Dorling: broad back, brown coat, shapeless hat, lugging suitcase, opportunistic greedy, impolite inhospitable — opens chink, denies, shuts door — possessive of things misused by daughter as everyday junk. Mother: trusting, vulnerable, marvels at risk, reprovingly defends friend, gives address as safeguard, polishes silver with daughter lovingly. Trust versus exploitation; wartime fear reveals character — one saves love, other saves goods. Narrator inherits mother's loss, not Dorling's gain.

### Q8. Why can the small rented room never hold the past?
**Answer:** Room has shreds black-out paper still along windows, barely handful cutlery fits narrow drawer. Past needs large house, wall plates, vases, table-cloth, family rituals. Physical impossibility mirrors emotional truth — past severed cannot be rehoused. Keeping would cram and distort. Forgetting address accepts present limits, honouring former life by not shrinking it to drawer. Space becomes metaphor for irretrievability.

## 9. Mother's Day — J.B. Priestley — 8 Questions

### Q1. How does the play expose the status of the mother as unpaid servant?
**Answer:** Living-room small semi-detached villa London suburb afternoon early autumn. Mrs Annie Pearson pleasant worried forties light flurried suburban tone is ordered — Doris demands yellow silk ironed for Charlie Spence evening, Cyril briskly wants tea and things laid out, George wants tea ready yet announces special snooker club needing none. No wages thanks, she stays home nightly while they enjoy. Fitzgerald cards read could be good-bad depends — "Put foot down be mistress own house boss own family," spoiling ruin them as well you. Issue universal: homemaker taken natural, denied leisure-respect. Humour carries satire.

### Q2. Why is the body-swap dramatically justified instead of simple advice?
**Answer:** Pearson too fond, hating unpleasantness, only dropping hints, admits cannot confront however tried. Stronger neighbour, real fortune-teller learning cards-tricks twelve years out East where husband Lieutenant Quartermaster, proposes impossible change places via occult chant "Arshtatta dum-lam-lamdumbona." Pearson soul alarmed next door, Fitzgerald bold dominates in Pearson body — "They'll have me to deal with only won't know." Only shock wakes deaf family; hints failed. Fantasy is social surgery — borrowing courage lets timid see bold effect, audience laugh then recognise. Later guided restoration keeps affection.

### Q3. How is Doris humbled, and what vanity is targeted?
**Answer:** Bursting for yellow silk and Charlie Spence outing, she finds Mum smoking playing patience, no tea made "Yours isn't," Mum may eat Clarendon square meal. Told she works hard, gets back twice hours no wages thanks. Charlie mocked buck teeth half-witted — at her age better found. Runs nearly tears. Vanity is youthful choice — pretty early twenties prizing outing over mother, expecting ironing as right. Bluntness forces seeing self through adult eyes, first crack in spoiling.

### Q4. How does Cyril's "forty-hour week" lesson turn his own logic against him?
**Answer:** Asking tea, told couldn't bother, things not put decided don't like mending. He cites work Union barring dislikes — she replies joined movement Changes. Family claims eight-hour day, so she now forty-hour week — watch weekend two days off — though favour may make bed-cook if asked nicely thanked; may go weekend herself. Whispering giggling pair silenced. Satire mirrors labour rights at home: if son can refuse at factory, mother can refuse at home. Demand for thanks reframes service as work, not duty.

### Q5. How is George's pomposity punctured by "Pompy-ompy Pearson"?
**Answer:** Fifty, decent solemn self-important, scandalised by stout hour, annoyed about tea he doesn't want — try telling club bar. Told club members laugh behind back calling Pompy-ompy Pearson because slow pompous, leaving wife home nightly. Asked directly, Cyril admits "yes Dad afraid." Staggers as if hit over head. Later lectured manners, warned if shouts again slap big fat silly face, rude son called silly spoilt young piecan. Nickname proves public mockery private dignity lacks; confirmation by son prevents denial. Vanity collapses, preparing humble "Suits me."

### Q6. How do Mrs Pearson and Mrs Fitzgerald contrast, and why must they swap?
**Answer:** Pearson pleasant worried fortyish flurried, fond, hates unpleasantness, drops hints, can't say no. Fitzgerald older heavier strong sinister deep voice smokes, experienced bold decisive, supplies courage lacking. Swap lets timid borrow toughness, tough learn limits — Fitzgerald soon cannot bear family's misery begs swap-back. Contrast shows assertiveness can be learned, not innate; both need balance — firmness with affection, not tyranny.

### Q7. Why is the advice "don't explain or apologise" crucial to lasting change?
**Answer:** Restored, Fitzgerald coaches sternly: don't go soft, don't explain-apologise or done for; just give look-tone suggesting tough. Test comes — apprehensive family told tonight nice family game rummy, children supper ready while Mother talks Father. Humbled George "Suits me," Doris lovely, curtain clusters round Mother. Explanation would invite debate, apology would restore doormat. Firm mystery sustains respect; affectionate tone prevents fear alone ruling. Cure is assertion, not aggression — dignity demanded, love kept.

### Q8. Is the rummy ending an acceptable resolution to a genuine issue?
**Answer:** Yes, as comic lesson. Issues genuine — unpaid round-clock labour, absence of thanks-company. Resolution through shock plus coaching — refusing tea-stout, exposing Charlie, teaching week, puncturing pomposity — replaces orders-absence with attention-thanks-company. Family stays home for rummy, cooks supper, talks. Acceptable because insists not revenge but dignity: mother demands, not abandons, love. Caveat — lasting needs willing heart change, not fear — yet as drama demanding mothers put foot down, lands perfectly, modelling balance restored with love.
