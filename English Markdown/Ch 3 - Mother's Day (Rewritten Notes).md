# Ch 3 — Mother's Day (Play)
**Author:** J.B. Priestley · **Book:** Snapshots (Class 11 Supplementary Reader) · **Type:** One-act comedy/satire on the status of the mother in the family

> One line to remember: *Doormat Mrs Pearson is ordered about by her spoilt husband and children until fortune-teller neighbour Mrs Fitzgerald magically swaps bodies with her; as "Mrs Pearson" she shocks each family member with cold truths — no tea, stout, "Pompy-ompy Pearson" — and wins respect: a firm hand turns servants' treatment into a family game of rummy.*

## 1. Characters & Setting

Living-room of the Pearsons' small semi-detached villa in a **London suburb**, an afternoon in early autumn, present day. **Mrs Annie Pearson** — pleasant but worried, in her forties, speaks in a light, flurried suburban tone. **Mrs Fitzgerald** — older, heavier, strong and sinister personality, deep voice, smokes; a **real fortune-teller** who learnt cards (and other tricks) during **twelve years "out East"** where her husband rose to be Lieutenant Quartermaster. The spoilt family: **Doris** — pretty daughter in her early twenties; **Cyril** — her masculine counterpart; **George Pearson** — about fifty, fundamentally decent but solemn, self-important and pompous.

---

## 2. The Fortune & the Body Swap

Mrs Fitzgerald lays down the cards: the fortune "could be good or bad" — all depends on Mrs Pearson now: **"Put your foot down… an' be the mistress of your own house an' the boss of your own family."** Her husband and children give her orders as if she were the servant, go out enjoying themselves nightly while she stays home; being spoilt is *"the ruin of them as well as you."* But Mrs Pearson, too fond of them and hating unpleasantness, only "keeps dropping a hint." Since she cannot speak up herself, Mrs Fitzgerald proposes the impossible: **they change places — or really bodies** — using an occult chant learnt out East (*"Arshtatta dum—arshtatta lam—arshtatta lamdumbona"*). After the swap, Mrs Fitzgerald's body carries Mrs Pearson's timid soul (alarmed, sent next door), while Mrs Pearson's body houses the bold, dominating visitor: *"They'll have me to deal with — only they won't know it."*

---

## 3. Doris Gets a Shock

Doris bursts in demanding her **yellow silk** be ironed for her evening out with **Charlie Spence** — and finds Mum smoking and playing patience. No tea has been made ("Yours isn't"), and Mrs Pearson may go out for a square meal at the Clarendon. When Doris protests she has worked hard all day, she gets it back twice over: *"I put in twice the hours you do, and get no wages nor thanks for it."* She mocks Charlie Spence — **buck teeth and half-witted**: "When I was your age I'd have found somebody better." Doris runs out nearly in tears.

---

## 4. Cyril & the Forty-Hour Week

Cyril, briskly asking for tea, is told she **couldn't bother**; his things weren't put out because she's "decided I don't like mending." His logic — at work you get the Union to bar what you dislike — is turned on him: *"Now all that's happened is that I've joined the movement… Changes."* To the whispering, giggling pair she announces her new terms: they claim an **eight-hour day**, so she now works a **forty-hour week** — *"Just watch it at the weekend when I have my two days off"* — though as a favour she might make a bed or cook **if asked very nicely and thanked**; she might even go off for the week-end herself.

---

## 5. George — Pompy-Ompy Pearson

George enters, pompous, scandalised by the stout at that hour, and announces he needs no tea because of **special snooker night at the club**. She skewers him: annoyed about tea he doesn't even want — try telling the club bar that. Then the cruel truth: the club members laugh at him behind his back and call him **"Pompy-ompy Pearson"** because he is slow and pompous, while he leaves his wife home night after night. Cyril, asked directly, admits: *"Well — yes, Dad, I'm afraid they do."* George staggers out "as if somebody had hit him over the head." Later, when Cyril rudely calls Mrs Fitzgerald "that silly old bag," he is silenced as a **"silly, spoilt, young piecan"**; George is lectured on manners and warned: *"If you shout at me again like that, George Pearson, I'll slap your big, fat, silly face."*

---

## 6. Changing Back & the Firm Hand

The real Mrs Fitzgerald-in-Mrs-Pearson's-body cannot bear the family's misery and insists on swapping back. Once restored, she coaches her friend sternly: **don't go soft, don't explain or apologise — or you're done for**; just give them a look or a tone now and then suggesting you might be tough. The test comes at once. The apprehensive family is told: tonight there'll be **a nice family game of rummy**, then the children will get the supper ready **while Mother has a talk with Father**. Humbled, George answers, "Suits me"; Doris hastily agrees it would be lovely — and the curtain falls with the family clustered round Mother.

## 7. Exam Corner 📝✅

### Long Answer (Exam-Ready)

*"The play raises the issue of the status of the mother in the family. How does it resolve it? Do you agree with the resolution?"*

Written as humour with a satirical edge, the play shows Mrs Annie Pearson treated as an unpaid servant: Doris expects her silk ironed, Cyril his things laid out, George his tea kept ready — orders given, never thanks, while she stays home every night. The issues are genuine and universal even today: the homemaker taken for granted, denied leisure, wages or respect. The resolution arrives through Priestley's comic fantasy — the assertive Mrs Fitzgerald swaps bodies with her and, as Mrs Pearson, refuses tea, drinks stout, exposes Doris's choice of Charlie Spence, teaches Cyril the meaning of a forty-hour week, and punctures George's pomposity with the club's nickname for him, "Pompy-ompy Pearson." Back in her own body, coached never to explain or apologise, Mrs Pearson holds firm: rummy together, supper cooked by the children, a talk with her husband — and the family, relieved and respectful, clusters round her. The resolution is acceptable because the play insists not on revenge but on **assertion with affection**: the mother does not abandon love, she demands dignity. One may add a caveat — lasting change needs the family's willing change of heart, not fear alone; still, as a dramatic lesson that mothers must "put their foot down," it lands perfectly.

### Expected Questions & Pointers ✅

**Q1. Contrast Mrs Pearson and Mrs Fitzgerald.**
→ Mrs Pearson: pleasant, worried, fortyish, flurried voice, fond of family, hates unpleasantness, drops hints, can't say no. Mrs Fitzgerald: older, heavier, strong and sinister, deep Irish-ish voice, smokes, experienced fortune-teller (12 years out East), bold, decisive — supplies the courage Mrs Pearson lacks.

**Q2. What did Mrs Pearson say/do to each family member?**
→ **Doris:** no tea made, mocked yellow silk & Charlie Spence ("buck teeth and half-witted"), reduced her to tears. **Cyril:** "I couldn't bother" about tea and mending; joined "the movement"; forty-hour week, two days off. **George:** laughed away his tea grievance; revealed the club's "Pompy-ompy Pearson" mockery; threatened to slap his "big, fat, silly face" if shouted at; demanded manners towards guests.

**Q3. Why did Mrs Fitzgerald suggest changing places? Was it justified?**
→ Mrs Pearson admitted she couldn't confront her family however much she tried, so the stronger neighbour lent her own bold personality through the occult body-swap. Dramatically justified: only shock could wake a family deaf to hints; and she later guides the gentle restoration so affection survives.

**Q4. How was George's vanity punctured?**
→ Told bluntly that club-mates call him "Pompy-ompy Pearson," laughing behind his back while he left his wife home nightly; son Cyril confirmed it — leaving the self-important man dazed, "as if somebody had hit him over the head."

**Q5. What changes came over the family at the end?**
→ Apprehension replaced arrogance: George humbly says "whatever you say / Suits me"; Cyril and Doris agree hastily to cook supper; the family stays home for rummy with Mother — attention, thanks and company replace orders and absence.

**Q6. Is drama a good medium for a social message?**
→ Yes — live characters make abstract injustice visible; laughter lowers defences, so satire enters without sermonising; the audience both laughs at and recognises itself, which discussion and enactment (reading parts aloud) turn into reflection.

## 9. Theme & Message 💡

- **The unpaid servant at home** — a wife/mother working round the clock for "no wages nor thanks" is taken as natural; the play calls this ruin "of them as well as you."
- **Spoiling harms everyone** — children and husbands who order mothers about grow selfish and hollow; respect must be taught at home.
- **Assertiveness, not aggression** — the cure is putting your foot down firmly, without apology; meekness invites exploitation.
- **Fantasy as social surgery** — the body-swap device lets a timid woman borrow courage and lets the audience see both sides (Fitzgerald soon begs to stop — toughness also has limits).
- **Balance restored with love** — the goal is not a tyrant mother but a family that asks nicely, thanks warmly, and plays rummy together.
