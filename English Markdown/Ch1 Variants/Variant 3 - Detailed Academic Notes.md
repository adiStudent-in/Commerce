# CBSE Class 11 English Hornbill Chapter 1 – Complete Notes (Variant 3)
## PROSE: The Portrait of a Lady – Khushwant Singh | POEM: A Photograph – Shirley Toulson

### 1. Chapter Overview

Chapter 1 of NCERT Hornbill pairs two deeply moving texts on the same human experiences — **love, memory, ageing and loss**.

1. **The Portrait of a Lady** is an autobiographical portrait by Khushwant Singh of his grandmother — her daily life, values, quiet dignity, gradual withdrawal, and peaceful death.
2. **A Photograph** is an elegy by Shirley Toulson in which a daughter looks at an old photograph of her dead mother as a 12-year-old girl and reflects on how time erases laughter and life itself.

Together they teach how relationships change with time, how death creates silence, and how memory preserves what time destroys.

---

### 2. About the Authors

#### 2.1 Khushwant Singh (1915–2014)

- Eminent Indian writer, journalist, editor and historian, born in Hadali, Punjab (now in Pakistan).
- Educated at Government College Lahore, St. Stephen's College Delhi, and King's College London; practised law before turning to writing.
- Famous for *Train to Pakistan* and his columns *With Malice Towards One and All*.
- Known for lucid, honest prose, deep humanism and Sikh cultural roots.
- *The Portrait of a Lady* is autobiographical, written in affectionate remembrance of his grandmother who brought him up in his village childhood.

#### 2.2 Shirley Toulson (1924–2018)

- English poet, writer and journalist, born in Henley-on-Thames, England.
- Worked as reporter, magazine editor, and writer of local history and biographies.
- Her poetry is simple, lyrical, image-driven and contemplative.
- *A Photograph* is a personal elegy mourning her mother, exploring transience and loss through a single cardboard photograph.

### 3. PROSE – The Portrait of a Lady: Detailed Summary

Khushwant Singh divides his grandmother's life with him into three phases: **village childhood, city boyhood, and later separation and death.**

#### 3.1 Paragraph-wise Explanation

**Para 1-2: The Last Image – Wrinkled and Serene**
The author remembers his grandmother as an extremely old, short, fat, slightly bent woman. Her face was a criss-cross of wrinkles. He says she was beautiful like the winter landscape in the mountains – white hair, serene face. She hobbled about with one hand on her waist. He cannot imagine her ever being young, pretty, or playing games as a child. The portrait is of contentment, purity and peace, not physical beauty.

**Para 3-5: Early Village Life – The Constant Companion**
The author and grandmother were good friends in the village. His parents had gone to the city, leaving him with her. She woke him up, got his wooden slate ready, gave him breakfast of stale chapatti with butter and sugar, and walked him to the village school attached to the temple. She carried scriptures and sang morning prayers. On the way back they fed the village dogs with stale chapattis. This was the period of closest intimacy.

**Para 6-9: Turning Point – Coming to the City**
When they moved to the city, their friendship was strained. The author went to an English school in a motor bus. He learnt English words, science and music. Grandmother could not help him. She was distressed that there was no teaching of God and scriptures. She was particularly disturbed by music lessons, as for her music had lewd associations — meant for harlots and beggars, not respectable families. She said nothing; her silence was disapproval.

**Para 10-11: Withdrawal – Sparrows, Spinning and Prayer**
As the author grew up and went to university, he got his own room. The common link of friendship was snapped. Grandmother accepted seclusion with resignation. She spent most of her time at the spinning wheel, reciting prayers, and feeding sparrows in the afternoon — her happiest half-hour, with birds perched on her legs, shoulders and head.

**Para 12-14: Going Abroad and Return**
The author went abroad for higher studies for five years. Grandmother went to the railway station to see him off but did not show emotion — kept praying and kissed his forehead. After five years he returned. Grandmother came to receive him, clasped him in her arms, gathered neighbourhood women, and sang songs of the homecoming of warriors while beating an old dilapidated drum. For the first time she missed her prayers — she was overjoyed.

**Para 15-17: Peaceful Death**
Next morning she was taken ill with mild fever. The doctor said it would go. But she knew her end was near. She stopped talking to the family, lay in bed praying and telling her beads. Soon the rosary fell from her lifeless fingers and she died serenely.

**Para 18-19: The Sparrows' Mourning**
In the evening, thousands of sparrows gathered silently around her corpse. They did not chirrup. The author's mother offered breadcrumbs, but they ignored them. When the corpse was carried off, they flew away quietly — nature's mourning for a noble soul.

#### 3.2 Character Sketch of the Grandmother

1. **Old, serene and content:** Short, fat, wrinkled, white-haired, bent, always in white. Symbol of peace and purity.
2. **Deeply religious:** Morning prayers, temple, scriptures, rosary beads, spinning wheel and prayer. Faith was her strength.
3. **Loving and caring:** Woke grandson, fed him, took him to school, fed dogs and sparrows.
4. **Conservative but tolerant:** Disapproved of English school and music but never interfered or complained. Accepted change with silence.
5. **Selfless and dignified:** Accepted loneliness, bore separation calmly, faced death bravely.
6. **Close to nature:** Village dogs and city sparrows show compassion for all living beings.

She embodies traditional Indian womanhood – love, piety, sacrifice, dignity and acceptance.

#### 3.3 Character Sketch of the Narrator

1. **Observant and honest:** Notices wrinkles, stoop, sparrows, rosary — paints a truthful picture.
2. **Loving grandson:** Respects and admires grandmother, calls her beautiful.
3. **Modern educated youth:** Moves from village to English school to university to abroad — symbol of generational change.
4. **Sensitive:** Feels the pain of growing distance; moved by her death and the sparrows' silence.

#### 3.4 Themes

- **Love and companionship:** Grandmother-grandson bond across generations.
- **Change vs tradition:** Village values versus English modern education.
- **Ageing, loneliness and acceptance:** Dignity in resignation.
- **Spirituality and death:** Prayer as a way of life; death as peaceful culmination.
- **Human-animal harmony:** Compassion beyond human relations.

#### 3.5 Literary Devices and Style

- **Portraiture:** Whole story is a loving verbal portrait, physical description leading to inner qualities.
- **Imagery:** Criss-cross of wrinkles, winter landscape, silver hair, sparrows on shoulders.
- **Simile:** "Like the winter landscape in the mountains."
- **Symbolism:** Prayer beads = devotion; spinning wheel = detachment; sparrows = purity and companionship; dogs = village simplicity.
- **Irony:** Music, a noble art, seen as vulgar; education creates distance instead of closeness.
- **Tone:** Affectionate, nostalgic, respectful, elegiac. Simple, lucid language.

### 4. POEM – A Photograph: Line-by-Line Explanation

#### Stanza 1 – The Photograph of Childhood

> *The cardboard shows me how it was / When the two girl cousins went paddling, / Each one holding one of my mother's hands, / And she the big girl – some twelve years or so.*

An old cardboard-framed photograph shows her mother as a girl of about twelve, with two younger cousins – Betty and Dolly – paddling in the sea on a beach holiday. The mother, the eldest, holds one cousin in each hand. "Cardboard" stresses age and fragility.

> *All three stood still to smile through their hair / At the uncle with the camera. A sweet face, / My mother's, that was before I was born.*

They posed for the poet's uncle, smiling with wind-blown hair. The poet, not yet born then, calls her young mother's face sweet with tender affection.

#### Stanza 2 – The Mother Laughs at the Past

> *And the sea, which appears to have changed less, / Washed their terribly transient feet.*

The sea is vast and almost unchanged, while human feet — human life — is "terribly transient", short-lived. Alliteration emphasises inevitable change. The sea symbolises eternity and time.

> *Some twenty-thirty years later / She'd laugh at the snapshot. "See Betty / And Dolly," she'd say, "and look how they / Dressed us for the beach." The sea holiday / Was her past, mine is her laughter.*

Twenty to thirty years later the middle-aged mother laughs nostalgically at the old-fashioned beach clothes. For the mother, the sea holiday belonged to the past; for the daughter-poet, the memory is her mother's laughter itself.

#### Stanza 3 – Death and Silence

> *Both wry with the laboured ease of loss.*

Both the mother mourning her lost childhood and the daughter mourning her lost mother endure loss with "wry" (twisted, bitter-sweet) and "laboured ease" (oxymoron: effort to appear at ease while grieving).

> *Now she's been dead nearly as many years / As that girl lived. And of this circumstance / There is nothing to say at all. / Its silence silences.*

The mother has been dead for nearly twelve years — nearly as long as the girl in the photo had lived (twelve years). The poet is left speechless. The silence of death silences the living. The poem ends in stillness, echoing the still photograph.

#### 4.1 Themes of the Poem

- **Transience vs permanence:** Human life fleeting; sea endures; photograph freezes a moment but cannot stop death.
- **Loss and mourning:** Loss of childhood, loss of mother, loss of expression.
- **Memory:** Photograph preserves memory but deepens pain.
- **Time:** Twelve years of life, nearly twelve years of death.

#### 4.2 Tone and Mood

Nostalgic, tender, melancholic, reflective. Moves from sweet remembrance to warm laughter to deep silent grief. Quiet acceptance, no bitterness.

#### 4.3 Literary Devices

| Device | Example | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Alliteration | *terribly transient feet, silence silences, stood still to smile* | Emphasis and music |
| Oxymoron | *laboured ease* | Grief pretends to be easy but needs effort |
| Transferred epithet | *terribly transient feet, sweet face* | Transience belongs to life, not feet |
| Imagery | *paddling, smile through hair, sea washed feet* | Visual beach-holiday images |
| Symbolism | *cardboard, sea, silence* | Fragility, eternity, death |
| Contrast / Irony | *sea unchanged vs feet transient; girl lived 12 years, mother dead nearly 12 years* | Highlights mortality |
| Enjambment | Lines flowing without pause | Natural conversational flow |

### 5. Critical Appreciation Paragraph (For Exam)

Both *The Portrait of a Lady* and *A Photograph* are elegies of love that turn personal loss into universal truth. Khushwant Singh celebrates a grandmother whose beauty lies in serenity, service and faith, showing how modern education widens the generation gap yet cannot diminish spiritual dignity; the silent sparrows elevate her to sainthood. Similarly, Shirley Toulson uses a single faded photograph to compress three stages — girlish innocence, maternal laughter, daughterly grief — contrasting the changeless sea with terribly transient human feet. Both use simple language, vivid imagery and symbolism: change and death are inevitable, but love preserved in memory triumphs over loss, and silence becomes the most eloquent language of mourning.

### 7. Exam Q&A with Value Points

**Q1. How did the author describe his grandmother? Why did he call her beautiful?**
- Short, fat, slightly bent, silver white hair, wrinkled face, hand on waist.
- Face like winter landscape — white, serene, content.
- Beautiful by inner peace and spiritual calm, not physical youth.
- Full-marks line: wrinkles were marks of a lived, selfless, pious life.

**Q2. Trace the three phases of the relationship.**
- **Village:** Close friendship; morning routine, temple school, feeding dogs.
- **City:** Distance grows; English school, science, music disapproved; spinning wheel and prayer.
- **University/Abroad:** Link snapped; separate room, five years abroad; warm welcome, then death.

**Q3. Why was grandmother distressed by city school?**
- Taught English, science, no God or scriptures. She could not help; felt useless. Music shocked her — associated with harlots/beggars. Silent disapproval, never interfered.

**Q4. Describe the death and the sparrows. What does it signify?**
- Mild fever; knew end near; lay praying, telling beads till rosary fell; died peacefully. Sparrows gathered silently, ignored bread, flew away after body removed. Signifies bond with nature, purity of soul, universal mourning.

**Q5. Explain "The sea... / Washed their terribly transient feet."**
- Sea = eternity, unchanged. Feet = mortal humans washed away by time. Alliteration stresses change and death. Central theme: nature endures, human life fleeting.

**Q6. Meaning of "Its silence silences"? How do loss and silence connect both texts?**
- Mother dead nearly 12 years; poet has no words; grief beyond language. In prose too, death brings sparrows' silence. Both show: love → memory → loss → silence. Silence is deepest mourning.
