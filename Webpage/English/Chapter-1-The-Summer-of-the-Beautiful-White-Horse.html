Snapshots · Class XI · Chapter 1

Ch 1 — The Summer of the Beautiful White Horse

Author: William Saroyan · Book: Snapshots (Class 11) · Type: Short story about childhood, family honour and trust

1. Chapter Summary

The story is told by Aram, looking back at the summer when he was nine years old. He begins by remembering the "good old days" when "the world was full of every imaginable kind of magnificence, and life was still a delightful and mysterious dream." One morning at four o'clock, his cousin Mourad — a boy considered crazy by everybody who knew him except Aram — woke him by tapping on his window and said, in Armenian, that he was sitting on a horse: "It's a horse. You're not dreaming. Make it quick if you want to ride." Aram could not believe his eyes: his cousin was sitting on a beautiful white horse.

Two things made this impossible to believe. First, Aram's earliest memories had been memories of horses and his first longings had been longings to ride — so his dream stood right in front of him. Second, and more important, the boys were poor. Their whole tribe, the Garoghlanian family, was poverty-stricken; every branch of it lived in "the most amazing and comical poverty in the world", and nobody, not even the old men of the family, could understand where they got enough money to keep food in their bellies. Above all, the family was famous for its honesty — famous for something like eleven centuries, even when it had been the wealthiest family in what it liked to think was the world. The family's order of pride was clear: "We were proud first, honest next, and after that we believed in right and wrong." None of them would take advantage of anybody, let alone steal. So Aram could not believe the horse had anything to do with Mourad, because Mourad could not have bought it, and if he could not have bought it he must have stolen it — and no member of the Garoghlanian family could be a thief.

Aram asked straight out, "Mourad, where did you steal this horse?" Mourad's answer was only, "Leap out of the window if you want to ride." So it was true. Yet Aram reasoned that stealing a horse for a ride was not the same as stealing something else, such as money — if you were crazy about horses the way Mourad and he were, it wasn't stealing, and it would not become stealing until they offered to sell the horse, which they would never do. He dressed quickly, jumped into the yard, and leaped up behind Mourad.

That year the family lived at the edge of town, on Walnut Avenue, with the country behind the house — vineyards, orchards, irrigation ditches and country roads. In less than three minutes they were on Olive Avenue and the horse began to trot. The air was new and lovely, and the feel of the horse running was wonderful. Mourad — "the natural descendant of the crazy streak" in the tribe — began to sing, or rather to roar. The story then turns to Uncle Khosrove, the man before Mourad in that crazy line: an enormous man with a powerful head of black hair and the largest moustache in the San Joaquin Valley, so furious, irritable and impatient that he stopped anyone from talking by roaring, "It is no harm; pay no attention to it." Once his own son Arak ran eight blocks to the barber's shop to tell him their house was on fire, and Khosrove simply roared, "It is no harm; pay no attention to it." Mourad was considered the natural descendant of this man, although his real father was Zorab, "who was practical and nothing else" — because in that tribe a man could be the father of his son's flesh without being the father of his spirit, and the distribution of spirit was "capricious and vagrant".

Mourad made Aram get down so that he could ride alone, kicked his heels into the horse and shouted, "Vazire, run." The horse stood on its hind legs, snorted and "burst into a fury of speed that was the loveliest thing I had ever seen." Mourad raced it across a field of dry grass to an irrigation ditch, crossed it, and returned five minutes later dripping wet. Then it was Aram's turn. He leaped onto the horse and knew "the most awful fear imaginable" — the horse did not move until he kicked into its muscles. Then it ran, but instead of heading for the ditch it ran down the road to the vineyard of Dikran Halabian and began to leap over vines. The horse leaped over seven vines before Aram fell, and then it ran on. Mourad came running and told him not to worry about himself — they had to get the horse; if Aram came upon it, he should "be kindly". It took Mourad half an hour to find the horse and bring it back.

By then the whole world was awake. Mourad said they would either take the horse back or hide it until tomorrow morning, and Aram knew he would hide it, at least for a while. Mourad took it quietly to the barn of a deserted vineyard which at one time had been the pride of a farmer named Fetvajian, where there were some oats and dry alfalfa. On the way home Aram asked how long ago he had stolen the horse, and it dawned on him that Mourad had been taking these early morning rides for some time and had come for him that morning only because he knew how much Aram longed to ride. Mourad denied stealing, then admitted, "Of course not... but if we are found out, that's what you're to say. I don't want both of us to be liars. All you know is that we started riding this morning." He explained that he had "an understanding with a horse" — "a simple and honest one" — and that Aram, still a small boy, would know how to do it when he got to be thirteen.

That afternoon Uncle Khosrove came for coffee and cigarettes, and a farmer named John Byro, an Assyrian who "out of loneliness, had learned to speak Armenian", also arrived. Sighing sadly, Byro said his white horse, stolen last month, was still gone and he could not understand it. Khosrove became irritated and shouted, "It's no harm. What is the loss of a horse? Haven't we all lost the homeland? What is this crying over a horse?" Byro replied that Khosrove was a city dweller and could say that, but what of his surrey — "What good is a surrey without a horse?" Byro said he had walked ten miles to get there and that the horse had cost him sixty dollars; Khosrove roared "Pay no attention to it" and "I spit on money", then stalked out slamming the screen door. Aram's mother explained gently that Khosrove "has a gentle heart... It is simply that he is homesick and such a large man."

Aram ran to Mourad's house and found him under a peach tree, trying to repair the hurt wing of a young robin that could not fly. Aram told him about Byro and asked him to promise not to take the horse back until he had learnt to ride; he even suggested they could keep it a year. Mourad leaped to his feet and roared, "What? Are you inviting a member of the Garoghlanian family to steal? The horse must go back to its true owner." Asked when, he said, "In six months at the latest" — and threw the robin into the air, where it tried hard, almost fell twice, and at last flew away high and straight.

Early every morning for two weeks the boys took the horse out of the barn and rode it, and every morning, when it was Aram's turn, the horse leaped over grape vines and small trees, threw him and ran away. Aram still hoped to learn to ride the way his cousin rode. One morning, on the way to Fetvajian's deserted vineyard, they ran into John Byro on his way to town. Mourad, who said he had "a way with farmers", did the talking. Byro studied the horse eagerly, greeted Mourad as "son of my friends", and asked the horse's name. Mourad answered in Armenian, "My Heart". Byro called it a lovely name for a lovely horse and said he could swear it was the horse stolen from him many weeks ago. He looked into the horse's mouth and said, "Tooth for tooth... I would swear it is my horse if I didn't know your parents. The fame of your family for honesty is well known to me. Yet the horse is the twin of my horse. A suspicious man would believe his eyes instead of his heart. Good day, my young friends."

Early the following morning the boys took the horse to John Byro's vineyard and put it in the barn. The dogs followed them without making a sound — "They would at somebody else," Mourad said, "I have a way with dogs." Mourad put his arms around the horse, pressed his nose into the horse's nose, patted it, and they went away. That afternoon John Byro came to their house in his surrey and showed Aram's mother the horse that had been stolen and returned, saying, "I do not know what to think. The horse is stronger than ever. Better-tempered, too. I thank God." Uncle Khosrove, in the parlour, became irritated and shouted, "Quiet, man, quiet. Your horse has been returned. Pay no attention to it." The horse had been given back better than it was taken, and the boys were left with the quiet pride of knowing why.

3. Theme

Honesty and family honour (izzat) above everything. The Garoghlanian family is poverty-stricken but has been famous for its honesty for something like eleven centuries. Its members are "proud first, honest next", and no member could be a thief. This pride is what finally makes the boys return the horse: the moment Aram even suggests keeping it for a year, Mourad roars, "Are you inviting a member of the Garoghlanian family to steal?" The family name is worth more to them than the horse they love.

Childhood longing and the temptation it creates. Aram's "first longings had been longings to ride". Because he wants the horse so badly, he bends his own morality to fit — arguing that "stealing a horse for a ride was not the same thing as stealing something else, such as money", and that it "wouldn't become stealing until we offered to sell the horse". The story shows honestly that even a good, honest boy can talk himself into a wrong when his longing is strong enough, and that he is not proud of it afterwards.

Trust, and trust that is rewarded. John Byro looks into the horse's mouth and knows "tooth for tooth" that this is his own horse. Yet he chooses to believe "his heart instead of his eyes" because "the fame of your family for honesty is well known to me". His trust is what shames the boys into returning the horse without being forced — and his trust turns out to be justified, because the horse comes back "stronger than ever, better-tempered, too".

The innocence and warmth of childhood. The story has "no breathless adventure and exciting action"; its interest lies in the two boys, their crazy streak, the early-morning ride, the singing and roaring, and the gentle humour of Uncle Khosrove. Saroyan shows childhood as a "delightful and mysterious dream" — full of magnificent small things, forgiven later with affection.

The meaning of family and belonging. The family is poor in money but rich in identity. Its members carry the names of an old country they still remember, they speak Armenian, and they "belonged" there "at least according to some of our neighbours". The boys' honesty is not just personal — it is the family's, kept alive across centuries and across oceans.

4. Character Sketch

Aram (the narrator)

Aram is a boy of nine, telling the story years later, and he is the story's eyes and conscience. He is honest by upbringing but very human: his "first longings had been longings to ride", and when the white horse appears at four in the morning he cannot believe it because his family is too poor to buy it. He is quick with a childish argument, deciding that "stealing a horse for a ride was not the same thing as stealing something else, such as money". He knows his family's rule perfectly — "No member of the Garoghlanian family could be a thief" — and he is troubled rather than triumphant about the stolen horse. He is also a poor rider, thrown by the horse again and again, but he never stops hoping to "learn to ride the way my cousin Mourad rode". His real growth comes at the end, when he asks Mourad to keep the horse and hears the family's honour spoken out loud. He is the one who remembers the whole summer with nostalgia and quiet shame-turned-pride.

Mourad

Mourad is Aram's cousin, older and a far better rider, and "considered crazy by everybody who knew him except me" (Aram). He is the "natural descendant of the crazy streak" in the tribe — loud, impulsive, delighting in the early morning. He sings — "I mean, he began to roar" — as he rides, and he is wildly happy being alive. He comes for Aram at four in the morning purely because he knows how much Aram longs to ride. He is completely at ease with animals: he has "a way with a horse", an "understanding" that is "a simple and honest one", and he even has "a way with dogs" and with farmers. Yet the crazy boy is the most loyal to the family's code: the instant Aram suggests keeping the horse, he leaps up and roars, "Are you inviting a member of the Garoghlanian family to steal? The horse must go back to its true owner." He is careless about the letter of the law but never about the family's honour, and he is kind enough to be nursing a robin's hurt wing when Aram finds him.

John Byro

John Byro is an Assyrian farmer who "out of loneliness, had learned to speak Armenian" — a detail that shows how far from home he is. His white horse was stolen a month ago, it had cost him sixty dollars, and he had walked ten miles to Aram's house to complain; his left leg pains him, and without the horse his surrey is useless. He is, however, a generous and trusting man. He looks into the horse's mouth, finds it matches "tooth for tooth", and admits "I would swear it is my horse if I didn't know your parents" — then chooses to trust the family rather than his own eyes: "A suspicious man would believe his eyes instead of his heart." When the horse is returned he does not accuse anyone; he simply says he does not know what to think, that it is stronger than ever and better-tempered too, and thanks God. He is the story's moral mirror: his faith in the boys' family is what makes them worthy of it.

Uncle Khosrove

Uncle Khosrove is the family's earlier "crazy" man — the one before Mourad. He is "an enormous man with a powerful head of black hair and the largest moustache in the San Joaquin Valley", "so furious in temper, so irritable, so impatient" that he silences everybody by roaring, "It is no harm; pay no attention to it." He says this no matter what the subject is — even when his own son Arak runs eight blocks to tell him their house is on fire. At Aram's house he dismisses John Byro's loss: "What is the loss of a horse? Haven't we all lost the homeland?" He shouts "I spit on money" and stalks out slamming the screen door. But Aram's mother explains him kindly: "He has a gentle heart... It is simply that he is homesick and such a large man." His roar at the end — "Quiet, man, quiet. Your horse has been returned. Pay no attention to it." — is the same irritable line used, for once, to comfort somebody.

Zorab, and the Garoghlanian family as a whole

Zorab, Mourad's father, is mentioned only to make a point: he "was practical and nothing else", and so was not the father of Mourad's spirit. This is the family's belief that "a man could be the father of his son's flesh, but that did not mean that he was also the father of his spirit", and that the family's spirit is distributed in a way that is "capricious and vagrant". As a body, the Garoghlanian family is poverty-stricken — nobody, not even the old men, could understand how it kept food in its members' bellies — yet it has been famous for its honesty for something like eleven centuries, even when it was the wealthiest family in what it liked to think was the world. It is "proud first, honest next", and it "would not take advantage of anybody in the world, let alone steal". The family is the true hero of the story: its reputation is what makes John Byro trust two boys with a horse they should not have, and its pride is what makes them give it back.

5. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. Who was Mourad and why was he considered crazy? 2 marks

Answer:
Mourad was the narrator Aram's cousin, a member of the Garoghlanian family. He was "considered crazy by everybody who knew him except" Aram. He was thought to be the "natural descendant of the crazy streak" in the tribe — he came to Aram's house at four in the morning, rode a horse he had not bought, and sang, or rather roared, as he rode. He enjoyed "being alive more than anybody else who had ever fallen into the world by mistake."

Q3. Why could Aram not believe that Mourad had stolen the horse? 2 marks

Answer:
Aram could see, smell and hear the magnificent horse, yet he refused to believe it was stolen, because "No member of the Garoghlanian family could be a thief." The family was poor — Mourad could not have bought the horse — so the only other possibility was that he had stolen it, and Aram's pride in his family's eleven-century-old honesty made that impossible for him to accept. He was "delighted" and at the same time "frightened" by what he saw.

Q4. How did Aram convince himself that riding the stolen horse was not really stealing? 3 marks

Answer:
Aram tells us that "it seemed to me stealing a horse for a ride was not the same thing as stealing something else, such as money." He argues that if you were "crazy about horses" the way he and Mourad were, it wasn't stealing at all. He adds that it "wouldn't become stealing until we offered to sell the horse, which of course, I knew we would never do." In this way a boy raised to believe that no Garoghlanian could steal made a small private bargain with his own conscience so that he could enjoy the ride. The story presents this honestly as a child's self-justification, not as a real moral defence.

Q6. What did Mourad mean by "I have a way with a horse"? How did he explain it? 3 marks

Answer:
When Aram asked how he managed to get the horse to behave so nicely, Mourad said that at first the horse "wanted to run wild", but "I have a way with a horse. I can get it to want to do anything I want it to do. Horses understand me." Pressed further, he said he had "an understanding with a horse", and when Aram asked what sort of understanding, he answered, "A simple and honest one." Aram wished he knew how to reach such an understanding, and Mourad told him, "You're still a small boy... When you get to be thirteen you'll know how to do it." The phrase shows Mourad's natural, unforced closeness with animals — the same ease he shows later with the dogs at John Byro's vineyard.

Q9. Why did Mourad roar at Aram when Aram suggested keeping the horse for a year? What does this reveal about him? 4 marks

Answer:
When Aram learnt that John Byro wanted his horse, he went to Mourad and asked him to promise not to take it back "until I learn to ride", adding that "we could keep the horse a year". Mourad "leaped to his feet" and roared, "What? Are you inviting a member of the Garoghlanian family to steal? The horse must go back to its true owner." His fury reveals the real shape of his character. Though he is the "craziest" member of the family and had taken the horse without permission, he never thinks of the horse as his own — he calls it something that must go back to its "true owner". He had only ever meant to borrow it for rides. His family's honour matters more to him than his own love of riding, and it is he, not the more cautious Aram, who states the family's code most plainly.

Q10. How was the horse finally returned, and what did John Byro say afterwards? Why was this ending important? 6 marks

Answer:
Early the following morning the boys took the horse to John Byro's vineyard and put it in the barn. The dogs followed them around "without making a sound"; when Aram whispered that he thought they would bark, Mourad said, "They would at somebody else... I have a way with dogs." Mourad then "put his arms around the horse, pressed his nose into the horse's nose, patted it, and then we went away." The ending is important because the horse went back better than it came. Nothing had to be proved and no accusation was made: the boys returned what was not theirs because their family's honesty demanded it, and the horse itself bore witness to the care Mourad had given it. The boys are left with a secret pride — they had done wrong in taking it, but they had done right in giving it back, and the family's eleven-century reputation for honesty was kept intact.

Q11. The story has no breathless adventure or exciting action. What, in your opinion, makes it interesting? 6 marks

Answer:
The interest of the story lies not in events but in people and in the small, exact details of a remembered childhood. First, there is the mood of nostalgia: Aram begins with "One day back there in the good old days when I was nine and the world was full of every imaginable kind of magnificence, and life was still a delightful and mysterious dream", and everything is seen through a boy's wondering eyes. Second, there is the quiet moral tension — Aram and Mourad are poor boys from a family famous for its honesty for something like eleven centuries, riding a horse they know is stolen; the reader keeps waiting to see how that can possibly end well.

Third, the characters are memorable and funny in themselves: Mourad, who is "considered crazy by everybody" and roars instead of singing; Uncle Khosrove, with the largest moustache in the San Joaquin Valley, who answers everything with "It is no harm; pay no attention to it" even when his house is on fire. Fourth, there are the beautifully observed episodes — the four o'clock tapping at the window, the horse leaping over seven vines in Dikran Halabian's vineyard, the boys meeting John Byro on the road, and the farmer choosing to believe "his heart instead of his eyes". Above all, the story is interesting because it ends in trust rewarded: the horse comes back stronger and better-tempered, and a family's honesty is proved true rather than merely claimed.

Q12. Value-based: Did the boys return the horse because they were conscience-stricken or because they were afraid? Give reasons from the story. 6 marks

Answer:
They returned the horse because of their conscience and their family's sense of honour, not out of fear. The story gives no sign that they were ever in danger of being punished. John Byro, who had every right to accuse them, chose not to: he said, "I would swear it is my horse if I didn't know your parents. The fame of your family for honesty is well known to me", and let them go with "Good day, my young friends." No one threatened them, and no authority was ever involved. If fear were their reason, they would have returned the horse at once after that meeting — but they did not return it that day; they returned it early the following morning, quietly, before anyone was about.

The real force at work is the family's pride and honesty. Aram, who wants the horse desperately, asks Mourad to keep it for a year, and Mourad leaps up in genuine anger: "Are you inviting a member of the Garoghlanian family to steal? The horse must go back to its true owner." That is a reaction of shame and self-respect, not of fear. The boys had always known the horse was not theirs to keep, and Aram's own conscience had been uneasy from the start — he was both "delighted" and "frightened" by what he saw, and he had to invent arguments to convince himself that a ride was not stealing.

The way they return it confirms this. They slip the horse into Byro's barn, and Mourad embraces it, presses his nose to the horse's nose and pats it before leaving — the farewell of someone who loves the animal and is giving it up because it is right, not because he has been caught. The result is a horse that is "stronger than ever, better-tempered, too". Their honesty was tested by a great longing and it held; and their secret pride at the end is the reward of a conscience obeyed, not of a punishment escaped.
