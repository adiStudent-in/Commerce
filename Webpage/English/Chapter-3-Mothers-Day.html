Snapshots · Class XI · Chapter 3

Ch 3 — Mother's Day

Author: J. B. Priestley · Book: Snapshots (Class 11) · Type: One-act comic play about a mother's place in her family

1. Chapter Summary

The play opens in the living-room of the Pearsons' house in a London suburb. It is afternoon, on an afternoon in early autumn, and the room is a comfortably furnished, much lived-in room in a small suburban semi-detached villa. The set has two doors — one up left leading to the front door and the stairs, and the other in the right wall leading to the kitchen and the back door — a muslin-covered window in the left wall and possibly one in the right wall, the fireplace in the fourth wall, a settee up right, an armchair down left and one down right, and a small table with two chairs on either side of it at the centre.

When the curtain rises, Mrs Pearson (at right) and Mrs Fitzgerald (at left) are sitting opposite each other at the small table, on which are two tea-cups and saucers and the cards with which Mrs Fitzgerald has been telling Mrs Pearson's fortune. The stage direction describes Mrs Pearson as a pleasant but worried-looking woman in her forties, and Mrs Fitzgerald as older, heavier and a strong and sinister personality who is smoking. The play stresses that the two must have sharply contrasting voices — Mrs Pearson speaking in a light, flurried sort of tone, with a touch of suburban Cockney perhaps; and Mrs Fitzgerald with a deep voice, rather Irish perhaps. The five characters are Mrs Annie Pearson, George Pearson, Doris Pearson, Cyril Pearson and Mrs Fitzgerald.

Mrs Fitzgerald collects up the cards and tells her neighbour that the fortune "Could be a good fortune. Could be a bad one. All depends on yourself now. Make up your mind — and there it is." Mrs Pearson is grateful — "It's wonderful having a real fortune-teller living next door" — and asks whether Mrs Fitzgerald learnt it out East. Mrs Fitzgerald says she did: "Twelve years I had of it, with my old man rising to be Lieutenant Quartermaster. He learnt a lot, and I learnt a lot more." She then gives the play its central advice: "Put your foot down, once an' for all, an' be the mistress of your own house an' the boss of your own family." Mrs Pearson smiles apologetically and says "That's easier said than done. Besides I'm so fond of them even if they are so thoughtless and selfish. They don't mean to be..." Mrs Fitzgerald cuts in that "it'ud be better for them if they learnt to treat you properly", and that spoiling is "the ruin of them as well as you" — husbands, sons and daughters "should be taking notice of wives an' mothers, not giving 'em orders an' treating 'em like dirt". Mrs Pearson admits, dubiously, that she "keep[s] dropping a hint", and that she hates unpleasantness and doesn't know where to start. Then she glances at her watch — "Oh — good gracious! Look at the time. Nothing ready and they'll be home any minute and probably all in a hurry to go out again." As she is about to rise, Mrs Fitzgerald reaches out across the table and pulls her down: "Let 'em wait or look after themselves for once. This is where your foot goes down. Start now." She lights a cigarette from the one she has just finished.

Mrs Pearson protests that she could not keep a promise to have it out with the family, so Mrs Fitzgerald offers: "Then let me do it." Mrs Pearson is flustered, thinking a stranger's interference would be resented — but Mrs Fitzgerald says coolly, "I did. But not as me—as you." She explains the play's central device: "We change places. Or—really—bodies. You look like me. I look like you." When Mrs Pearson says that is impossible, she replies, "How do you know? Ever tried it?" and admits, "I have. Not for some time but it still ought to work. Won't last long, but long enough for what we want to do. Learnt it out East, of course, where they're up to all these tricks." She holds out her hand: "Gimme your hands, dear." — "It's your only chance. Give me your hands an' keep quiet a minute. Just don't think about anything. Now look at me." They stare at each other, and she mutters the words "Arshtatta dum—arshtatta lam—arshtatta lamdumbona..."

The stage direction is very precise here: "This little scene should be acted very carefully. We are to assume that the personalities change bodies. After the spell has been spoken, both women, still grasping hands, go lax, as if the life were out of them. Then both come to life, but with the personality of the other. Each must try to adopt the voice and mannerisms of the other. So now Mrs Pearson is bold and dominating and Mrs Fitzgerald is nervous and fluttering." This is the point students must not miss: after the swap, the woman who looks like Mrs Pearson is really Mrs Fitzgerald inside, and the woman who looks like Mrs Fitzgerald is really Mrs Pearson inside. The one wearing Mrs Pearson's body therefore speaks with Mrs Fitzgerald's personality and is marked in the play as "now with Mrs Fitzgerald's personality"; the other is marked "with Mrs Pearson's personality". The "new" Mrs Pearson snatches the cigarette — "Here — you don't want that" — and puffs contentedly, saying "Of course it's happened. Very neat. Didn't know I had it in me." The other woman looks down at herself, sees her body has changed and gives a scream of fright: "Oh — it's happened." She is alarmed that "George and the children can't see me like this", and the reply is grim: "They aren't going to—that's the point. They'll have me to deal with—only they won't know it." When she worries that they may not be able to change back, Mrs Fitzgerald (in Mrs Pearson's body) says it is "easier changing back", but refuses to do it yet — "Not likely. I've got to deal with your family first." She tells her to "Go into my house for a bit—there's nobody there—then pop back and see how we're doing", and the real Mrs Pearson crosses and hurries out through the door right. Left alone, the transformed Mrs Pearson smokes away — lighting another cigarette — and begins laying out the cards for patience on the table.

Doris Pearson comes bursting in left — a pretty girl in her early twenties, who would be pleasant enough if she had not been spoilt. Before she has taken anything in she demands, "Mum —you'll have to iron my yellow silk. I must wear it tonight." Then she sees what is happening and is astounded: "But you're smoking!" Her mother answers cool and incisive — "What d'you think I'm doing — whitewashing the ceiling?"; "That's right, dear. No law against it, is there?"; "Then you thought wrong." Doris asks whether tea is in the kitchen; the answer is "Have it where you like, dear" and "Yours isn't. I've had all I want. Might go out later and get a square meal at the Clarendon." Doris says she has "been working hard all day", and gets back, "Want it ironed? What d'you think it's going to do—iron itself?" and "I put in twice the hours you do, and get no wages nor thanks for it." Asked where she is going, Doris says "Out with Charlie Spence", and the mother is severe: "Can't you find anybody better? I wouldn't be seen dead with Charlie Spence. Buck teeth and half-witted..." Doris runs out left nearly in tears.

Cyril Pearson enters left — he is the masculine counterpart of Doris. "Hello — Mum. Tea ready?" — "No." — "Why not?" — "I couldn't bother." He tells her to "snap out of it, Ma—and get cracking", and is checked by "I've plenty of time." He asks whether she put his things out; she says, "Can't remember. But I doubt it." He protests that she promised that morning to look through them in case there was any mending, and she replies, "Yes — well now I've decided I don't like mending." When he says, "what would happen if we all talked like that?", she answers, "You all do talk like that... If it's something at your work, you get the Union to bar it. Now all that's happened is that I've joined the movement." Asked what is going on, she says only, "Changes."

Doris returns, dressing and wearing a wrap, pale and red-eyed, and is told, "You look terrible. I wouldn't wear that face even for Charlie Spence." Mrs Pearson then asks for stout — "Have we any stout left?" — and when Cyril objects she says, "To drink—you clot!" and exits right. Instantly Cyril and Doris are in a huddle... rapidly whispering. Doris says, "she's suddenly all different. An' that's what made me cry. It wasn't what she said but the way she said it—an' the way she looked." They wonder whether she has concussion; Cyril asks, "Do you mean she's barmy?" and Doris calls him a fathead. Doris giggles at the thought of how she will be when Dad comes home, and Cyril guffaws, "I'm staying in for that—two front dress circles for the first house..."

Mrs Pearson returns carrying a bottle of stout and a half-filled glass, catches them laughing and asks coldly, "why don't you both try for once to be your age?" She tells them their life is to "come in, ask for something, go out again, then come back when there's nowhere else to go". When Cyril and Doris both claim a hard day's work, the mother answers "Eight hour day!", then, "I've done my eight hours." When they say that is different, she says calmly, "It was. Now it isn't. Forty-hour week for all now. Just watch it at the weekend when I have my two days off." She adds that she might make a bed or two and do a bit of cooking as a favour, but that anyone expecting to be waited on hand and foot on Saturday and Sunday with no thanks for it is in for a nasty disappointment, and she might go off for the week-end perhaps. Doris asks if she fell or hit herself; the answer is cold: "No. But I'll hit you with something, girl, if you don't stop asking silly questions." Doris begins to cry and is told, "Stop blubbering. You're not a baby."

George Pearson now enters left — he is about fifty, fundamentally decent but solemn, self-important, pompous, and preferably... a heavy, slow-moving type. He finds Doris in tears ("You'll see", she says, and runs out). He then notices the stout and is amazed: "What are you drinking stout for?" — "Because I fancied some." — "At this time of day?" — "Yes — what's wrong with it at this time of day?" He says he has never seen her do it before and doesn't like it; she answers, "Well, that ought to be a nice change for you" and "It must be some time since you were surprised at me, George." He then mentions he forgot to say he wants no tea — there is a special snooker match night at the club — and she replies, "That's all right. There isn't any." He is aggrieved that she did not get any ready even though he doesn't want it, and she makes the play's sharpest comic point: "Annoyed because I don't get a tea for him that he doesn't even want... Going up to the bar and telling 'em you don't want a glass of beer but you're annoyed because they haven't already poured it out." Then she tells him the truth about the club: "You're one of their standing jokes. Famous. They call you Pompy-ompy Pearson because they think you're so slow and pompous." He is horrified — "Never!" — and she adds that they laugh at him behind your back, while he leaves his wife at home, night after night.

Cyril comes in with a glass of milk and a thick slice of cake, and George appeals to him: "They don't laugh at me and call me Pompy-ompy Pearson, do they?" Cyril hesitates, then admits, "Well—yes, Dad, I'm afraid they do." George exits left, slowly, almost as if somebody had hit him over the head. Cyril complains that his mother should not have told him — "You've hurt his feelings. Mine, too." — and she replies, "Sometimes it does people good to have their feelings hurt. The truth oughtn't to hurt anybody for long." She then turns on Cyril: he spends too much time and good money at greyhound races and dirt tracks and ice shows, and she doubts whether your opinion's worth having.

A sharp hurried knocking is heard off left: it is "that silly old bag from next door — Mrs Fitzgerald", and Mrs Pearson insists, "Certainly I do. Ask her in. And don't call her a silly old bag either." In comes Mrs Fitzgerald — but wearing her own body with Mrs Pearson's personality inside, hesitating in the doorway and asking anxiously "if everything's—all right..." Cyril says it isn't, and is told to be quiet; when he protests, the transformed mother shouts, "Because I tell you to—you silly, spoilt, young piecan." Mrs Fitzgerald protests nervously ("Oh—no— surely...") and is told, "Now, Mrs Fitzgerald, just let me manage my family in my own way—please!" Cyril snaps, "Mr Cyril Pearson to you, please, Mrs Fitzgerald", and stalks off into the kitchen. Whispering, Mrs Fitzgerald asks what is happening; the answer is, "Nothing much. Just putting 'em in their places, that's all. Doing what you ought to have done long since." She tells her that George now knows what the club thinks of him — "No, they don't. And now he knows it."

George enters left glumly and, seeing the visitor, greets her flatly. Mrs Fitzgerald — who doesn't know what she is saying — answers "Well — yes — I suppose so, George." George is aghast at the familiarity, and Mrs Pearson cuts in, "Your name's George, isn't it? Who d'you think you are—Duke of Edinburgh?" George complains of "No tea. Pompy-ompy Pearson. And poor Doris has been crying her eyes out upstairs" and tries to get rid of the visitor — "we're at sixes and sevens here just now" — but Mrs Pearson refuses to excuse him and demands "some decent manners in this house — or I'll know the reason why". She taunts him about the club wanting to have a good laugh; he loses his temper and shouts, "Have you gone barmy—or what?", and she jumps up savagely: "If you shout at me again like that, George Pearson, I'll slap your big, fat, silly face..." Mrs Fitzgerald moans, "Oh—no—no—no—please, Mrs Fitzgerald...", which bewilders George completely — "you're Mrs Fitzgerald. So why are you telling yourself to stop when you're not doing anything?... I think you must be tiddly." He is silenced with "Say that again, George Pearson", and mutters, "All right—all right—all right ..."

Doris returns, still wearing the wrap and miserable. Mrs Fitzgerald greets her warmly and asks about Charlie Spence; Doris is rude ("What's that to do with you?"), is sharply checked by her mother, and made to answer properly. Doris says she was going out with Charlie Spence "but now I've called it off" — because her mother has been going on at her and saying he's got buck-teeth and is half-witted. Mrs Fitzgerald (bolder now) says, "Oh—you shouldn't have said that...", and is told, "Mrs Fitzgerald, I'll manage my family—you manage yours." George, grimly, "Ticking her off now, are you, Annie?"

Then Mrs Fitzgerald gets up with sudden decision: "That's enough — quite enough." She asks George and Doris to leave them alone — "I want to have a private little talk with Mrs Fitz — [she corrects herself hastily] with Mrs Pearson... There's something here that only I can deal with." George: "I'm glad somebody can—'cos I can't. Come on, Doris." Once they are gone, she moves to left of the small table and sits and eagerly beckons Mrs Pearson to do the same. She says, "Mrs Fitzgerald, we must change back now —we really must..." because "this has gone far enough. I can see they're all miserable—and I can't bear it...". The other refuses at first — "A bit more of the same would do 'em good. Making a great difference already..." — but gives in: "Well—if you insist..." She moves to right of the table and sits, says "Quiet now. Relax." They stretch their hands across the table, stare at each other, and she mutters exactly as before: "Arshtatta dum —arshtatta lam—arshtatta lamdumbona..." They go lax and then come to life, and this time, of course, they become their proper personalities.

Mrs Fitzgerald says, "Ah well — I enjoyed that." Mrs Pearson says, "I didn't." Mrs Fitzgerald warns her: "Don't go soft on 'em again, else it'll all have been wasted..." and "They've not had as long as I'd like to have given 'em—another hour or two's rough treatment might have made it certain..." Mrs Pearson worries about how she will explain, and is told severely: "Don't you start any explaining or apologising—or you're done for." Mrs Fitzgerald presses the point: "You admitted yourself you were spoiling 'em—and they didn't appreciate you. Any apologies—any explanations—an' you'll be straight back where you were... Just give 'em a look—a tone of voice—now an' again, to suggest you might be tough with 'em if you wanted to be—an' it ought to work." She offers to test it, and asks what Mrs Pearson would like the family to do. Mrs Pearson says she would like them to stop at home for once and give me a hand with supper, and admits she like[s] a nice game of rummy — but... hardly ever ha[s] one — except at Christmas. Mrs Fitzgerald says, "That'll do then", and at the door turns with her final warning: "But remember—keep firm—or you've had it." She calls, "Hoy! You can come in now", and quietly adds, "But remember—remember—a firm hand."

George, Doris and Cyril file in through the doorway, looking apprehensively at Mrs Pearson. Mrs Fitzgerald says, "I'm just off. To let you enjoy yourself." The family looks anxiously at Mrs Pearson, who smiles; much relieved, they smile back at her. Doris asks, "Yes, Mother?" and Mrs Pearson, smiling, announces: "Seeing that you don't want to go out, I tell you what I thought we'd do." With a final "Remember!" from Mrs Fitzgerald, she looks sharply at the family — "No objections, I hope?" — and George answers humbly, "No, Mother — whatever you say..." She proposes: "I thought we'd have a nice family game of rummy—and then you children could get the supper ready while I have a talk with your father..." George says firmly, "Suits me"; Cyril answers hastily, "Yes—that's all right"; and Doris, after one hesitation and a sharp "What? Speak up!", says, "Oh—I think it would be lovely..." Mrs Pearson says good-bye to Mrs Fitzgerald ("Come again soon"), Mrs Fitzgerald answers, "Yes, dear. 'Night all—have a nice time", and exits left — and the family cluster round Mother as — the curtain falls.

Last-minute revision hook: A fortune-telling neighbour tells the overworked Mrs Pearson to "put your foot down" → the two women swap bodies at the table ("Arshtatta dum...") so that Mrs Fitzgerald is inside Mrs Pearson → the bold "mother" puts Doris, Cyril and George in their places and tells them the truth → the swap back, and Mrs Pearson keeps firm by planning a family game of rummy and making the children get the supper.

3. Theme

The self-effacing housewife and her thankless place in the family. The play opens by showing us exactly what Mrs Pearson's family life has become: she runs after everyone, takes their orders as if she were the servant in the house, and gets no wages nor thanks. Doris walks in and simply announces that her yellow silk must be ironed; Cyril wants his tea and his things put out; George expects dinner and then announces he doesn't even want it. Priestley's point is not that the family is cruel but that the mother's own habit of pleasing everybody has made her invisible, and that spoiling is "the ruin of them as well as you".

Taking a person for granted is a quiet form of injustice. No one in the family behaves like a villain; they are simply thoughtless. But the play shows the cost: the mother's days are "Stuck here day after day, week after week", her affection is met with demands, and her whole identity has been reduced to the word "Mum". Mrs Fitzgerald's blunt line — that husbands, sons and daughters "should be taking notice of wives an' mothers, not giving 'em orders an' treating 'em like dirt" — is the play's moral centre. The children even admit, once roused, that she is "suddenly all different" — proof that they had never really looked at her before.

A mother's need to assert her own identity. Mrs Pearson does not ask for a new life; she asks to be asked very nicely, thanked, and generally made a fuss of, and to have an afternoon of rummy — something she hardly ever has "except at Christmas". The forty-hour week and the two days off are the play's comic language for a serious claim: a mother's work is work, and she is entitled to a self of her own. The play's answer is that she must "put your foot down" and "be the mistress of your own house" rather than never-endingly tolerant.

The comic method — the personality swap as the device that makes the family see her value. Priestley does not let Mrs Pearson lecture her family. Instead he uses the body-swap: the two women change places. Or—really—bodies, and from that moment the woman who looks like Mrs Pearson is actually the bold, dominating Mrs Fitzgerald. The joke is that the family can only respect the mother when a stranger's personality is doing the talking inside her — and the discomfort is that they never suspected "They'll have me to deal with—only they won't know it." Humour becomes the vehicle of the message: the family's shock, George's bewilderment at being called "George" by a neighbour, and the running joke that everyone thinks the mother has concussion, barmy or tiddly, all push the audience to laugh — and then to recognise the truth.

Assertiveness must be kept up, not just performed once. The play refuses a fairy-tale ending. Mrs Fitzgerald warns that "Any apologies—any explanations—an' you'll be straight back where you were", and that they need "a firm hand". So the resolution is deliberately modest and practical: Mrs Pearson stays firm, and uses her new authority for ordinary things — a family game of rummy and the children getting the supper. The lasting lesson is that dignity in a family has to be maintained by steady self-respect, not by a single dramatic outburst.

4. Character Sketch

Mrs Annie Pearson

Mrs Pearson is, in the stage direction's words, a pleasant but worried-looking woman in her forties, who speaks in a light, flurried sort of tone, with a touch of suburban Cockney perhaps. She is fond of her family "even if they are so thoughtless and selfish", hates unpleasantness, and has spent years smiling apologetically and dropping a hint instead of speaking plainly. Her kindness has curdled into self-effacement: she runs after everyone, takes their orders as if she were the servant in the house, and puts in twice the hours her children do for no wages nor thanks. She is not weak-minded, though — she is observant and witty (her sarcasm, "whitewashing the ceiling", and the beer-at-the-club comparison, are sharp), and once her personality is replaced by Mrs Fitzgerald's she becomes bold and dominating, putting the whole family in their places. Most important is what she learns: at the end she does not apologise or explain, keeps firm, and takes charge of her own household in a small, liveable way with the rummy game and the supper — the play's version of a mother claiming her own identity.

Mrs Fitzgerald

Mrs Fitzgerald is the neighbour — older, heavier and a strong and sinister personality, a smoker, with a deep voice, rather Irish perhaps, in sharp contrast to Mrs Pearson's flurried tone. She is a real fortune-teller who lived twelve years out East, where her old man rose to be Lieutenant Quartermaster and where she learnt all these tricks, including the ability to change bodies. She is blunt, fearless and worldly: she tells Mrs Pearson plainly that she is spoiling her family, that "It's more than hints your family needs", and that they are treating her like dirt. It is her idea to "change places. Or—really—bodies", and inside Mrs Pearson's body she is cool, incisive and savage — "Didn't know I had it in me" — ordering the children about, puncturing George's pomposity, and threatening to "slap your big, fat, silly face". Yet she is not cruel: she is the one who calls the experiment off because "I can see they're all miserable—and I can't bear it", and who then coaches Mrs Pearson to "keep firm". She is the play's instrument of change and its voice of good sense.

George Pearson

George Pearson is the father — about fifty, fundamentally decent but solemn, self-important, pompous, and preferably... a heavy, slow-moving type. He is a man of habit who announces that he is all for a steady going on and who do[es]n't like surprises; even his reasons for not wanting tea (a special snooker match night at the club) are delivered as if the household should already know them. He is not a bully, but he is complacent: he complains that there is no tea and then reveals he did not want any, an absurdity the mother exposes with her beer-at-the-bar comparison. His real weakness is vanity, and the play's sharpest blow falls on it when he learns that the club men call you Pompy-ompy Pearson because they think you're so slow and pompous — and that even Cyril confirms it. He exits left, slowly, almost as if somebody had hit him over the head, but he is also decent enough to be hurt, to be quietened, and finally to accept the new order humbly: "No, Mother — whatever you say..." and, firmly, "Suits me."

Doris Pearson

Doris Pearson is the daughter — a pretty girl in her early twenties, who would be pleasant enough if she had not been spoilt. She is entirely used to being served: she bursts in and issues orders about her yellow silk before she has even looked at her mother, and she justifies her demands with the complaint that she has been working hard all day. When she doesn't get her way she sulks, goes nearly in tears, runs out, and comes back pale and red-eyed in her wrap, blaming her mother for making her cry. She is also quick to notice a change she cannot explain — "she's suddenly all different... It wasn't what she said but the way she said it" — and, with Cyril, treats the whole thing as a joke (concussion, barmy) until her own plans and her evening with Charlie Spence are disrupted. Her rudeness to Mrs Fitzgerald is checked sharply, and by the end, after one hesitation, she agrees to the family evening: "Oh—I think it would be lovely..."

Cyril Pearson

Cyril Pearson is the son, the masculine counterpart of Doris — brisk, demanding and self-absorbed. His first words on entering are "Hello — Mum. Tea ready?", and he wants his things put out and mended, telling her to "snap out of it, Ma—and get cracking" because he "Haven't too much time". He talks about work like a trade unionist — claiming his eight hour day, and being told by his mother that at home they don't do what they don't want to do while at work you get the Union to bar it. He spends too much time and good money at greyhound races and dirt tracks and ice shows, defends himself with "I've got to enjoy myself somehow", and is embarrassed into admitting in front of his father that the club men really do call George Pompy-ompy Pearson. He is also petty and sulky — he tells his mother she has hurt his feelings too, and insists on being called "Mr Cyril Pearson" by the neighbour — yet he too falls into line, agreeing hastily, "Yes—that's all right."

Q1. Name the five characters of the play and state where the action takes place. 2 marks

Answer:
The characters are Mrs Annie Pearson, her husband George Pearson, their daughter Doris Pearson, their son Cyril Pearson, and their neighbour Mrs Fitzgerald. The action takes place in the living-room of the Pearsons' house in a London suburb — a comfortably furnished, much lived-in room in a small suburban semi-detached villa — on an afternoon in early autumn. The time of the play is given simply as "The Present".

Q2. What advice does Mrs Fitzgerald give Mrs Pearson at the beginning of the play? 2 marks

Answer:
Mrs Fitzgerald tells her neighbour to stop being a doormat and to take charge of her own home. She says, "Put your foot down, once an' for all, an' be the mistress of your own house an' the boss of your own family." She warns that spoiling the family does no one any good — it is "the ruin of them as well as you" — and that husbands, sons and daughters should be "taking notice of wives an' mothers, not giving 'em orders an' treating 'em like dirt". When Mrs Pearson says she keeps dropping a hint, Mrs Fitzgerald replies, "It's more than hints your family needs."

Q3. How did Mrs Fitzgerald come to know the trick of changing personalities, and what limit does she set on it? 2 marks

Answer:
She learnt it out East, where she spent twelve years with her husband while he rose to be Lieutenant Quartermaster; as she says, "He learnt a lot, and I learnt a lot more", and it was there, "where they're up to all these tricks", that she picked up the fortune-telling and the body-change. She admits she has not used it for some time but says "it still ought to work". The limit she sets is one of time: "Won't last long, but long enough for what we want to do." She also reassures Mrs Pearson that changing back is easier — "I can do it any time we want".

Q4. How do Doris and Cyril treat their mother when they come home? 3 marks

Answer:
Both treat her as a servant rather than a mother. Doris bursts in and, before she has taken anything in, orders, "Mum —you'll have to iron my yellow silk. I must wear it tonight"; later she insists, "you're going to iron it for me... You always do." Cyril enters asking, "Hello — Mum. Tea ready?", and, when it is not, tells her to "snap out of it, Ma—and get cracking", asking whether she put his things out and reminding her that she had promised to look through them in case there was any mending. Neither asks about her day; both claim to have been working hard all day and expect to be looked after. Their mother's own summary of them is exact: they "come in, ask for something, go out again, then come back when there's nowhere else to go".

Q5. How does the transformed Mrs Pearson deal with Doris's demand about the yellow silk? 3 marks

Answer:
She refuses flatly and turns the demand back on Doris. When Doris says the dress must be ironed, she answers coolly, "Want it ironed? What d'you think it's going to do—iron itself?", and when Doris says, "No, you're going to iron it for me... You always do", she replies, "Well, this time I don't." She also destroys Doris's excuse of hard work: "don't talk rubbish to me about working hard... I put in twice the hours you do, and get no wages nor thanks for it." Asked where she is going, Doris says "Out with Charlie Spence", and the mother is deliberately rude about him — "Can't you find anybody better? I wouldn't be seen dead with Charlie Spence. Buck teeth and half-witted..." — which reduces Doris to "Oh — shut up!" and drives her out of the room nearly in tears.

Q6. What does Mrs Pearson tell George about the club, and how does he react? 3 marks

Answer:
When George mentions that he forgot to say he wants no tea because it is a special snooker match night at the club, his wife first turns his complaint upside down — "Annoyed because I don't get a tea for him that he doesn't even want" — and compares him to a man who goes to a bar and "tell[s] 'em you don't want a glass of beer but you're annoyed because they haven't already poured it out." Then she tells him the truth: "You're one of their standing jokes. Famous. They call you Pompy-ompy Pearson because they think you're so slow and pompous." He is horrified and cries "Never!", then appeals to Cyril for a denial. Cyril hesitates and admits, "Well—yes, Dad, I'm afraid they do." George slowly says "Well — I'll be—damned!" and exits left, slowly, almost as if somebody had hit him over the head.

Q7. Explain exactly how the play stages the change of personalities. Why is this the central device of the play? 4 marks

Answer:
The change is staged at the small table in the centre of the room. Mrs Fitzgerald asks for Mrs Pearson's hands — "Gimme your hands, dear... Give me your hands an' keep quiet a minute. Just don't think about anything. Now look at me" — and, taking her hands, mutters the spell: "Arshtatta dum—arshtatta lam—arshtatta lamdumbona..." The stage direction explains what the audience must accept: "We are to assume that the personalities change bodies. After the spell has been spoken, both women, still grasping hands, go lax, as if the life were out of them. Then both come to life, but with the personality of the other." So the woman who looks like Mrs Pearson is now really Mrs Fitzgerald inside — bold and dominating — and the woman who looks like Mrs Fitzgerald is really Mrs Pearson inside — nervous and fluttering. The play signals this in every speech: the first is marked "now with Mrs Fitzgerald's personality", the second "with Mrs Pearson's personality". This is the central device because it lets the family be scolded "not as me—[but] as you": the mother's own face and voice deliver the scolding, so the family cannot dismiss it, and "They'll have me to deal with—only they won't know it." The comedy (George being called "George" by a neighbour, everyone thinking the mother is barmy or tiddly) comes from the same device, and the swap back at the end restores the real Mrs Pearson — now determined to "keep firm".

Q8. Why does Mrs Fitzgerald want to change back, and what warning does she give Mrs Pearson before leaving? 4 marks

Answer:
Mrs Fitzgerald calls a halt because she cannot bear the family's misery: she gets up with sudden decision and says, "That's enough — quite enough", then tells Mrs Pearson, "Because this has gone far enough. I can see they're all miserable—and I can't bear it..." When Mrs Pearson objects that "A bit more of the same would do 'em good", she insists, "No, I can't stand any more of it—I really can't." She also feels the treatment was not long enough: "They've not had as long as I'd like to have given 'em—another hour or two's rough treatment might have made it certain..." Her warning is that Mrs Pearson must not slip back into her old softness: "Don't go soft on 'em again, else it'll all have been wasted"; "Don't you start any explaining or apologising—or you're done for"; and "Any apologies—any explanations—an' you'll be straight back where you were." Her practical advice is to "just give 'em a look—a tone of voice—now an' again, to suggest you might be tough with 'em if you wanted to be". At the door she repeats her final instruction twice over: "But remember—keep firm—or you've had it" and "But remember—remember—a firm hand."

Q9. How does the play end, and what does Mrs Pearson's plan at the end tell us about her change? 4 marks

Answer:
After the second spell the two women become their proper personalities again. Mrs Fitzgerald says, "Ah well — I enjoyed that", and Mrs Pearson answers, "I didn't". Then Mrs Fitzgerald tells the children to come in — "Hoy! You can come in now" — and leaves, saying "I'm just off. To let you enjoy yourself." George, Doris and Cyril file in... looking apprehensively at Mrs Pearson, but the family looks anxiously at Mrs Pearson, who smiles and much relieved, they smile back at her. Mrs Pearson keeps her new firmness: she looks sharply at them and asks, "No objections, I hope?"; George answers humbly, "No, Mother — whatever you say..." She then announces her plan: "I thought we'd have a nice family game of rummy—and then you children could get the supper ready while I have a talk with your father..." George says "Suits me", Cyril agrees hastily, and Doris, after one sharp "What? Speak up!", says, "Oh—I think it would be lovely..." The play ends with Mrs Fitzgerald exits left and the family cluster round Mother as — the curtain falls.

Her plan shows a change that is real but sensible. She does not throw her family out or take revenge; she asks for the ordinary things she has always wanted — a game of rummy (she says she hardly ever has one, "except at Christmas"), help with the supper, and a talk with her husband — and she gets them by being firm instead of flurried and apologetic, exactly as Mrs Fitzgerald advised.

Q10. The play is called Mother's Day. Justify the title, and show how the "day" that the mother wins for herself is a serious point and not just comedy. 4 marks

Answer:
The title works on two levels. Literally, the play turns on a single afternoon in the Pearsons' living-room in which Mrs Pearson finally gets a day of her own — a day on which she is not ironing a yellow silk dress, not making tea, and not being shouted for; instead she decides to have "a nice family game of rummy" and to let "you children... get the supper ready". Earlier in the play she spells out what such a day is worth: her life is "Stuck here day after day, week after week", she puts in twice the hours the children do "and get[s] no wages nor thanks for it", and she proposes a "Forty-hour week for all now", with "Just watch it at the weekend when I have my two days off." The title is therefore also a complaint: a mother has no day of her own at all, and must be reminded by a neighbour to "Put your foot down... an' be the mistress of your own house".

The serious point is that the "mother's day" she wins is not a holiday but a rebalancing of respect. What she asks for is small — to be "asked very nicely and thanked for everything and generally made a fuss of", and help with supper — and the play shows this is exactly what she was denied. The comedy (the body swap, the family thinking she is barmy, tiddly, or has concussion) is only the vehicle: as Mrs Fitzgerald insists, "Any apologies—any explanations—an' you'll be straight back where you were", so the change has to be held with "a firm hand".

Q11. "Mother's Day is a humorous but serious comment on the plight of the self-effacing housewife." Discuss the play's message, using the play's own words. 6 marks

Answer:
Priestley's play is built on a single question: what is a mother worth in her own house? The opening gives the answer in action rather than speech. Doris bursts in left and demands that her yellow silk be ironed before she has taken anything in; Cyril asks "Tea ready?" and reminds her she promised to sort his things and do the mending. Nobody greets her, nobody thanks her, and George, when he finally arrives, announces he wants no tea and then is aggrieved that none was ready. Mrs Fitzgerald names the situation exactly: Mrs Pearson "take[s] their orders as if you were the servant in the house", and such treatment is "the ruin of them as well as you", because "Husbands, sons, daughters should be taking notice of wives an' mothers, not giving 'em orders an' treating 'em like dirt." The housewife's plight, then, is not cruelty but being taken for granted — invisible labour with no wages nor thanks.

The second half of the play is Priestley's comment on how that invisibility is maintained. Mrs Pearson cannot reform her family by lecturing them, because she has spent years smiling apologetically, dropping a hint and saying "That's easier said than done". So the playwright supplies the body swap, letting Mrs Fitzgerald act "not as me—[but] as you" — "They'll have me to deal with—only they won't know it." Inside Mrs Pearson's body she does what the real mother never dared: she refuses to iron the dress, tells Doris that Charlie Spence has "Buck teeth and half-witted", exposes the club's nickname "Pompy-ompy Pearson", demands "some decent manners in this house", and threatens to "slap your big, fat, silly face". The effect, she reports to Mrs Fitzgerald, is "Just putting 'em in their places, that's all. Doing what you ought to have done long since" — and even the family admits, "she's suddenly all different... It wasn't what she said but the way she said it."

The play's serious message is completed by its refusal to be sentimental. Mrs Fitzgerald pulls the experiment back because "I can see they're all miserable—and I can't bear it", and warns that "Any apologies—any explanations—an' you'll be straight back where you were." So the resolution is not a fairy tale but a lesson in holding a line: Mrs Pearson keeps "a firm hand", makes no explaining or apologising, and wins back only the ordinary decencies — a game of rummy, help with the supper, a talk with her husband, and George's "No, Mother — whatever you say...". Humour has been used the whole way through (the spell, the scream of fright, everyone thinking she is barmy or tiddly), but it carries a genuine complaint: a mother is a person with a right to her own hours, her own identity and her own small pleasures, and she must "be the mistress of your own house" to be given them.

Q12. Value-based: Mrs Pearson's family changes only when they are frightened of her. Do you think the play's solution — that she must "keep firm" — is a good way to fix a family? What values should a family really run on? 6 marks

Answer:
The play does show the family changing out of shock rather than understanding. Doris and Cyril are reduced to whispering that something must be wrong with their mother — Doris wonders about concussion, Cyril asks if she is barmy — and George, faced with the changed Mrs Pearson, can only splutter, "Either I'm off my chump or you two are" and "I think you must be tiddly." What they obey is her sharpness, not her worth: Doris agrees to the family evening only after a sharp "What? Speak up!", and George submits with "No, Mother — whatever you say...". So the play is honest enough to admit that the lesson has not gone deep — as Mrs Fitzgerald says, "another hour or two's rough treatment might have made it certain."

And yet the play's solution is not really fear; it is self-respect. What Mrs Pearson wants is not obedience but ordinary consideration — to be "asked very nicely and thanked for everything and generally made a fuss of" — and she asks for nothing for herself but a game of rummy and help with the supper. The "firm hand" Mrs Fitzgerald recommends is a guard against relapse, not a weapon: she warns that "any apologies—any explanations" would put Mrs Pearson "straight back where you were", because the family had simply been trained to expect them. The play therefore suggests that a family cannot run on one member's self-effacement: it must run on mutual respect, gratitude and shared work. Mrs Pearson's forty-hour week speech — "I've done my eight hours" — makes the same point in comic form.

Two limits of this solution are worth noticing. First, it changes habits in the house, not the attitudes behind them; the children never say they were wrong. Second, the play's method is borrowed magic — the family still does not know that the change came from a neighbour's spell, and Mrs Pearson herself admits, "I don't know how I'm going to explain". A real family would need something the play cannot give it: to be told, plainly, what the mother does and to be asked to see her. Within the play, though, the outcome is fair and modest — good manners, thanks, shared supper and time together, exactly the values Mrs Pearson asks for when she says, "We'll have some decent manners in this house — or I'll know the reason why."
