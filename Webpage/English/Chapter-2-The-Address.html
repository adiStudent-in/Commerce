Snapshots · Class XI · Chapter 2

Ch 2 — The Address

Author: Marga Minco · Book: Snapshots (Class 11) · Type: Post-war short story about loss, memory and moving on

1. Chapter Summary

The story opens with the narrator standing at a door in Holland and asking, "Do you still know me?" The woman opens the door only a chink. She looks at the narrator searchingly and answers flatly, "No, I don't know you." The narrator steps closer and says, "I'm Mrs S's daughter." The woman keeps her hand on the door as though she wants to prevent it opening any further, and her face gives absolutely no sign of recognition. The narrator begins to doubt herself — she had seen this woman only once, fleetingly, and that was years ago — but then the woman lets go of the door and steps aside, and the narrator sees she is wearing her mother's green knitted cardigan, its wooden buttons rather pale from washing. When the woman notices her looking, she half hides herself again behind the door. Now the narrator knew she was right.

She asks, "Well, you knew my mother?" The woman replies, "Have you come back? I thought that no one had come back." The narrator answers, "Only me." A door opens and closes in the passage behind her and a musty smell emerges. The woman says, "I regret I cannot do anything for you." The narrator explains she has come specially on the train and wants to talk for a moment, but the woman refuses — "It is not convenient for me now... I can't see you. Another time." — and cautiously closes the door as though no one inside the house should be disturbed. The narrator stands on the step, sees the curtain in front of the bay window move, and knows someone inside is asking who she is and that the woman will say, "It was nothing." She looks at the name-plate: Dorling, in black letters on white enamel, and on the jamb, a bit higher, the number — Number 46.

Walking slowly back to the station, she thinks of her mother, who had given her the address years ago, in the first half of the War. Home for a few days, she had noticed that something about the rooms had changed and that she missed various things. Her mother, surprised she had noticed so quickly, then told her about Mrs Dorling — an old acquaintance she had not seen for years who had suddenly turned up and renewed their contact, and who had come regularly since. "Every time she leaves here she takes something home with her," the mother says. She had taken all the table silver in one go, then the antique plates that hung there; she had trouble lugging those large vases, and her mother worried she got a crick in her back from the crockery. The mother says she would never have dared ask her — "She suggested it to me herself. She even insisted. She wanted to save all my nice things. If we have to leave here we shall lose everything, she says." When the narrator asks whether she agreed that Mrs Dorling should keep everything, her mother cries that it would simply be an insult to talk like that, and points to the risk Mrs Dorling was running, each time she left their door with a full suitcase or bag. The mother looked at her reprovingly, and after that they spoke no more about it.

She reaches the station without having paid much attention to things on the way, walking in familiar places again for the first time since the War, not wanting to go further than was necessary or to upset herself with streets and houses full of memories from a precious time. On the train she sees Mrs Dorling again as she had the first time she met her: the morning after her mother had told her about her, coming downstairs she saw her mother about to see someone out — a woman with a broad back, who nodded and picked up the suitcase under the coat-rack, wearing a brown coat and a shapeless hat. Asked where she lived, the mother said, "In Marconi Street. Number 46. Remember that." The narrator had remembered it, but waited a long time to go there. Initially after the Liberation she was absolutely not interested in all that stored stuff and rather afraid of it — afraid of being confronted with things that had belonged to a connection that no longer existed, hidden away in cupboards and boxes, waiting in vain until they were put back in their place, and which had endured all those years because they were 'things'. But gradually everything became more normal again — bread was getting to be a lighter colour, there was a bed you could sleep in unthreatened, a room with a view you were more used to glancing at each day. One day she noticed she was curious about all the possessions that must still be at that address. She wanted to see them, touch, remember.

After her first visit in vain, she decided to try a second time. Now a girl of about fifteen opened the door; her mother was doing an errand. The narrator said she would wait and followed the girl along the passage, where an old-fashioned iron Hanukkah candle-holder hung next to a mirror — one they had never used because it was much more cumbersome than a single candlestick. In the living-room she stopped, horrified: "I was in a room I knew and did not know." She found herself among things she did want to see again but which oppressed her in the strange atmosphere — because of the tasteless way everything was arranged, the ugly furniture or the muggy smell that hung there — and she scarcely dared to look around. She sat and stared at the woollen table-cloth, rubbed it, followed the lines of the pattern, knowing that somewhere on the edge there should be a burn mark that had never been repaired. The girl — who had a broad back, just like her mother — made tea, poured it from a white pot whose only decoration was a gold border on the lid, and opened a box to take out spoons, saying her mother said it was antique and "We've got lots more." The narrator looked at the still life over the tea-table, remembering how as a child she had always fancied the apple on the pewter plate. The girl said, "We use it for everything... Once we even ate off the plates hanging there on the wall." The narrator found the burn mark, and said that you get so used to touching all these lovely things that you hardly look at them any more — you only notice when something is missing. She recalled how her mother once asked her to help polish the silver, and how she had asked which silver — and her mother, surprised, replied that it was the spoons, forks and knives, of course; the strange thing was that she didn't know the cutlery they ate off every day was silver. She asked the girl whether she knew what they ate with. The girl hesitated, walked to the sideboard and wanted to open a drawer — "I'll look. It's in here." The narrator jumped up: "I was forgetting the time. I must catch my train." She refused to wait for the mother and walked out, hearing behind her the jingling of spoons and forks as the girl pulled the drawer open.

At the corner of the road she looked up at the name-plate — Marconi Street — and realised she had been at Number 46. The address was correct. But now she didn't want to remember it any more. She would not go back, because the objects that are linked in your memory with the familiar life of former times instantly lose their value when, severed from them, you see them again in strange surroundings. And what could she have done with them in a small rented room where the shreds of black-out paper still hung along the windows and no more than a handful of cutlery fitted in the narrow table drawer? "I resolved to forget the address. Of all the things I had to forget, that would be the easiest."

3. Theme

The futility of clinging to objects of a lost past. The whole story turns on the discovery that things are not the same as the people and the life they belonged to. The narrator sees her mother's table silver, antique plates, vases, crockery, pewter plate, woollen table-cloth and silver cutlery standing in a house that is not her home, arranged in a tasteless way, surrounded by a muggy smell, and she feels oppressed rather than comforted. Her own conclusion is the theme in one line: the objects "instantly lose their value when, severed from them, you see them again in strange surroundings." Keeping the belongings cannot bring back the world that gave them meaning.

The pain of memory and the courage to move on. For years the narrator avoided the address. She was "afraid of being confronted with things that had belonged to a connection that no longer existed." Only when life became more normal again — lighter bread, a bed you could sleep in unthreatened — did she feel curious, and want to see them, touch, remember. But the visit gives her not the past but its absence. Her final act is therefore an act of strength, not defeat: "I resolved to forget the address. Of all the things I had to forget, that would be the easiest." Choosing to let go is how she survives.

Possessiveness set against renunciation. Mrs Dorling had taken the things away one load at a time — "Every time she leaves here she takes something home with her" — and keeps them, uses them, and shows them off ("We've got lots more"). She guards the door, half hides behind it, and pretends not to know the daughter. The narrator, by contrast, is offered the same objects simply by reaching for a drawer, and walks out without taking a single thing. The contrast between the woman who hoards and the daughter who renounces is the moral centre of the story.

The human predicament that follows war. The story shows what war leaves behind for ordinary people: a musty house, a storeroom of other people's goods, a door that opens a chink, and the terrible line — "Have you come back?... I thought that no one had come back." answered by "Only me." War scatters possessions, breaks connections, and forces people into small rented rooms where the shreds of black-out paper still hung along the windows. What remains is not a reunion but an address that has to be forgotten.

4. Character Sketch

The Narrator (Mrs S's daughter)

She is a young woman who, after the War, goes in search of her mother's belongings at Marconi Street, Number 46, in Holland. She is observant and quick: she recognises Mrs Dorling from a fleetingly seen face years ago, and finally from the green knitted cardigan with wooden buttons rather pale from washing. For a long time she avoids the address — after the Liberation she was "absolutely not interested in all that stored stuff" and afraid of being confronted with a world that no longer existed. She is sensitive and easily hurt: inside the house she stopped, horrified, felt oppressed, scarcely dared to look around, and noticed that her own voice sounded "unnatural" and "strange" in that room. Yet she is also decisive and dignified. She refuses to beg, refuses to wait, jumps up and leaves on the excuse of catching her train, and then makes the story's bravest choice — she will not take back a single object, and resolves to forget the address. Her strength lies in accepting that things cannot replace people.

Mrs Dorling

She is an old acquaintance of the narrator's mother who had not been seen for years, then suddenly turned up and renewed their contact and began to come regularly. During the War she carried the mother's possessions home in full suitcases and bags — all the table silver in one go, then the antique plates that hung there, then the large vases and the crockery — and she kept them, saying she wanted to "save all my nice things" because "if we have to leave here we shall lose everything." At the door she is guarded and false: she opens it only a chink, holds her hand on the door as though to stop it opening further, and her face gives absolutely no sign of recognition. She pretends not to know the daughter and says, "I regret I cannot do anything for you" and "It is not convenient for me now." She is possessive and comfortable with her gains — the girl reports her mother's pride in the antique box and the things "we've got lots more" — and her household simply uses the narrator's mother's treasures as everyday objects. She is not a dramatic villain but a self-serving woman who cannot even name what she has done.

The Girl (Mrs Dorling's daughter)

She is a girl of about fifteen who opens the door on the narrator's second visit and is openly friendly — she invites her in, offers her a chair and tea, and chats without suspicion. Her most telling trait is the physical echo of her mother: she has a broad back, just like her mother. She is innocent and unaware of what the objects mean: she cheerfully says "My mother says it is antique. We've got lots more," "We use it for everything," and boasts that they once even ate off the plates hanging there on the wall. When the narrator asks whether she knows that their everyday cutlery is silver, she hesitates, walks to the sideboard and wants to open a drawer to check — the exact gesture that sends the narrator out of the house.

The Narrator's Mother (Mrs S, as remembered)

She appears only through memory, but she shapes the whole story. She gave her daughter the address years ago, in the first half of the War, and made her remember it: "In Marconi Street. Number 46. Remember that." She is trusting and generous to a fault, and unwilling to suspect anyone: when her daughter asks whether she has agreed that Mrs Dorling should keep everything, she cries that it "would simply be an insult to talk like that." She notices Mrs Dorling's burdens with pity rather than resentment — worrying that she got a crick in her back from the crockery — and she thinks of the risk Mrs Dorling is running each time she carries things out. She was surprised that her daughter noticed the rooms had changed, and looked at her reprovingly when she seemed not entirely convinced. Her last wordless effect on the story is the thief's cardigan and the silver cutlery that her daughter can still recognise by touch and memory.

Q7. "Have you come back?" said the woman. "I thought that no one had come back." Does this statement give some clue about the story? If yes, what is it? 4 marks

Answer:
Yes, the statement is one of the most revealing lines in the story. The woman's question shows that she recognises the narrator as someone from the family she once knew, even though she has just said "No, I don't know you" — her later "Have you come back?" and "I thought that no one had come back" give her away. It also tells us what the War had done to the family: the narrator's answer, "Only me," means that she is the only one who has returned. The world of the address in Marconi Street belongs to people who did not come back. Finally, the line explains the woman's whole behaviour — her guarded door, her "I regret I cannot do anything for you," her "It is not convenient for me now" — for she had believed the belongings would never be claimed, and now the rightful owner's daughter is standing on her step.

Q8. Why did the narrator of the story want to forget the address? 4 marks

Answer:
The narrator wanted to forget the address because the visit taught her that the objects that are linked in your memory with the familiar life of former times instantly lose their value when, severed from them, you see them again in strange surroundings. She found her mother's things — the table-cloth with the burn mark, the pewter plate, the silver cutlery, the antique plates — in a house that had a muggy smell, ugly furniture and a tasteless arrangement, and the sight oppressed her instead of comforting her. She understood that carrying the things back would not bring her mother back. So she decided that the past itself — the address — was what she must give up. Her words close the story: "I resolved to forget the address. Of all the things I had to forget, that would be the easiest."

Q10. Compare and contrast the characters of Mrs Dorling and the narrator. 6 marks

Answer:
Mrs Dorling and the narrator stand at opposite poles of the same experience — both are looking after the same set of objects, but with completely different intentions. Mrs Dorling is possessive. She took the mother's things home "each time she leaves here" — the table silver in one go, the antique plates, the large vases, the crockery — and she keeps them, uses them and displays them, so that her daughter can boast, "My mother says it is antique. We've got lots more." She defends her gains with small, mean deceptions: she opens the door only a chink, keeps her hand on the door, claims "No, I don't know you," and says "I regret I cannot do anything for you" and "It is not convenient for me now." She is also comfortable in the possession of another family's life: the narrator finds her own mother's belongings arranged tastelessly in a house with a muggy smell, used as ordinary furniture. The narrator, by contrast, is sensitive, honest and finally free of possessiveness. She comes openly, asking "Do you still know me?" and identifying herself — "I'm Mrs S's daughter." She feels her mother's things deeply: she knows exactly where the burn mark should be on the woollen table-cloth, and remembers the apple on the pewter plate from her childhood. Yet she is not tempted for a moment. When the girl moves to the sideboard drawer to show her the silver, she jumps up, makes the excuse of catching her train, and leaves — taking nothing. Where Mrs Dorling clings and lies, the narrator lets go and tells the truth. The story's judgement is clear: Mrs Dorling keeps the objects but loses all dignity, while the narrator loses the objects and keeps her self-respect.

Q11. 'The Address' is a story of human predicament that follows war. Comment. 6 marks

Answer:
The story shows, through one small episode, what war does to ordinary people long after the fighting stops. The narrator's family is scattered: at the very door she says "Only me," and the woman's words — "Have you come back? I thought that no one had come back" — make clear that she is the only one who returned. The home she remembers is gone, and its contents survive only because they were moved out piece by piece to Marconi Street, Number 46. The mother's gesture of protection, sending her daughter away with an address to remember — "In Marconi Street. Number 46. Remember that" — turns into a burden of memory the daughter cannot bear to carry. War also leaves its marks on the daily texture of life: even after the Liberation, the narrator lives in a small rented room where the shreds of black-out paper still hung along the windows, and ordinary comforts had to return slowly — bread was getting to be a lighter colour, there was at last a bed you could sleep in unthreatened. Human relationships break too: an old acquaintance who had once been trusted becomes a woman who opens the door a chink, denies knowing the daughter, and says, "I regret I cannot do anything for you." The narrator's final insight is the deepest comment on this predicament — that the objects "instantly lose their value when, severed from them, you see them again in strange surroundings." What war takes cannot be got back by claiming its leftovers, and healing lies elsewhere: "I resolved to forget the address." The story is thus not just about one lost cupboard of silver; it is about a whole world of ordinary people left to rebuild meaning from what is left over.

Q12. The narrator walked away from her mother's belongings without taking anything. Do you think she was right? What value does her decision teach us?
[6 marks] (Value-based / Higher-order)

Answer:
Yes, the narrator was right, because the alternative was to hold on to objects that had already lost the life that made them precious. She had every opportunity to claim them: the girl invited her in, chatted with her, and even walked to the sideboard and wanted to open a drawer to show her the silver cutlery. A single word would have been enough. Instead she jumped up, said "I was forgetting the time. I must catch my train," and left without a thing. Her reason is stated plainly in the story: the objects that are linked in your memory with the familiar life of former times instantly lose their value when, severed from them, you see them again in strange surroundings. What she found in that house was not her mother's home but a tasteless, muggy room where other people used her mother's pewter plate and ate off the plates hanging on the wall. Taking such things back into a small rented room where the shreds of black-out paper still hung along the windows would have given her objects, not a life. Her decision teaches several values. The first is courage to let go: remembering something is not the same as being imprisoned by it. The second is self-respect over greed — unlike Mrs Dorling, who accumulated her mother's things one suitcase at a time, she refuses to cling, and she does not plead. The third is the value of honest attachment: her love for her mother is shown not by what she carries home but by the fact that she still knows exactly where the burn mark should be on the table-cloth. Her final line — "I resolved to forget the address. Of all the things I had to forget, that would be the easiest." — is not coldness towards her mother; it is the strength to close a door that war had already closed.
