Snapshots · Class XI · Chapter 2

Ch 2 — The Address

Author: Marga Minco · Book: Snapshots (Class 11 Supplementary Reader) · Type: Post-War short story (Holocaust backdrop, Holland) — a daughter's search for her mother's belongings

One line to remember: After the War, the narrator tracks Mrs Dorling — who "saved" her mother's belongings during the Holocaust — to Number 46, Marconi Street; but finding the treasured things stripped of love in strange surroundings, she realizes memories live in hearts, not objects, and resolves to forget the address.

1. The First Visit — Refused

After the War, the narrator rings the bell of a house in Marconi Street. A woman opens the door only a chink, stares and says, "No, I don't know you." When the narrator says, "I'm Mrs S's daughter," the woman holds the door back, gives no sign of recognition, and murmurs, "Have you come back? I thought that no one had come back." The narrator notices she is wearing her mother's green knitted cardigan, its wooden buttons pale from washing — proof she is at the right address: the name-plate reads Dorling, Number 46. But the woman pleads it is not convenient now and shuts the door; someone peeps from behind the bay-window curtain. Rejected on the very step, the narrator walks slowly back to the station.

2. Flashback — The War Years

The address had been given by her mother in the first half of the War, when the narrator was home for a few days and instantly noticed that things were missing from their rooms. Her mother explained: Mrs Dorling, an old acquaintance not seen for years, had suddenly renewed contact and now visited regularly — and every time she left, she took something home: all the table-silver in one go, the antique plates hanging on the wall, the large vases ("I'm worried she got a crick in her back from the crockery"). It was Mrs Dorling herself who suggested and insisted — she wanted to "save" everything if the family had to leave, though the mother marvelled at the risk of each trip with a full suitcase. When the daughter seemed unconvinced, her mother looked reprovingly and the subject was dropped. The narrator also remembers her only sight of Mrs Dorling: a woman with a broad back, brown coat and shapeless hat, lifting a heavy suitcase — "In Marconi Street, Number 46. Remember that," her mother had said.

3. Between Fear & Curiosity

After the Liberation the narrator was at first not interested in the stored things — even afraid of confronting belongings of a connection that no longer existed, hidden in cupboards and boxes "waiting in vain." But as life turned normal again — bread growing lighter in colour, sleep unthreatened, a familiar view from the window — curiosity woke in her: she wanted to see, touch and remember.

4. The Second Visit — Inside Number 46

On the second try a girl of about fifteen opened the door; her mother was out on an errand. In the passage hung an old-fashioned iron Hanukkah candle-holder they never used because it was more cumbersome than a single candlestick. Entering the living-room, the narrator stopped, horrified: "a room I knew and did not know" — full of her family's things but arranged tastelessly amid ugly furniture and a muggy smell. She rubbed the woollen table-cloth and found the burn mark on its edge that had never been repaired. Tea was poured from the white pot with a gold border on the lid; spoons came from the antique box ("We've got lots more"); the still-life over the tea-table held the apple on the pewter plate she had loved as a child; the girl said once they had even eaten off the plates that hung on the wall — "it wasn't anything special."

A strange voice in her, the narrator recalled how as a girl she never knew the everyday cutlery was silver until her mother asked for help polishing it. When the girl pulled open the drawer to show whether their eating cutlery was silver too, the narrator suddenly fled — "I must catch my train" — leaving behind her the jingling of spoons and forks.

5. Why She Forgets the Address

At the corner she looked up: Marconi Street, Number 46 — the address was correct, yet she no longer wished to remember it. The truth she had discovered: "the objects that are linked in your memory with the familiar life of former times instantly lose their value when, severed from them, you see them again in strange surroundings." What use would cutlery and vases be in her small rented room, where shreds of black-out paper still hung along the windows and barely a handful of cutlery fitted the narrow drawer? "I resolved to forget the address. Of all the things I had to forget, that would be the easiest."

6. Exam Corner 📝✅

Long Answer (Exam-Ready)

"Why did the narrator want to forget the address?"

During the War, Mrs Dorling had carried away the narrator's mother's precious belongings — silver, antique plates, vases, crockery — promising to "save" them. After losing her whole family in the War, the narrator clung to the hope that these objects would restore her lost world, and twice visited Number 46, Marconi Street. The first time, Mrs Dorling refused even to recognise her; the second time, surrounded by her own heirlooms displayed tastelessly in a stranger's home — the burn-marked table-cloth, the gold-bordered teapot, the pewter plate — she felt a chill rather than joy. She realised the painful truth: objects linked with the familiar life of former times instantly lose their value when, severed from them, we see them again in strange surroundings. Her mother lived in those things, not the things themselves; without the mother, the rooms, and the love that had used them, they were dead weight. Moreover, her small rented room — with black-out paper still on the windows and a drawer too narrow for the cutlery — could never hold that past. So she chose healing over hoarding: she resolved to forget the address, for of all things she had to forget, that would be the easiest.

Expected Questions & Pointers ✅

Q1. "Have you come back? I thought that no one had come back." What clue does this give?
→ It reveals the story's hidden background — the persecution and deportation of Jews (the Holocaust): Mrs Dorling never expected any member of the narrator's family to survive; the mother and the household are gone, only the daughter returns.

Q2. What hardships did the girl undergo in pre-War and post-War times?
→ Pre-War: the creeping fear of war; watching a stranger carry away her home's treasures piece by piece; separation from her mother. Post-War: total loss of family and home; loneliness; trauma so deep she feared even the memory-laden streets; and finally disillusionment when the "saved" things brought back only pain.

Q3. How did Mrs Dorling treat the narrator? Why?
→ Coldly and rudely: opened the door just a chink, denied recognition despite the green knitted cardigan, said nothing could be done, shut the door. Guilt and greed — the belongings were now hers, and acknowledging the daughter might mean returning them.

Q4. Describe the second visit.
→ The fifteen-year-old daughter lets her in; the Hanukkah candle-holder, burn mark on the woollen cloth, gold-bordered tea-pot, pewter apple-plate, wall-hung plates used casually, the silver-cutlery anecdote; the jingling of spoons and forks drives her out mid-visit.

Q5. Sketch the character of Mrs Dorling.
→ Opportunistic and greedy — exploited wartime danger to appropriate a frightened friend's valuables; impolite and inhospitable to the orphaned daughter; possessive of things she neither understood nor valued (her daughter treats antiques as everyday junk).

Q6. What is the significance of the title 'The Address'?
→ The address is the thread of hope connecting the survivor to her lost mother and past; forgetting it marks her final acceptance of loss — memory must be carried within, not retrieved in objects.

8. Theme & Message 💡

  • War's true cost is human, not material — silver and crockery survive while mothers, homes and entire families are annihilated.
  • Greed wears the mask of help — Mrs Dorling's "saving" of valuables was quiet plunder; crisis reveals character.
  • Memories live in hearts, not objects — the same table-cloth warms in memory and chills in a stranger's drawing-room.
  • Letting go is survival — forgetting the address is not betrayal but the narrator's first act of healing after trauma.
  • Human predicament after war — displacement, guilt, indifference and the struggle to rebuild identity from ashes.
