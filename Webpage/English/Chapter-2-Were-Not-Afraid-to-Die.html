Hornbill · Class XI · Chapter 2

Ch 2 — We're Not Afraid to Die... if We Can All Be Together

Authors: Gordon Cook and Alan East · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: First-person true adventure account

1. Chapter Summary

In July 1976, the narrator — a 37-year-old businessman — set sail from Plymouth, England, with his wife Mary, his son Jonathan (6) and his daughter Suzanne (7), to duplicate the round-the-world voyage made 200 years earlier by Captain James Cook. For the longest time Mary and he had dreamt of sailing in the wake of the famous explorer, and for the past 16 years they had spent all their leisure time honing their seafaring skills in British waters. Their boat was the Wavewalker, a 23 metre, 30 ton wooden-hulled beauty, professionally built and then fitted out and tested for months in the roughest weather they could find.

The first leg of their planned three-year, 105,000 kilometre journey passed pleasantly as they sailed down the west coast of Africa to Cape Town. There, before heading east, they took on two crewmen — American Larry Vigil and Swiss Herb Seigler — to help them tackle one of the world's roughest seas, the southern Indian Ocean. On the second day out of Cape Town they met strong gales, which blew continuously for weeks. The gales did not worry the narrator, but the size of the waves did — up to 15 metres, as high as their main mast. December 25 found them 3,500 kilometres east of Cape Town; despite atrocious weather they had a wonderful holiday with a Christmas tree. New Year's Day brought no improvement, and the weather did change — for the worse.

At dawn on January 2 the waves were gigantic. Sailing under only a small storm jib and still making eight knots, they dropped the jib and lashed a heavy mooring rope in a loop across the stern, double-lashed everything, went through their life-raft drill, attached lifelines, put on oilskins and life jackets — and waited. The first indication of impending disaster came at about 6 p.m. with an ominous silence: the wind dropped and the sky immediately grew dark. Then came a growing roar and an enormous cloud towered aft of the ship — but it was not a cloud. It was a wave like no other the narrator had ever seen, perfectly vertical and almost twice the height of the other waves, with a frightful breaking crest. The roar became a thunder, a tremendous explosion shook the deck, a torrent of green and white water broke over the ship, his head smashed into the wheel and he was thrown overboard. As he sank below the waves he accepted his approaching death and, losing consciousness, felt quite peaceful.

Unexpectedly his head popped out of the water. Wavewalker was near capsizing, her masts almost horizontal, when a wave hurled her upright. His lifeline jerked taut, he grabbed the guard rails and was thrown into Wavewalker's main boom; later waves tossed him around the deck like a rag doll. His left ribs cracked and his mouth filled with blood and broken teeth. Somehow he found the wheel, lined up the stern for the next wave and hung on. Then the front hatch was thrown open and Mary appeared: "We're sinking! The decks are smashed; we're full of water." Larry and Herb were pumping like madmen; broken timbers hung at crazy angles, the whole starboard side bulged inwards, and clothes, crockery, charts, tins and toys sloshed about in deep water. In the children's cabin the children said they were all right, though Sue had a big bump above her eyes.

With a hammer, screws and canvas the narrator struggled back on deck and, with the starboard side bashed open, managed to stretch canvas and secure waterproof hatch covers across the gaping holes, so that most of the water was deflected over the side. But the hand pumps began to block up with debris and the electric pump short-circuited, and the water level rose threateningly; their two spare hand pumps had been wrenched overboard, along with the forestay sail, the jib, the dinghies and the main anchor. Then he remembered another electric pump under the chartroom floor, connected it to an out-pipe and was thankful to find that it worked. The night dragged on in an endless, bitterly cold routine of pumping, steering and working the radio, and there were no replies to their Mayday calls in that remote corner of the world. Sue's head had swollen alarmingly, she had two enormous black eyes and a deep cut on her arm — she had hidden her injuries because she "didn't want to worry" them while they were trying to save everyone.

By the morning of January 3 the pumps had the water level sufficiently under control for them to take two hours' rest in rotation. But there was still a tremendous leak below the waterline, and on checking the narrator found that nearly all the boat's main rib frames were smashed down to the keel; nothing was holding up a whole section of the starboard hull except a few cupboard partitions. They had survived 15 hours since the wave hit, but Wavewalker would not hold together long enough to reach Australia. From the charts he calculated that there were two small islands a few hundred kilometres to the east, one of them Ile Amsterdam, a French scientific base. Their only hope was to reach these pinpricks in the vast ocean, and their chances would be slim unless the wind and seas abated so that they could hoist sail — the great wave had put their auxiliary engine out of action. On January 4, after 36 hours of continuous pumping, they reached the last few centimetres of water and now had only to keep pace with the water still coming in. They could not set any sail on the main mast, because pressure on the rigging would pull the damaged section of the hull apart, so they hoisted the storm jib and headed for where the narrator thought the two islands were. Mary found some corned beef and cracker biscuits, and they ate their first meal in almost two days.

Their respite was short-lived. At 4 p.m. black clouds began building up behind them; within the hour the wind was back to 40 knots and the seas were getting higher, and by dawn on January 5 their situation was again desperate. When the narrator went in to comfort the children, Jon asked, "Daddy, are we going to die?" and then went on, "we aren't afraid of dying if we can all be together — you and Mummy, Sue and I." The narrator could find no words to respond, but he left the cabin determined to fight the sea with everything he had. To protect the weakened starboard side he decided to heave-to, with the undamaged port hull facing the oncoming waves, using an improvised sea anchor of heavy nylon rope and two 22 litre plastic barrels of paraffin. That evening Mary and he sat together holding hands as more and more water came in through the broken planks; both felt the end was very near. But Wavewalker rode out the storm.

By the morning of January 6, with the wind easing, the narrator tried to get a reading on the sextant and then worked in the chartroom on wind speeds, changes of course, drift and current. The best he could determine was that they were somewhere in 150,000 kilometres of ocean looking for a 65 kilometre-wide island. While he was thinking, Sue, moving painfully, joined him — the left side of her head was now very swollen and her blackened eyes narrowed to slits — and gave him a card she had made. On the front she had drawn caricatures of Mary and him with the words: "Here are some funny people. Did they make you laugh? I laughed a lot as well." Inside was a message: "Oh, how I love you both. So this card is to say thank you and let's hope for the best." Somehow they had to make it.

He checked and rechecked his calculations — they had lost their main compass and he was using a spare which had not been corrected for magnetic variation, so he made an allowance for this and another estimate of the influence of the westerly currents that flow through this part of the Indian Ocean. About 2 p.m. he asked Larry to steer a course of 185 degrees, telling him with a conviction he did not feel that he could expect to see the island at about 5 p.m. Then, with a heavy heart, he went below and amazingly dozed off. When he woke it was 6 p.m. and growing dark; he knew they must have missed the island, and with the sail they had left they could not hope to beat back into the westerly winds. At that moment a tousled head appeared by his bunk — Jonathan, asking for a hug "because you are the best daddy in the whole world — and the best captain." Sue, right behind him, said in a matter-of-fact voice, "You found the island," and the two chorused that it was "out there in front of us… as big as a battleship." He rushed on deck and gazed with relief at the stark outline of Ile Amsterdam — only a bleak piece of volcanic rock with little vegetation, but "the most beautiful island in the world!"

They anchored offshore for the night, and the next morning all 28 inhabitants of the island cheered as they helped them ashore. With land under his feet again, the narrator's thoughts were full of Larry and Herbie, cheerful and optimistic under the direst stress, and of Mary, who stayed at the wheel for all those crucial hours — and most of all of a seven-year-old girl who did not want them to worry about a head injury that subsequently took six minor operations to remove a recurring blood clot between skin and skull, and of a six-year-old boy who was not afraid to die.

3. Theme

Courage and the will to survive. The chapter is the story of a family that refuses to give up even when their boat is breaking apart and the end seems certain. The narrator's left ribs are cracked, the boat's main rib frames are smashed to the keel, the pumps fail and the Mayday calls get no reply — yet every time a new problem appears, someone finds an answer: canvas and waterproof hatch covers, an electric pump under the chartroom floor, an improvised sea anchor, a fresh calculation on the sextant. Survival here is not luck; it is courage plus practical skill, repeated hour after hour.

Optimism as a way of enduring. The narrator remembers Larry and Herbie as "cheerful and optimistic under the direst stress", and Mary as the one who stayed at the wheel for all the crucial hours. Even when Mary and the narrator sit holding hands feeling "the end was very near", the family's optimism pulls them on. The chapter answers its own question — optimism does not remove danger, but it keeps people working long enough to be saved.

Family togetherness and love. The title itself carries the message. Jonathan's words — "we aren't afraid of dying if we can all be together — you and Mummy, Sue and I" — turn fear into strength and make the narrator "determined to fight the sea with everything I had". Sue hides her injuries because she does not want to worry her parents, and her home-made card thanking them shows love flowing in both directions. The family survives because each member thinks of the others.

The human spirit against nature's power. The sea is shown as gigantic, perfectly vertical, towering — a force no human can control. Against it stand only a small wooden boat and six people. The chapter's message is that nature cannot be defeated, only faced with preparedness, presence of mind and humility, and that human beings can endure far more than they believe.

Adventure and risk-taking. The voyage is a deliberate choice — a dream of sailing in the wake of Captain James Cook, prepared for over 16 years. The chapter does not condemn the risk; it shows both sides of it, the 105,000 kilometre dream and the terror of the 150,000 kilometres of ocean they were lost in, and it leaves the reader admiring the courage that made the journey possible.

4. Character Sketch

The Narrator (the Captain)

He is a 37-year-old businessman who has dreamt for years of sailing in the wake of Captain James Cook, and who has spent 16 years of his leisure time honing his seafaring skills. He is a careful planner — the Wavewalker is professionally built and tested "in the roughest weather we could find", and he takes on two crewmen at Cape Town for the southern Indian Ocean. Under disaster he is brave and practical: though thrown overboard with cracked ribs and a mouth full of blood, he finds the wheel, stretches canvas over the gaping holes, remembers the spare electric pump under the chartroom floor, heaves-to to protect the damaged side, and keeps rechecking his navigation despite a lost compass. He is also honest and humble — he admits he "could find no words" when Jon asks about dying, and he speaks to Larry "with a conviction I did not feel". In the end he gives the credit to others: Larry and Herbie, Mary, and his two children.

Mary

Mary is the narrator's wife and the one who had shared the dream of the voyage for the longest time. She is courageous in a quiet, steady way: when the wave strikes she bursts out of the front hatch and shouts the plain, terrible truth — "We're sinking! The decks are smashed; we're full of water" — and then takes the wheel while the narrator goes to look at the damage. She stays at the wheel "for all those crucial hours", a fact the narrator remembers with deep gratitude at the end. She is also the family's practical carer: on 4 January it is Mary who finds corned beef and cracker biscuits so they can eat their first meal in almost two days, and in the worst moment of all she sits holding hands with her husband, facing the end together rather than apart.

Jonathan (Jon)

Jonathan is the narrator's six-year-old son. He is affectionate and direct — he appears as a tousled head at his father's bunk asking "Can I have a hug?" and tells him he is "the best daddy in the whole world — and the best captain". He is also astonishingly brave for his age: on 5 January, he asks his father "Daddy, are we going to die?" and then answers his own fear with the words that give the chapter its title — "we aren't afraid of dying if we can all be together — you and Mummy, Sue and I." His simple love gives the narrator the strength to keep fighting, and the narrator ends by remembering "a six-year-old boy who was not afraid to die."

Suzanne (Sue)

Suzanne is the narrator's seven-year-old daughter. She shows a maturity far beyond her years. Injured when the wave struck — a big bump above her eyes, then two enormous black eyes, an alarmingly swollen head and a deep cut on her arm — she tells nobody, and when her father asks why she replies, "I didn't want to worry you when you were trying to save us all." Her head injury later takes six minor operations to remove a recurring blood clot between skin and skull. Even in pain, she thinks of others: she makes her parents a card with caricatures of them and the message "Oh, how I love you both. So this card is to say thank you and let's hope for the best." She is also the one who, in a calm, matter-of-fact voice, tells her despairing father, "You found the island."

Larry Vigil

Larry is the American crewman taken on at Cape Town to help the family tackle the southern Indian Ocean. He is strong and completely reliable in a crisis — when the ship is flooded he and Herb are found "pumping like madmen" in deep water among broken timbers and floating belongings. He is also the man the narrator trusts with the most critical task of all: on 6 January the narrator asks Larry to steer a course of 185 degrees, and Larry does so. The narrator's final thoughts name him first among those who were "cheerful and optimistic under the direst stress".

Herb Seigler ("Herbie")

Herb is the Swiss crewman who joins the boat with Larry at Cape Town. Like Larry, he works without complaint through the worst of the storm, pumping hour after hour to keep the water level down while the ship threatens to break apart. He never speaks in the account, but he is remembered twice at the end — once as part of the team that "pumped like madmen", and finally, affectionately, as "Herbie", among the cheerful and optimistic people who kept the narrator going under the direst stress.

Q10. Describe the shifts in the narration of the events as indicated in the three sections of the text. Give a subtitle to each section. 4 marks

The account is divided into three sections separated by breaks in the text, and each section has its own focus and mood. Section 1 — from the family's departure in July 1976 to the night of 2 January — moves from calm, confident narration to sudden violence: it describes the voyage, the crew, the growing gales, the preparations, and finally the giant wave and the first desperate hours of pumping, ending on Sue's quiet words, "I didn't want to worry you when you were trying to save us all." A suitable subtitle is "The Wave and the Fight to Stay Afloat." Section 3 — beginning "I checked and rechecked my calculations" — is tighter and more technical, following the navigator's mind as he corrects for magnetic variation, sets a course of 185 degrees, dozes off, wakes believing they have missed the island, and then hears the children's news before sighting Ile Amsterdam and landing. A suitable subtitle is "Finding Ile Amsterdam." The shifts show the narrator moving from action to reflection to resolution.

Q11. Describe in detail the events from the evening of 2 January to the family's landing at Ile Amsterdam. 6 marks

Answer:
At about 6 p.m. on 2 January an ominous silence was followed by a wave that was perfectly vertical and almost twice the height of the other waves. It broke over the ship; the narrator's head smashed into the wheel and he was thrown overboard, Wavewalker nearly capsized, his left ribs cracked, and the starboard side was bashed open so that water poured in with every wave. Mary screamed, "We're sinking!", while Larry and Herb pumped like madmen. The narrator stretched canvas and waterproof hatch covers over the holes; the hand pumps blocked up and the electric pump short-circuited, but he found another electric pump under the chartroom floor and it worked. All night they pumped, steered and worked the radio, and their Mayday calls went unanswered in that remote corner of the world. By 3 January the water level was under control and they could rest two hours in rotation, but nearly all the main rib frames were smashed down to the keel.

Their only hope now was Ile Amsterdam, a French scientific base, one of two small islands a few hundred kilometres east — pinpricks in the vast ocean. On 4 January, after 36 hours of continuous pumping, they reached the last few centimetres of water, hoisted the storm jib and ate their first meal in almost two days. But at 4 p.m. the wind returned at 40 knots, and by dawn on 5 January they were desperate again. Jon's words — "we aren't afraid of dying if we can all be together" — made the narrator determined to fight on, and he heaved-to with an improvised sea anchor of nylon rope and two 22 litre plastic barrels of paraffin. That evening he and Mary held hands, both feeling the end was very near, but Wavewalker rode out the storm. On 6 January he took a sextant reading, worked out his position — somewhere in 150,000 kilometres of ocean looking for a 65 kilometre-wide island — corrected for magnetic variation, and asked Larry to steer 185 degrees. He dozed off, woke at 6 p.m. fearing they had missed the island, and then heard from Sue and Jonathan that it lay "as big as a battleship" in front of them. They anchored offshore for the night, and the next morning all 28 inhabitants of the island cheered as they helped them ashore.

Q12. Why do you think people undertake such adventurous expeditions in spite of the risks involved? What values does the narrator's family show when they are face-to-face with death? 6 marks

Answer:
People undertake such expeditions because of a deep human desire to explore, to test themselves and to fulfil a long-cherished dream. In this chapter the narrator and Mary had dreamt for years of sailing in the wake of Captain James Cook, and they prepared for it seriously — 16 years of honing their seafaring skills, a professionally built boat, months of fitting it out and testing it in the roughest weather, a life-raft drill, and two crewmen taken on for the southern Indian Ocean. Adventure, in other words, is not recklessness in this account; it is a dream pursued with discipline. The risk is real and terrible — the family ends up lost in 150,000 kilometres of ocean — but the chapter shows that the answer to risk is preparation and calm judgement, not the abandonment of the dream.

Face-to-face with death, the family shows remarkable values. Presence of mind is the first: even with cracked ribs the narrator repairs the hull, finds a working pump, and keeps navigating. Courage and optimism are the second: Larry and Herb remain "cheerful and optimistic under the direst stress", and Mary holds the wheel for all the crucial hours. Family love and togetherness are the strongest of all — Jonathan's words, "we aren't afraid of dying if we can all be together — you and Mummy, Sue and I", turn fear into resolve, while Sue hides her injuries and makes a card of thanks so that her parents will not worry. Selflessness, responsibility towards one's family, and gratitude (the narrator's closing thoughts for Larry, Herbie, Mary and his children) complete the picture. The lesson is that hardship does not create character; it reveals it, and that a family that faces danger together can endure more than any one of its members could alone.
