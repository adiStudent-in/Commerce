Hornbill · Class XI · Poem 2

Poem 2 — The Laburnum Top

Poet: Ted Hughes · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: Nature poem using the laburnum tree and the goldfinch as a study of life and motion

1. Stanza-wise Explanation

Stanza 1

"The Laburnum top is silent, quite still
In the afternoon yellow September sunlight,
A few leaves yellowing, all its seeds fallen."

Explanation: The poem opens on a picture of complete stillness. The laburnum — the short tree with hanging branches and yellow flowers — is introduced only through its top, the upper part of the tree, and that top is "silent, quite still": not resting, but utterly quiet and motionless. The time is fixed exactly: an afternoon in yellow September sunlight, so the whole scene is bathed in one colour, yellow. The tree is already past its best — "A few leaves yellowing" tells us the leaves are turning colour and only a few are left, and "all its seeds fallen" tells us the tree has shed everything it had. The laburnum here is not a tree full of life; it is an empty, seedless, silent thing, waiting. The poet deliberately gives it no bird, no sound and no movement, so that the reader feels the emptiness before anything happens.

One-liner: In the yellow September afternoon the laburnum tree stands totally silent and still, its leaves yellowing and all its seeds already fallen — a picture of emptiness and pause.

Stanza 2

"Till the goldfinch comes, with a twitching chirrup
A suddenness, a startlement, at a branch end.
Then sleek as a lizard, and alert, and abrupt,
She enters the thickness, and a machine starts up
Of chitterings, and a tremor of wings, and trillings —
The whole tree trembles and thrills.
It is the engine of her family.
She stokes it full, then flirts out to a branch-end
Showing her barred face identity mask"

Explanation: The word "Till" turns the poem around. Into the stillness the goldfinch — the small singing bird with yellow feathers on its wings — arrives at a branch end, and her coming is not gentle: it is a "twitching chirrup", a sharp, nervous little sound joined to a quick jerking movement. The poet calls it "A suddenness, a startlement", so her arrival feels like a shock that breaks the afternoon's silence. She is then described with a simile: "sleek as a lizard, and alert, and abrupt" — smooth and quick like a lizard, watchful, and sudden in her movements. She "enters the thickness", that is, she dives into the thick mass of leaves and branches, and at once "a machine starts up / Of chitterings, and a tremor of wings, and trillings —". This is the poem's central metaphor: the tree is imagined as a machine that switches on. The "machine" is the noise of life inside it — the chitterings of the family, the tremor of wings, the trillings of the birds — all created by the bird's arrival. The result is "The whole tree trembles and thrills." The tree is personified: the dead-looking laburnum now shakes and quivers with excitement, as if it had come alive. The next line explains why: "It is the engine of her family." The tree is the engine, the power source that keeps her family going, and she "stokes it full" — she feeds and fuels it the way one stokes a fire or an engine, carrying food to the young ones hidden in the thickness. Having done her work, she "flirts out to a branch-end", moving lightly and playfully to the tip of a branch, and there she shows "her barred face identity mask" — her striped, patterned face, which is her identity, the mark that tells us exactly who she is. The laburnum is no longer empty; for these moments it is a working, humming engine.

One-liner: The goldfinch's sudden, twitching arrival switches the tree on like a machine — its chitterings, wing-tremors and trillings make the whole laburnum tremble and thrill, because the tree is the engine that feeds her family.

Stanza 3

"Then with eerie delicate whistle-chirrup whisperings
She launches away, towards the infinite
And the laburnum subsides to empty."

Explanation: The work is done and the poem closes as quickly as it opened. The goldfinch leaves "with eerie delicate whistle-chirrup whisperings" — soft, strange, fine sounds that are a mixture of whistling, chirruping and whispering, quite unlike the sharp "twitching chirrup" of her arrival. The word "eerie" makes the departure feel mysterious and a little haunting. She "launches away, towards the infinite" — she throws herself off into the open, endless sky. And with her going, the life goes too: "the laburnum subsides to empty." The tree sinks back, quietens down and becomes empty once more, exactly as it was at the start. The poem has come full circle, and the last word, "empty", leaves us with the silence we began with.

One-liner: The goldfinch flies off with soft, eerie whisperings towards the endless sky, and the laburnum sinks back into the same emptiness it began with.

3. Theme

The poem is a study of life and motion set against stillness and emptiness. It opens with the laburnum standing "silent, quite still" in the afternoon yellow September sunlight, its few leaves yellowing and all its seeds fallen — a tree that looks finished, bare and lifeless. Into this stillness arrives the goldfinch, and her coming is a "twitching chirrup", a "suddenness" and a "startlement" that breaks the silence. What follows is the heart of the poem: she enters the thickness and "a machine starts up" — the chitterings, the tremor of wings and the trillings of the family hidden inside — so that "The whole tree trembles and thrills." The poet's central idea is that the tree is "the engine of her family", which she "stokes it full": the laburnum is not merely a tree but the power source, the fuel and the shelter that keeps the bird's family alive, and the bird is the spark that makes the dead-looking tree hum with life. Hughes is showing us how life and energy are borrowed and momentary: the goldfinch pauses only long enough to "flirt out to a branch-end", show her "barred face identity mask" and then, with "eerie delicate whistle-chirrup whisperings", "launches away, towards the infinite" — and the tree immediately "subsides to empty." So the poem's message is the transience of vitality: energy arrives suddenly, does its work for a family, and leaves; vitality is a brief visit against a long background of stillness, silence and emptiness. That is why the poem both begins and ends in the same quiet — the life the goldfinch brings is real and beautiful, but it is also momentary.

4. Reference to Context (RTC) Questions

RTC 1

"The Laburnum top is silent, quite still
In the afternoon yellow September sunlight,
A few leaves yellowing, all its seeds fallen." (Stanza 1)

(a) What is the laburnum tree's condition in these lines? (b) What does the mention of "September sunlight" and "yellowing" leaves tell us about the season and the tree? (c) Name the poetic device used in "silent, quite still".

Answers (a) The laburnum tree is completely silent and motionless, with only a few yellowing leaves left and all its seeds already fallen — it looks empty and lifeless. (b) It is an afternoon in September, so the season is early autumn; the "yellow" sunlight and the yellowing leaves show the tree is past its full bloom, already shedding its leaves and seeds. (c) The words "silent, quite still" repeat the same idea of quietness and lack of movement, so the device is repetition (reinforced by the alliteration of the "s" sounds), which creates the poem's atmosphere of total stillness.

Q5. Why is the image of the engine evoked by the poet? 3 marks

Answer:
The image of the engine is evoked to show that the tree is the source of life and energy for the goldfinch's family. The poet says, "It is the engine of her family", and immediately adds, "She stokes it full" — she fills the engine up, as one stokes a fire or fuels a machine. The tree gives the family its food and shelter, just as an engine supplies the power that keeps a machine running. The image also explains the sudden change in the tree: the bird's arrival is like starting an engine, when the chitterings, the tremor of wings and the trillings all begin together and the tree "trembles and thrills."

Q6. What does the phrase "her barred face identity mask" mean? 3 marks

Answer:
The phrase describes the goldfinch's face. "Barred" means marked with stripes or bars, and these stripes are her "identity" — the pattern by which she is recognised as a goldfinch — while "mask" suggests a face that can be seen clearly, as if worn on the outside. The phrase comes at the point where she has finished her work in the tree and "flirts out to a branch-end", so the poet shows her to us plainly for a moment: the striped face that reveals who she is before she flies away. It also keeps the sense of the bird being partly hidden and mysterious inside the tree's thickness.

Q7. What do you notice about the beginning and the ending of the poem? 4 marks

Answer:
The beginning and the ending of the poem are deliberately alike, so that the poem turns full circle. It opens with "The Laburnum top is silent, quite still", an empty tree with "A few leaves yellowing, all its seeds fallen", and it closes with "And the laburnum subsides to empty." In both places the tree is silent and empty, and in between there is only a brief burst of life brought by the goldfinch. The difference is that in the first stanza the emptiness is simply stated, while at the end the tree "subsides" — it has sunk back into emptiness after being alive, so the word carries the weight of everything that has just happened. The matching beginning and ending make us feel how momentary the bird's visit and its energy were, and how stillness and emptiness are the tree's real, lasting state. The life the bird brings is real, but it is a visit, not a permanent condition.

Q8. How does the poet use sound and movement words to bring the poem alive? 4 marks

Answer:
The poet packs the poem with sound words and movement words, and the two work together. The sound words — "chirrup", "chitterings", "trillings", "whistle-chirrup whisperings" — give us the actual noise of the goldfinch and her family, and they change with the bird's mood: the arrival is a sharp "twitching chirrup", the life inside the tree is a rush of chitterings and trillings, and the departure is soft and "eerie". The movement words — "twitching", "sleek", "alert", "abrupt", "enters", "flirts out", "launches away" — give us the bird's body: quick, smooth, sudden, playful and finally shooting off. Even the tree moves: it "trembles and thrills" and then "subsides". Many of these words repeat the "ch" and "tr" sounds, which makes the poem sound like the bird it describes. The result is that the reader both hears and sees the movement of life through the still tree.

Q9. Discuss how the poem brings out the contrast between stillness and life, and what it says about the nature of vitality. 6 marks

Answer:
The whole poem is built on a contrast between stillness and life. It begins and ends in stillness: the laburnum top is "silent, quite still" in the "afternoon yellow September sunlight", with "A few leaves yellowing, all its seeds fallen", and at the very end "the laburnum subsides to empty." Between these two moments of silence comes a short, intense burst of life. The goldfinch arrives with "a twitching chirrup", "A suddenness, a startlement", moves "sleek as a lizard, and alert, and abrupt", and the moment she "enters the thickness", "a machine starts up / Of chitterings, and a tremor of wings, and trillings". The dead-looking tree "trembles and thrills" — the poet personifies it so that it seems to wake up, and the reason is given in the line "It is the engine of her family", which she "stokes it full". The tree is the engine and the fuel; the bird's family is what the engine works for. What the poem says about life is that vitality is real but momentary. The bird does not stay: she "flirts out to a branch-end", shows "her barred face identity mask", and "with eerie delicate whistle-chirrup whisperings" she "launches away, towards the infinite." The life she brought goes with her — the engine stops, and the tree "subsides to empty." So the poem presents energy and life as something borrowed and passing, and silence and emptiness as the background against which that life is even noticeable. The goldfinch's brief visit, its purpose in feeding her family, and its departure together make the poem not sad but quietly truthful: life flickers, does its work, and moves on.

Q10. The poem is about a bird that comes to a tree for her family and then leaves. What value does this small scene teach us about noticing life around us? 4 marks] [value-based / higher-order

Answer:
The poem shows a tiny, ordinary event — a goldfinch visiting a laburnum tree — and treats it as something worth the whole poem, which is itself the lesson. Nothing "important" happens: the tree is "silent, quite still", the bird arrives with "a twitching chirrup", the tree "trembles and thrills" as she "stokes it full" for "her family", and then she "launches away" and the tree "subsides to empty." Yet in those few moments we see purpose, care and effort — the bird works for her family, and the tree, which looked finished and seedless, is proved to be full of hidden life. The value the poem teaches is attention: to notice the small lives and small movements around us, and to see that the dullest, emptiest-looking thing may be an engine of life for someone. It also teaches us to accept transience — the bird's visit is brief and the emptiness returns, and the poem does not complain about it; it simply watches. In our own lives, the same lesson applies: the meaningful events are often short and quiet, and they are worth pausing for.
