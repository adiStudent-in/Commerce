Hornbill · Class XI · Chapter 5

Ch 5 — The Adventure

Author: Jayant Narlikar · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: Science fiction short story

1. Chapter Summary

Professor Gaitonde, a historian, is travelling by the Jijamata Express on the Pune–Bombay route — a train that runs considerably faster than the Deccan Queen. He notices that there are no industrial townships outside Pune; the first stop, Lonavala, comes in 40 minutes; the ghat section that follows is no different from what he knows; the train stops at Karjat only briefly and goes on at even greater speed, roaring through Kalyan. Meanwhile, his racing mind has arrived at a plan of action for Bombay: he will go to a big library and browse through history books, because that is the surest way of finding out how the present state of affairs was reached. He also plans to return to Pune and have a long talk with Rajendra Deshpande — "assuming that in this world there existed someone called Rajendra Deshpande!"

The train stops beyond the long tunnel at a small station called Sarhad, where an Anglo-Indian in uniform goes through the train checking permits. His fellow traveller, Khan Sahib, tells him, "This is where the British Raj begins. You are going for the first time, I presume?" Khan Sahib himself is bound for Peshawar: this train goes to Victoria Terminus, and from Central he will take the Frontier Mail — Bombay to Delhi, then Lahore, then Peshawar, reaching the day after tomorrow. Gaitonde listens willingly to Khan Sahib's talk about his business, for in that way he gets "some flavour of life in this India that was so different." The suburban carriages are blue and carry the letters GBMR — Greater Bombay Metropolitan Railway — with a tiny Union Jack painted on each, "a gentle reminder that we are in British territory." The train slows beyond Dadar and stops at Victoria Terminus, a station that looks remarkably neat and clean, with staff mostly made up of Anglo-Indians and Parsees along with a handful of British officers.

Emerging from the station, Gangadharpant finds himself facing an imposing building whose letters proclaim east india house, headquarters of the east india company. This is the great shock he had not expected: the Company, which history books said had been wound up shortly after the events of 1857, is here not only alive but flourishing. So history had taken a different turn, perhaps before 1857 — and he has to find out how and when. Walking along Hornby Road, he finds a different set of shops and office buildings: there is no Handloom House building, but instead Boots and Woolworth departmental stores and the imposing offices of Lloyds, Barclays and other British banks, "as in a typical high street of a town in England." He turns right along Home Street and enters the Forbes building, asking the English receptionist for Mr Vinay Gaitonde. She searches the telephone list, the staff list and the directory of employees of every branch of the firm, and finds no one of that name. It is a blow, though not totally unexpected — if he himself were dead in this world, what guarantee had he that his son would be alive? Indeed, he may not even have been born.

Characteristically unbothered about where he will stay, his main concern is the library of the Asiatic Society at the Town Hall, where he means to solve the riddle of history. To his relief the Town Hall is there and does house the library. He asks for a list of history books including his own, and his five volumes duly arrive. Volume one takes the history up to Ashoka, volume two up to Samudragupta, volume three up to Mohammad Ghori, and volume four up to the death of Aurangzeb — all exactly as he knew it. The change has evidently occurred in the last volume. Reading volume five from both ends inwards, he converges on the precise moment where history took a different turn.

That page describes the Battle of Panipat, and it mentions that the Marathas won it handsomely. Abdali was routed and chased back to Kabul by the triumphant Maratha army led by Sadashivrao Bhau and his nephew, the young Vishwasrao. The book does not go into a blow-by-blow account of the battle itself; instead it elaborates in detail its consequences for the power struggle in India. The professor reads the account avidly — the style of writing is unmistakably his, yet he is reading the account for the first time. The victory was not only a great morale booster to the Marathas but also established their supremacy in northern India; the East India Company, which had been watching these developments from the sidelines, got the message and temporarily shelved its expansionist programme. For the Peshwas, the immediate result was an increase in the influence of Bhausaheb and Vishwasrao, who eventfully succeeded his father in 1780 A.D.; the trouble-maker, Dadasaheb, was relegated to the background and eventually retired from state politics. To its dismay, the Company met its match in the new Maratha ruler, Vishwasrao. He and his brother, Madhavrao, combined political acumen with valour and systematically expanded their influence all over India, until the Company was reduced to pockets of influence near Bombay, Calcutta and Madras, just like its European rivals, the Portuguese and the French.

For political reasons, the Peshwas kept the puppet Mughal regime alive in Delhi. In the nineteenth century these de facto rulers from Pune were astute enough to recognise the importance of the technological age dawning in Europe and set up their own centres for science and technology; the Company's offered aid and experts were accepted only to make the local centres self-sufficient. The twentieth century brought about further changes inspired by the West, and India moved towards a democracy. By then the Peshwas had lost their enterprise and were gradually replaced by democratically elected bodies. The Sultanate at Delhi survived even this transition, largely because it wielded no real influence — the Shahenshah of Delhi was no more than a figurehead to rubber-stamp the 'recommendations' made by the central parliament. As he read on, Gangadharpant began to appreciate the India he had seen: a country that had not been subjected to slavery for the white man, that had learnt to stand on its feet and knew what self-respect was; from a position of strength and for purely commercial reasons it had allowed the British to retain Bombay as the sole outpost on the subcontinent, under a lease due to expire in the year 2001 according to a treaty of 1908. Yet he feels his investigations are incomplete: how did the Marathas win the battle?

Searching the books and journals before him, he finds one that gives him the clue: Bhausahebanchi Bakhar. Although he seldom relied on the Bakhars for historical evidence, he found them entertaining to read, and sometimes, buried in the graphic but doctored accounts, he could spot the germ of truth. He finds one now in a three-line account of how close Vishwasrao came to being killed: "... And then Vishwasrao guided his horse to the melee where the elite troops were fighting and he attacked them. And God was merciful. A shot brushed past his ear. Even the difference of a til (sesame) would have led to his death." At eight o'clock the librarian politely reminds him that the library is closing; he notices he is the only reader left in the magnificent hall, and asks that the books be kept for him the next morning. The librarian smiles — "Here was a user and researcher right after his heart." As the professor leaves the table he shoves some notes into his right pocket and, absent-mindedly, the Bakhar into his left.

He finds a guest house, has a frugal meal, and sets out for a stroll towards the Azad Maidan. There he finds a throng moving towards a pandal — a lecture is to take place. Force of habit takes him towards the pandal, but he is not looking at the audience; he is staring at the platform as if mesmerised. There is a table and a chair, and the presidential chair is unoccupied. The sight stirs him to the depths, and like a piece of iron attracted to a magnet he swiftly moves towards the chair. The speaker stops in mid-sentence, too shocked to continue. The audience soon finds voice: "Vacate the chair!" "This lecture series has no chairperson..." "Away from the platform, mister!" "The chair is symbolic, don't you know?" Gaitonde goes to the mike and gives vent to his views: "Ladies and gentlemen, an unchaired lecture is like Shakespeare's Hamlet without the Prince of Denmark." But the audience is in no mood to listen — they are sick of remarks from the chair, of vote of thanks, of long introductions; they only want to listen to the speaker, and they abolished the old customs long ago. Gangadharpant, who has the experience of speaking at 999 meetings and has faced the Pune audience at its most hostile, keeps on talking and becomes a target for a shower of tomatoes, eggs and other objects. Finally the audience swarms the stage to eject him bodily. And, in the crowd, Gangadharpant is nowhere to be seen.

"That is all I have to tell, Rajendra," he says at last. All he knows is that he was found in the Azad Maidan in the morning — but back in the world he is familiar with. Where exactly did he spend those two days when he was absent from here? Rajendra is dumbfounded by the narrative. He asks what the professor was doing just prior to his collision with the truck, and Gaitonde answers that he was thinking of the catastrophe theory and its implications for history. When Rajendra smiles smugly, Gaitonde produces his vital piece of evidence: a page torn out of a book. Rajendra reads the text and his face undergoes a change — gone is the smile, and in its place comes a grave expression; he is visibly moved. Gaitonde explains that he had inadvertently slipped the Bakhar into his pocket as he left the library, discovered his error when he was paying for his meal, and intended to return it the next morning — but in the melee of Azad Maidan the book was lost and only this torn-off page remained. The page describes how Vishwasrao narrowly missed the bullet, and how that event, taken as an omen by the Maratha army, turned the tide in their favour. Gangadharpant then produces his own copy of Bhausahebanchi Bakhar, opened at the relevant page, where the account runs: "... And then Vishwasrao guided his horse to the melee where the elite troops were fighting, and he attacked them. And God expressed His displeasure. He was hit by the bullet." Rajendra admits that until he saw this material evidence he had put the experience down to fantasy — "But facts can be stranger than fantasies."

Rajendra then tries to rationalise the experience on the basis of two scientific theories. First, the catastrophe theory, which Gaitonde had heard about at a seminar. Wars fought face to face on open grounds offer excellent examples of it. On the field of Panipat there was no great disparity between Abdali's troops and the opposing forces, and their armour was comparable, so a lot depended on the leadership and the morale of the troops. As history has it, the juncture at which Vishwasrao, the son of and heir to the Peshwa, was killed proved to be the turning point: his uncle Bhausaheb rushed into the melee and was never seen again (whether he was killed or survived is not known), and for the troops at that moment that blow of losing their leaders was crucial. They lost their morale and fighting spirit, and there followed an utter rout. The torn page, on the other hand, shows the course taken when the bullet missed Vishwasrao — a crucial event gone the other way, which boosted morale and provided just that extra impetus that made all the difference. Gaitonde objects that similar statements are made about the Battle of Waterloo, which Napoleon could have won, but "we live in a unique world which has a unique history" — the idea of 'it might have been' is fine for speculation but not for reality. Rajendra takes issue with him, and moves to his second point.

Second, the question of what we mean by reality. We experience it directly with our senses or indirectly via instruments — but is it limited to what we see? Experiments on very small systems, of atoms and their constituent particles, have shown that reality may not be unique: the behaviour of these systems cannot be predicted definitively even if all the physical laws governing them are known. If Rajendra fires a bullet from a gun in a given direction at a given speed, he knows where it will be at a later time — but he cannot make such an assertion for an electron. It may be here, there, anywhere; he can at best quote odds for it being found in a specified location at a specified time. Gaitonde names this: "The lack of determinism in quantum theory!" Rajendra asks him to imagine many world pictures — in one world the electron is found here, in another over there, in yet another in a still different location; once the observer finds where it is, we know which world we are talking about, but all those alternative worlds could exist just the same. Asked whether there is any contact between those many worlds, he answers, "Yes and no!" and gives the example of an electron orbiting the nucleus of an atom. It is not quite like planets around the sun, because we know the precise trajectory of a planet, whereas the electron could be orbiting in any of a large number of specified states, and these states may be used to identify the world. In state no. 1 the electron is in a state of higher energy, in state no. 2 in a state of lower energy; it can make a jump from high to low energy and send out a pulse of radiation, or a pulse of radiation can knock it out of state no. 2 into state no. 1. Such transitions are common in microscopic systems — "What if it happened on a macroscopic level?"

Gaitonde grasps the suggestion: that he made a transition from one world to another and back again. Rajendra says that fantastic though it seems, this is the only explanation he can offer: catastrophic situations offer radically different alternatives for the world to proceed, and so far as reality is concerned all alternatives are viable, but the observer can experience only one of them at a time. By making a transition Gaitonde was able to experience two worlds, one at a time — the one he lives in now, and the one where he spent two days; one has the history we know, the other a different history. The separation or bifurcation took place in the Battle of Panipat. He neither travelled to the past nor to the future; he was in the present but experiencing a different world. By the same token there must be many more different worlds arising out of bifurcations at different points of time. As for why he made the transition, Rajendra admits that if he knew the answer he would solve a great problem — there are many unsolved questions in science — but he guesses: "You need some interaction to cause a transition." Perhaps at the time of the collision Gaitonde was thinking about the catastrophe theory and its role in wars; maybe he was wondering about the Battle of Panipat; perhaps the neurons in his brain acted as a trigger. Gaitonde confirms the guess — he was indeed wondering what course history would have taken if the result of the battle had gone the other way, and that was going to be the topic of his thousandth presidential address. Rajendra laughs that he is now in the happy position of recounting a real-life experience rather than just speculating. But Gangadharpant is grave: his thousandth address was made on the Azad Maidan, when he was so rudely interrupted. The Professor Gaitonde who disappeared while defending his chair on the platform will never again be seen presiding at another meeting, and he has conveyed his regrets to the organisers of the Panipat seminar.

Last-minute revision hook: Jijamata Express on the Pune–Bombay route → Sarhad, where the British Raj begins → East India House of the East India Company still flourishing → the five volumes, and volume five's Battle of Panipat won by the Marathas (Vishwasrao survives, Abdali routed to Kabul) → Bhausahebanchi Bakhar and the torn page as proof → the unoccupied presidential chair at the Azad Maidan and the bodily ejection → Rajendra Deshpande's two theories: the catastrophe theory and many worlds / bifurcation → Gaitonde gives up presiding over meetings.

3. Theme

History is not inevitable — one small event can change everything.
The whole story turns on a single moment at the Battle of Panipat: in our history, a bullet hit Vishwasrao, the Maratha leaders were lost, morale broke and there was an utter rout; in Gaitonde's other world the shot brushed past his ear and the Marathas won handsomely. The difference of a til separates two completely different nations. The chapter therefore argues that a nation's story is not fixed in advance — it can turn on a single event gone the other way.

Science as a way of reading history.
The chapter's central intellectual move is to join two subjects that students usually keep apart — history and physics. Rajendra Deshpande refuses to dismiss Gaitonde's experience as fantasy and instead rationalises it with the catastrophe theory and with the behaviour of atoms and their constituent particles, where reality may not be unique. The message is that the same discipline of explanation used for wars can be used for electrons, and that "facts can be stranger than fantasies."

Reality and the limits of rational explanation.
Rajendra asks what we mean by reality — we experience it through our senses or through instruments, but is it limited to what we see? Quantum behaviour shows that alternatives remain viable even though an observer can experience only one at a time. Yet even Rajendra, who offers to "rationalise" the experience "on the basis of two scientific theories as known today," admits he does not know why the transition happened: "If I knew the answer I would solve a great problem. Unfortunately, there are many unsolved questions in science and this is one of them." The story honours reason but honestly marks its edges — it explains how the world could be many, without claiming to know everything about it.

Colonialism, self-respect and the road not taken.
The alternate India "had not been subjected to slavery for the white man"; it had "learnt to stand on its feet and knew what self-respect was." It deals with the British from "a position of strength and for purely commercial reasons," granting Bombay only as a leased outpost. By showing what India might have been, the chapter quietly measures what colonialism took away.

Living custom versus frozen custom.
The Azad Maidan episode is the story's sharpest satire. In one world abolishing the presidential chair is a proud reform — "We abolished the old customs long ago"; in the other, an unchaired lecture is "Hamlet without the Prince of Denmark." Gaitonde's whole identity is wrapped up in presiding over meetings, and that is exactly what he loses — a gentle comment on how habits of authority can make a person blind to a changed world.

4. Character Sketch

Professor Gaitonde (Gangadharpant)

A historian by profession and a researcher to the core — the librarian at the Town Hall smiles at him because "here was a user and researcher right after his heart." His instinct, on finding himself in an impossible Bombay, is not panic but method: he plans to go to a big library and browse through history books, because that is "the surest way of finding out how the present state of affairs was reached," and he then reads his own five volumes "from both ends inwards" to pin down the exact moment history changed. He is methodical and patient — he accepts that his son Vinay Gaitonde may never have been born in this world, and tells the receptionist nothing. He is also stubborn and a little vain about his experience on the platform: he has spoken at 999 meetings and faced the Pune audience at its most hostile, and he cannot bear to see a presidential chair unoccupied — the sight "stirred him to the depths." That stubbornness is what gets him ejected from the Azad Maidan with tomatoes and eggs, and it is what finally humbles him: the Professor Gaitonde who disappeared defending his chair "will now never be seen presiding at another meeting." He is, by his own admission, an "ignoramus historian" in physics, but honest enough to test Rajendra's theory against hard evidence — the torn page from the Bakhar.

Rajendra Deshpande

Professor Gaitonde's friend in Pune, "who would surely help him understand what had happened" — the first person he thinks of when he needs an explanation, and the reason he plans to return to Pune at all. He is rational and scientifically minded: he listens to a story that most people would call fantasy and refuses to dismiss it, saying, "I will try to rationalise your experience on the basis of two scientific theories as known today." He is not easily convinced — until he sees the material evidence he had "simply put your experience down to fantasy" — and when he reads the torn page and then Gaitonde's own copy of Bhausahebanchi Bakhar, his face changes, the smile is replaced by a grave expression, and he says, "You have given me food for thought." He is a careful teacher: he pauses "to marshall his thoughts," paces the room under "great mental strain," explains the electron example in steps, and is honest about the limits of his own science — "If I knew the answer I would solve a great problem. Unfortunately, there are many unsolved questions in science and this is one of them." His last joke — that the professor is now "in the happy position of recounting your real life experience rather than just speculating" — shows his light, affectionate side.

Khan Sahib

Gaitonde's fellow traveller on the Jijamata Express, and his first window into the other India. He is friendly and informative: he explains that the train has reached the point "where the British Raj begins," describes his route to Peshawar by the Frontier Mail, decodes GBMR as the Greater Bombay Metropolitan Railway, and points out the tiny Union Jack on each carriage as "a gentle reminder that we are in British territory." He is gregarious — he speaks "a lot about his business" — and it is precisely this ordinary talk that lets Gaitonde "get some flavour of life in this India that was so different." He is the reader's guide, filling in the world's rules without knowing that his listener is a stranger to them.

The librarian at the Town Hall

A quiet, courteous professional in the Asiatic Society library, and a small but telling figure. He politely reminds the professor at eight o'clock that the library is closing, and smiles at the request to keep the books for the morning — "Here was a user and researcher right after his heart." He is the one person in the other Bombay who treats Gaitonde exactly as he expects to be treated, which makes him the bridge between the two worlds: the same professional respect exists on both sides of the bifurcation. His courtesy is also a small piece of evidence that this is a civilised society that has "learnt to stand on its feet."

The English receptionist at the Forbes building

A minor figure with a big effect. She is efficient and thorough — she searches "the telephone list, the staff list and then through the directory of employees of all the branches of the firm" before shaking her head. Her polite question, "Are you sure he works here?", is the moment Gaitonde's alternate life collapses: Mr Vinay Gaitonde, his son, simply does not exist here. She is impersonal and business-like, and it is that very ordinariness that makes the loss so cold — a whole life erased from a directory.

Vishwasrao

The young Maratha prince — nephew of Sadashivrao Bhau and son of and heir to the Peshwa — whose fate pivots two worlds. In the history we know he was killed at the Battle of Panipat, and the loss of him and his uncle Bhausaheb broke the army's morale. In the world Gaitonde visits he survives because "a shot brushed past his ear" and the difference of a til spared him. There he becomes a ruler of great ability, who with his brother Madhavrao "combined political acumen with valour," succeeded his father in 1780 A.D., reduced the East India Company to "pockets of influence near Bombay, Calcutta and Madras," and gave the Marathas supremacy in northern India. He is not a character we meet, but he is the hinge on which the whole story's history — and its idea — turns.

Q4. What was the first great shock Gaitonde received after emerging from Victoria Terminus, and why was it a shock? 3 marks

Answer:
As he emerged from the station, Gangadharpant found himself facing an imposing building whose letters proclaimed: "east india house, headquarters of the east india company." It was a shock because the East India Company had been wound up shortly after the events of 1857 — at least, that is what history books said. Yet here it was, "not only alive but flourishing." Prepared as he was for many shocks, Professor Gaitonde had not expected this. He reasoned that history had taken a different turn, perhaps before 1857, and that he had to find out how and when it had happened.

Q5. How did Gaitonde trace the exact point at which history had taken a different turn? 3 marks

Answer:
To "solve the riddle of history," Gaitonde made his way to the library of the Asiatic Society, which was housed in the Town Hall, and asked for a list of history books including his own. His five volumes duly arrived, and he started from the beginning. Volume one took the history up to Ashoka, volume two up to Samudragupta, volume three up to Mohammad Ghori, and volume four up to the death of Aurangzeb — up to this period, history was as he knew it. So the change had evidently occurred in the last volume. Reading volume five "from both ends inwards," Gangadharpant finally converged on the precise moment where history had taken a different turn: the page describing the Battle of Panipat. The style of writing was unmistakably his own, yet he was reading the account for the first time.

Q6. What did the history book say about the Battle of Panipat in the world Gaitonde visited? 3 marks

Q7. Describe what happened at the Azad Maidan pandal. 3 marks

Answer:
Strolling towards the Azad Maidan, Gaitonde found a throng moving towards a pandal where a lecture was to take place. Force of habit took him towards the pandal, but he was not looking at the audience — he was staring at the platform as if mesmerised, because the presidential chair was unoccupied. The sight stirred him to the depths and, "like a piece of iron attracted to a magnet," he swiftly moved towards the chair. The speaker stopped in mid-sentence, too shocked to continue, and the audience found voice: "Vacate the chair!" "This lecture series has no chairperson..." "The chair is symbolic, don't you know?" Gaitonde went to the mike and gave vent to his views, saying an unchaired lecture was like Shakespeare's Hamlet without the Prince of Denmark. But the audience was in no mood to listen — they were sick of remarks from the chair, of vote of thanks and of long introductions, and they had abolished the old customs long ago. Gangadharpant, who had spoken at 999 meetings, kept on talking, became a target for a shower of tomatoes and eggs, and was finally ejected bodily when the audience swarmed the stage. And in the crowd, Gangadharpant was nowhere to be seen.

Q8. "Gangadharpant could not help comparing the country he knew with what he was witnessing around him." What kind of India did he find in the world he visited? 4 marks

Answer:
The India Gaitonde found was a country that "had not been subjected to slavery for the white man"; it had "learnt to stand on its feet and knew what self-respect was." Its history had diverged at the Battle of Panipat, where the Marathas won handsomely. For the Peshwas, the result was the rise of Bhausaheb and Vishwasrao, who eventfully succeeded his father in 1780 A.D., while the trouble-maker Dadasaheb was relegated to the background. Vishwasrao and his brother Madhavrao "combined political acumen with valour" and expanded Maratha influence all over India until the East India Company was reduced to pockets of influence near Bombay, Calcutta and Madras, just like its European rivals the Portuguese and the French. The Peshwas kept the puppet Mughal regime alive in Delhi, and in the nineteenth century these de facto rulers were astute enough to recognise the technological age dawning in Europe and to set up their own centres for science and technology, accepting the Company's aid only to become self-sufficient. In the twentieth century India moved towards democracy and the Peshwas were replaced by democratically elected bodies; the Shahenshah of Delhi survived only as a figurehead who rubber-stamped the central parliament's 'recommendations'. From a position of strength and for purely commercial reasons, this India had allowed the British to retain Bombay as the sole outpost on the subcontinent, under a lease due to expire in 2001 according to a treaty of 1908.

Q9. What was the "vital piece of evidence" Gaitonde produced, and why did it move Rajendra Deshpande? 4 marks

Q10. Explain the catastrophe theory as Rajendra Deshpande applies it to the Battle of Panipat. 4 marks

Answer:
Rajendra explains that wars fought face to face on open grounds offer excellent examples of the catastrophe theory — the theory Gaitonde had heard about at the seminar. On the field of Panipat there was "no great disparity" between Abdali's troops and the opposing Maratha forces, and their armour was comparable; so a lot depended on the leadership and the morale of the troops. As history has it, the juncture at which Vishwasrao, the son of and heir to the Peshwa, was killed proved to be the turning point. His uncle, Bhausaheb, rushed into the melee and was never seen again — whether he was killed in battle or survived is not known — and for the troops at that particular moment, "that blow of losing their leaders was crucial." They lost their morale and fighting spirit, and there followed an utter rout. What the torn page shows, Rajendra says, is "the course taken by the battle, when the bullet missed Vishwasrao" — a crucial event gone the other way, whose effect on the troops was also the opposite: it boosted their morale and provided "just that extra impetus that made all the difference." A tiny change of circumstance thus produced a completely different outcome.

Q11. How does Rajendra Deshpande use the behaviour of very small systems to explain Gaitonde's experience? 6 marks

Answer:
Rajendra begins by questioning what we mean by reality. We experience it directly with our senses or indirectly through instruments, but it is not limited to what we see. Reality may not be unique, and this has been found "from experiments on very small systems — of atoms and their constituent particles." When dealing with such systems, physicists discovered something startling: their behaviour cannot be predicted definitively even if all the physical laws governing those systems are known. His example makes this clear. If Rajendra fires a bullet from a gun in a given direction at a given speed, he knows where it will be at a later time; but he cannot make such an assertion for an electron — it may be here, there, anywhere. He can at best quote odds for it being found in a specified location at a specified time. Gaitonde names this: "The lack of determinism in quantum theory!"

Rajendra then asks him to imagine many world pictures: in one world the electron is found here, in another it is over there, and in yet another in a still different location; once the observer finds where it is, we know which world we are talking about, but all those alternative worlds could exist just the same. Asked whether there is any contact between those many worlds, he answers, "Yes and no!" and uses the image of an electron orbiting the nucleus of an atom. This is not quite like planets around the sun — we know the precise trajectory of a planet, whereas the electron "could be orbiting in any of a large number of specified states," and these states may be used to identify the world. In state no. 1 the electron is in a state of higher energy, in state no. 2 in a state of lower energy. It can make a jump from high to low energy and send out a pulse of radiation, or a pulse of radiation can knock it out of state no. 2 into state no. 1. Such transitions are common in microscopic systems — "What if it happened on a macroscopic level?"

Gaitonde sees the point at once: Rajendra is suggesting that he "made a transition from one world to another and back again." Rajendra agrees that, fantastic though it seems, this is the only explanation he can offer. His theory is that catastrophic situations offer radically different alternatives for the world to proceed; so far as reality is concerned, all alternatives are viable, but the observer can experience only one of them at a time. By making a transition, Gaitonde experienced two worlds, one at a time — the one he lives in now, and the one where he spent two days. One has the history we know, the other a different history; the separation or bifurcation took place in the Battle of Panipat. He neither travelled to the past nor to the future — he was in the present but experiencing a different world. By the same token there must be many more different worlds arising out of bifurcations at different points of time. As for why the transition happened, Rajendra admits he does not know — "If I knew the answer I would solve a great problem" — but he guesses that "you need some interaction to cause a transition," and that since Gaitonde was thinking about the catastrophe theory and the Battle of Panipat at the moment of his collision with the truck, perhaps "the neurons in your brain acted as a trigger."

Q12. "A single event may change the course of the history of a nation." Discuss this statement with reference to The Adventure. What does Professor Gaitonde finally decide about presiding over meetings, and what does that decision reveal about him? 6 marks

Answer:
The chapter is built entirely on the truth of this statement, and the single event it chooses is astonishingly small. At the Battle of Panipat, in the history we know, Vishwasrao — the son of and heir to the Peshwa — was killed and his uncle Bhausaheb rushed into the melee and was never seen again. For the troops at that moment the loss of their leaders broke their morale and fighting spirit, and "there followed an utter rout." In the world Gaitonde visits, the Bakhar records that a shot "brushed past his ear" and "even the difference of a til (sesame)" would have led to his death. That one bullet started a completely different nation: the Marathas won handsomely, Abdali was routed and chased back to Kabul, Maratha supremacy was established in northern India, and the East India Company was reduced to "pockets of influence near Bombay, Calcutta and Madras," just like its European rivals. That India was never subjected to slavery for the white man, "had learnt to stand on its feet and knew what self-respect was," ran its own centres of science and technology, moved to democracy, and allowed the British to keep Bombay only as a leased outpost. Rajendra's two theories give this a framework: the catastrophe theory explains how a small change in circumstance produces a sudden, total shift, and the behaviour of very small systems suggests that reality itself is not unique, so that a bifurcation can leave both alternatives viable.

At the end, Gangadharpant makes a decision that is easy to miss but is the story's real conclusion. He tells Rajendra that his thousandth address — the one he had planned as his thousandth presidential address, on exactly this topic — "was made on the Azad Maidan when I was so rudely interrupted." He goes on: "The Professor Gaitonde who disappeared while defending his chair on the platform will now never be seen presiding at another meeting — I have conveyed my regrets to the organisers of the Panipat seminar." The decision reveals two things about him. First, his honesty as a scholar: he cannot go on presiding and theorising about "what might have been" when he has actually lived it — he now knows he is "in the happy position of recounting your real life experience rather than just speculating." Second, his humility: the man who described an unchaired lecture as "Hamlet without the Prince of Denmark" has learnt that a tradition he thought essential can simply be abolished, and that he was ejected bodily for defending it. The lesson the chapter leaves is that history is not inevitable — a nation's future can turn on one small event and on whether its people have the self-respect to stand on their own feet.
