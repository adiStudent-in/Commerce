Hornbill · Class XI · Poem 4

Poem 4 — Childhood

Poet: Markus Natten · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: Reflective poem on the loss of childhood and the growth of independent thinking

1. Stanza-wise Explanation

Stanza 1

"When did my childhood go?
Was it the day I ceased to be eleven,
Was it the time I realised that Hell and Heaven,
Could not be found in Geography,
And therefore could not be,
Was that the day!"

Explanation: The poet begins by asking himself a question he cannot answer — "When did my childhood go?" He is not asking about a date; he is trying to find the exact moment when he stopped being a child. He offers his first guess: perhaps it was the day I ceased to be eleven — the day he crossed over from eleven to twelve, the age at which he stopped being a small child. But then he gives a sharper answer: perhaps it was the time I realised that Hell and Heaven, / Could not be found in Geography. As a child he had believed in Hell and Heaven as real places. Then, in the Geography class, he learnt about the earth — its countries, mountains, rivers, oceans — and found that Hell and Heaven appeared nowhere on the map. In the child's simple logic, if they are not in Geography, they "could not be" at all. So they do not exist. This is the moment his religious faith quietly slipped away and rational thinking took its place: he now wanted proof, not belief. The stanza ends with the wondering, almost sad exclamation "Was that the day!" — the poet is still not sure.

One-liner: Childhood began to slip away the day the boy grew out of being eleven and his Geography lesson proved to him that Hell and Heaven did not exist — the birth of rationalism and the loss of simple faith.

Stanza 2

"When did my childhood go?
Was it the time I realised that adults were not
all they seemed to be,
They talked of love and preached of love,
But did not act so lovingly,
Was that the day!"

Explanation: The poet asks the same question again and offers a second possible answer. Perhaps childhood went the day he realised that adults were not / all they seemed to be. As a small child he had looked up to grown-ups as perfect, wise and honest people. Then he began to notice something uncomfortable: "They talked of love and preached of love, / But did not act so lovingly." The adults around him were always speaking about love and advising others to be loving, yet their own behaviour did not match their words. In other words, he saw their hypocrisy — the gap between what they said and what they did. The child's innocent trust in grown-ups broke at that moment. This is the loss of faith in adults, and with it another piece of childhood disappeared, because childhood is exactly that state in which we believe grown-ups are what they claim to be.

One-liner: Childhood also went the day the boy saw through adult hypocrisy — grown-ups talked of love and preached of love but did not act so lovingly.

Stanza 3

"When did my childhood go?
Was it when I found my mind was really mine,
To use whichever way I choose,
Producing thoughts that were not those of other people
But my own, and mine alone
Was that the day!"

Explanation: The third answer is the most important one. Perhaps childhood went the day "I found my mind was really mine, / To use whichever way I choose." Till then the boy had thought what he was told to think — his ideas were borrowed from his parents, teachers and elders. Now he discovers that his mind belongs to him and he can use it whichever way I choose. Even more, he begins "Producing thoughts that were not those of other people / But my own, and mine alone." This is the arrival of independent thinking and individuality — the ability to form one's own opinions instead of repeating other people's. And here lies the poem's quiet irony: this very independence is what ends childhood. A child believes what he is given; an individual thinks for himself. Notice too that this stanza does not end with an exclamation of regret so much as of wonder — "Was that the day!"

One-liner: Childhood finally went the day the poet discovered that his mind was really his own and began producing thoughts that were his own, and mine alone — the birth of individuality and independent thinking.

Stanza 4

"Where did my childhood go?
It went to some forgotten place,
That's hidden in an infant's face,
That's all I know."

Explanation: In the last stanza the poet, for the first time, changes the question from "When" to "Where". This shift matters. Having failed to pin down the exact time his childhood left, he now asks where it went — he has stopped looking for a date and started looking for a hiding place. His answer is beautiful and simple: "It went to some forgotten place, / That's hidden in an infant's face." Childhood did not vanish from the world; it has only moved. It survives in every new-born baby, in the innocent face of an infant, in the very place the poet himself once came from — a place he has now forgotten. So childhood is not destroyed, only lost to the grown-up who has left it behind; every infant still carries it. The poem closes with the honest, humble line "That's all I know." — the poet admits that this is the limit of his understanding, and the poem ends not with a complaint but with quiet acceptance.

One-liner: Childhood did not disappear from the world — it went to some forgotten place hidden in an infant's face, and beyond that honest admission, "That's all I know."

3. Theme

Markus Natten's "Childhood" is a reflective poem about childhood as a state of innocence, faith and dependence that is lost with knowledge, experience and the growth of independent thought. The poet asks when his childhood went and offers three possible answers, each of which is a small turning point in growing up. The first is the day he ceased to be eleven and realised that Hell and Heaven could not be found in Geography, and therefore could not exist — the loss of simple religious faith and the arrival of rationalism. The second is the day he saw that adults were not all they seemed to be, because they talked of love and preached of love, but did not act so lovingly — the loss of a child's trusting belief in grown-ups, and the discovery of adult hypocrisy. The third and deepest is the day he found that his mind was really mine, free to use whichever way I choose, producing thoughts that were not those of other people but my own, and mine alone — the birth of individuality and independent thinking. It is precisely this arrival of the questioning, self-reliant mind that drives childhood away: a child accepts what he is given, while an individual insists on thinking for himself. Yet the poem does not end in sadness. In the last stanza the question shifts from "When" to "Where", and the poet answers that his childhood went to some forgotten place, that's hidden in an infant's face. Childhood is therefore not destroyed but relocated — it continues in every new-born baby, and it is only the grown-up, having moved on, who has forgotten where it lives. The wistful final line, "That's all I know," shows the poet's honesty: he has no full answer, only the certainty that something precious has gone somewhere he can no longer reach. The poem's message is that growing up is a necessary gaining of knowledge, reason and selfhood, but it is paid for with the loss of innocence, faith and unquestioning trust — and that innocence is never gone from the world, only from us, since it lives again in every infant's face.

5. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. When did the poet's childhood go? 2 marks

Answer:
The poet does not give a single definite answer, because he himself is not certain. He suggests three possible moments. It may have gone the day he ceased to be eleven and realised that Hell and Heaven could not be found in Geography; or the day he realised that adults were not all they seemed to be, since they talked of love and preached of love, but did not act so lovingly; or the day he found that his mind was really mine. After each answer he adds the doubtful cry "Was that the day!", so the poem names several days without fixing on one.

Q2. Identify the stanza that talks of each of the following: individuality, rationalism, hypocrisy. 3 marks

Answer:
The poem's four stanzas divide the process of growing up into three exact stages. Individuality is the subject of stanza 3, where the poet finds that his mind was really mine and that his thoughts were "my own, and mine alone". Rationalism is the subject of stanza 1, where the boy realises that Hell and Heaven could not be found in Geography and therefore could not be, so faith gives way to reason. Hypocrisy is the subject of stanza 2, where the poet notices that adults talked of love and preached of love, but did not act so lovingly — their words did not match their deeds. The fourth stanza stands apart, since it turns from when to where and gives the poem its answer.

Q3. What, according to the poem, is involved in the process of growing up? 3 marks

Answer:
According to the poem, growing up is not simply adding years — it is a series of realisations in which something innocent is given up. The first is the loss of religious faith: the boy finds that Hell and Heaven are not in Geography, so they cannot exist, and rationalism replaces belief. The second is the loss of faith in adults: he sees that grown-ups talked of love and preached of love, but did not act so lovingly, that is, he discovers their hypocrisy. The third, and most important, is the growth of individuality: he finds that his mind was really mine and begins producing thoughts that were not those of other people but my own, and mine alone. So growing up involves knowledge, experience and independent thinking — and the price of each is a little of one's childhood.

Q4. Explain the significance of the poet's change of question from "When did my childhood go?" to "Where did my childhood go?" 4 marks

Answer:
For the first three stanzas the poet asks the same question — "When did my childhood go?" — and each time he offers a possible day, only to end doubtfully with "Was that the day!" He is trying to fix the exact moment of his loss and cannot do it. In the fourth stanza, therefore, he changes his question to "Where did my childhood go?" This is a change of direction rather than just of wording: he stops hunting for a date and begins hunting for a place. Once he asks where, he can actually give an answer, because "It went to some forgotten place, / That's hidden in an infant's face." So the shift marks the movement of the poem from puzzled questioning to quiet resolution. It also shifts the poem's mood from regret to consolation, since it tells us that childhood is not destroyed but relocated — it still exists, in every new-born baby. And it explains the last line, "That's all I know," which is honest because what he can be certain of is not a date but a hiding place.

Q5. How does the poem show that childhood, once lost, still lives on in the world? 3 marks

Answer:
The poem's last stanza answers the poet's question by telling us that his childhood "went to some forgotten place, / That's hidden in an infant's face." The poet does not say his childhood was destroyed; he says it moved. It has gone to a place which is hidden in an infant's face — that is, it survives in the innocent face of every new-born baby, and therefore in every child who is still beginning life. The word "forgotten" is important: it is the grown-up poet who has forgotten where childhood lives, not the world that has lost it. So childhood continues to exist all around him, only not in him any more. That is why he closes with the humble line "That's all I know." — this much he is sure of, that childhood still lives on in each infant's face, even though he cannot return to it himself.

Q6. "The growth of independent thinking is what finally ends childhood." Discuss with reference to the poem. 6 marks

Answer:
The poem's third stanza makes this idea the heart of the poem. The poet asks if childhood went the day "I found my mind was really mine, / To use whichever way I choose, / Producing thoughts that were not those of other people / But my own, and mine alone." Until that moment, the boy had thought what he was told to think — his ideas came from his parents, his teachers and his elders. When he discovers that his mind belongs to him and that he can decide for himself how to use it, he stops being a child and becomes an individual. Childhood, in this poem, is a state of dependence: the child accepts the truth he is handed. Independent thinking is the opposite of that acceptance, so the moment it arrives, childhood has to go. The poet's use of the possessive pronouns — "mine", "my own", "mine alone" — stresses how completely the mind has become his own.

What makes the argument convincing is that the poem's other two answers work in the same way. In stanza 1, the boy stops accepting what he has been taught about Hell and Heaven and applies a test of his own — could it be found in Geography? — concluding that it could not be. That is already independent reasoning. In stanza 2, he stops accepting adults at their own valuation and notices that they talked of love and preached of love, but did not act so lovingly. Again, he has begun judging for himself instead of believing. So all three answers are forms of one single change: the child's habit of believing is replaced by the grown-up's habit of examining.

Yet the poem does not treat this as a tragedy without hope. In the last stanza the poet asks where his childhood went and answers that it went to "some forgotten place, / That's hidden in an infant's face." Independent thinking takes childhood away from the individual, but not from the world — every infant still has it, and the poet accepts this with the quiet, honest line "That's all I know." The poem thus balances loss with consolation: growing up must cost us our innocence, but innocence itself is never finished.

Q7. What is the poet's feeling towards childhood? Support your answer with lines from the poem. 4 marks

Answer:
The poet's feeling towards childhood is one of wistfulness, longing and quiet regret, mixed with acceptance. He speaks of childhood as a precious thing that has gone from him, and his repeated question "When did my childhood go?" shows how much he wants to know the exact moment of his loss. The cry that ends each of the first three stanzas — "Was that the day!" — is both a question and an exclamation, and it carries the ache of someone who is still not satisfied with any answer.

At the same time, his attitude is not bitter. When he asks "Where did my childhood go?" he is able to give a gentle and consoling answer: "It went to some forgotten place, / That's hidden in an infant's face." He is glad to recognise that childhood still exists in the world, even though he cannot have it back. The last line, "That's all I know," shows a mature, honest acceptance rather than complaint. So the poet looks back at his childhood with tenderness and a sense of loss, but he does not resent having grown up — he simply records what growing up cost.

Q8. Which do you think are the most poetic lines in the poem? Why? 4 marks

Answer:
Many readers would choose the whole last stanza, and especially the lines "It went to some forgotten place, / That's hidden in an infant's face, / That's all I know." These are the poem's most poetic lines for several reasons. First, they are unexpected: after three stanzas of asking when, the poet suddenly asks where, and instead of an argument he gives an image. Second, the image is concrete and tender — childhood is not an idea but a thing you could almost see, tucked away in a baby's face. Third, the words "forgotten place" and "hidden" give the lines a soft, mysterious quality, as if childhood were a secret the grown-up has mislaid. Fourth, the short closing line "That's all I know." is beautifully plain after the long questioning stanzas, and its simplicity is what makes it moving. Together these lines turn a poem of questions into a poem of quiet acceptance.

The lines of stanza 3 — "Producing thoughts that were not those of other people / But my own, and mine alone" — are also highly poetic, because the three possessive words "my own, and mine alone" are repeated like a heartbeat and make the reader feel the birth of an individual mind. But the last stanza is the more poetic of the two, since it uses an image rather than a statement to say what it means.

Q9. How does the poem "Childhood" define the difference between a child and a grown-up? 3 marks

Answer:
The poem draws the difference very simply: a child believes what he is given, while a grown-up examines it. The child in stanza 1 accepts Hell and Heaven as real simply because he has been told about them; the grown-up poet tests them against Geography and decides that they could not be. The child in stanza 2 looks up to adults as perfect; the grown-up notices that they talked of love and preached of love, but did not act so lovingly, that is, he sees their hypocrisy. The child in stanza 3 thinks other people's thoughts; the grown-up finds that his mind was really mine and produces thoughts that are "my own, and mine alone". So childhood is a state of trust and dependence, and adulthood is a state of questioning and independence — and the poem shows that the second can only be reached by giving up the first. It is worth noticing, though, that the poet does not call one better than the other; he merely records what changes.

Q10. Value-based question: The poet says his childhood is now "hidden in an infant's face". As a young person growing up, what do you think we should try to keep from our childhood even as we learn to think for ourselves? 6 marks

Answer:
The poem sets up what looks like a trade-off: to gain rationalism, individuality and independent thinking, the poet gives up his faith, his trust in adults and his childhood itself. But the last stanza quietly refuses to accept a total loss. Childhood did not vanish, it "went to some forgotten place, / That's hidden in an infant's face" — meaning that innocence is still alive in the world, in every new baby. Read closely, the poem is therefore not telling us that growing up is a tragedy; it is telling us that childhood and clear thinking are not enemies but neighbours. That is exactly the balance a young person should try to keep.

What we should consciously try to keep is the openness that a child has before he has learnt to be cynical. The poet's discoveries are all necessary ones — a person who believes Hell and Heaven are on a map is not thinking for himself, and a person who cannot see adult hypocrisy will be deceived all his life. These are hard-won gains and nobody should give them back. But there is a difference between noticing that adults talked of love and preached of love, but did not act so lovingly and concluding that all affection is fake, or between using "my own, and mine alone" thoughts and refusing to listen to anyone else. The value the poem quietly recommends is the ability to keep the trust and warmth of the child while keeping the honesty and independence of the grown-up. The line "That's all I know." is a lesson in itself: the mature mind is one that can admit the limits of its own knowledge, and that kind of humility is itself a child-like quality that never needs to be lost.

So the answer the poem leaves us with is: keep the thinking, keep the questioning, keep the mind that is "really mine" — but do not let the gains of growing up cost you the tenderness that makes an infant's face the place where childhood still lives.
