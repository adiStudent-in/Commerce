Hornbill · Class XI · Poem 5

Poem 5 — Father to Son

Poet: Elizabeth Jennings · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: Poem on the generation gap between a father and his son

1. Stanza-wise Explanation

Stanza 1

"I do not understand this child
Though we have lived together now
In the same house for years. I know
Nothing of him, so try to build
Up a relationship from how
He was when small. Yet have I killed"

Explanation: The poem opens with the father's own voice, and it opens with a confession that hurts: "I do not understand this child". This is not a stranger he is talking about — it is his own son, and the painful part is that they have "lived together now / In the same house for years". Sharing a house has not given him any knowledge of his boy: he knows "Nothing of him". So he tries to do something about it — he tries to "build / Up a relationship", but notice the material he has to build it with: not the son he meets today, but "how / He was when small", the little boy he once knew. The father is trying to raise a relationship out of an old memory. The stanza then turns into a question the father asks himself: "Yet have I killed / The seed I spent" — have I myself destroyed what I raised? The seed is the son, the growing life the father spent his love and care on. It is the poem's first real image of helplessness and self-doubt.

One-liner: The father admits he does not understand his own son even after years in the same house, and begins to wonder whether he himself has destroyed what he raised.

Stanza 2

"The seed I spent or sown it where
The land is his and none of mine?
We speak like strangers, there’s no sign
Of understanding in the air.
This child is built to my design
Yet what he loves I cannot share."

Explanation: The father's question finishes here: has he killed the seed he spent, or has he "sown it where / The land is his and none of mine"? In other words — is the boy's life a field that belongs to the son and to no one else, a world the father has no share in? That second possibility is just as painful as the first. Then comes the poem's plainest statement of the distance between them: "We speak like strangers". They do talk, but talking has not produced any "sign / Of understanding in the air" — the air between them is empty of understanding. The last two lines carry the ache of blood-relationship and difference at once: "This child is built to my design" — the son is his own flesh, his own making — "Yet what he loves I cannot share". The father and son are close in blood and completely separate in taste, in feeling, in the things they care about. That "Yet" is the wall between them.

One-liner: Father and son talk but remain strangers — the boy is the father's own making, and yet everything the boy loves lies outside the father's reach.

Stanza 3

"Silence surrounds us. I would have
Him prodigal, returning to
His father’s house, the home he knew,
Rather than see him make and move
His world. I would forgive him too,
Shaping from sorrow a new love."

Explanation: The stanza begins with an image of a wall: "Silence surrounds us." They do not fight loudly or shout; they are wrapped in a silence that is worse than words. Then the father says what he really wants. He "would have / Him prodigal, returning to / His father's house, the home he knew" — he would rather his son came back home, like a son who has gone away and returns, than to "see him make and move / His world", building and shifting a separate world of his own. The father is not asking for obedience; he is asking for nearness, for the old home to mean something again. And then the most generous line of the poem: "I would forgive him too, / Shaping from sorrow a new love." He is ready to forgive, without waiting for the son to ask. And the forgiveness is not just a wiping away — it is creation: the father wants to take his own sorrow and shape it into a new love, a fresh relationship made out of the pain of the old one.

One-liner: Wrapped in silence, the father longs for his son to come home like the prodigal, and out of his own sorrow he wants to shape a new love.

Stanza 4

"Father and son, we both must live
On the same globe and the same land,
He speaks: I cannot understand
Myself, why anger grows from grief.
We each put out an empty hand,
Longing for something to forgive."

Explanation: The last stanza states the whole tragedy in one simple fact. "Father and son, we both must live / On the same globe and the same land" — they share the planet, the country, the roof, and yet they cannot share their lives. Then "He speaks:" — the son too has a voice in this poem, even if only for a line and a half. And what does he say? That he "cannot understand / Myself, why anger grows from grief". This is the poem's deepest insight: their anger with each other is not hatred — it is grief that has grown sharp and turned into irritation. The father's rebukes and the son's sulks are both really sorrow about a love that has gone quiet. Then comes the closing picture, and it is unforgettable: "We each put out an empty hand, / Longing for something to forgive." Both of them reach out — but the hand holds nothing, gives nothing, receives nothing. The gesture of love is made and never completed. And yet those outstretched hands are the poem's hope too: both father and son long to forgive, and where the longing to forgive still exists, the distance is not final.

One-liner: They share the same world but not the same life; both stretch out an empty hand, longing for something to forgive.

3. Theme

Father to Son is a poem about the generation gap — the quiet, aching distance that opens between a father and his son even when they live under the same roof. The father speaks, and his very first words are the poem's key line: "I do not understand this child / Though we have lived together now / In the same house for years." Physical closeness has brought no understanding at all; they "speak like strangers", with "no sign / Of understanding in the air". What makes the poem so painful is that the father blames nobody but himself and cannot even decide what has gone wrong: "Yet have I killed / The seed I spent or sown it where / The land is his and none of mine?" His son is "built to my design", and yet "what he loves I cannot share" — the closest possible blood and the widest possible difference, summed up in the small hard word "Yet". Around them, meanwhile, "Silence surrounds us", and the father's deepest wish is simply for nearness: he would rather have his boy prodigal, "returning to / His father's house, the home he knew", than watch him "make and move / His world" alone. This is love without communication — a love that is completely sincere and completely unable to cross the gap, a father whose clumsy, awkward attempts to "build / Up a relationship" have to start from the memory of "how / He was when small". His helplessness is the helplessness of not knowing; and his one act of strength is forgiveness that asks for nothing in return — "I would forgive him too, / Shaping from sorrow a new love" — where sorrow itself becomes the material out of which a new love is shaped. The last stanza widens the picture to the whole world and shrinks it at the same time: "Father and son, we both must live / On the same globe and the same land" — a shared planet, and still no shared life. And then self-knowledge arrives, through the son's own voice: "He speaks: I cannot understand / Myself, why anger grows from grief." Neither of them is cruel — their anger is only grief that has nowhere to go, a sorrow that has grown a hard edge because it could never be spoken. The poem closes on the image every reader remembers: "We each put out an empty hand, / Longing for something to forgive." The hand is empty because nothing has been given, offered or taken; the gesture of love begins and never finishes. But the longing to forgive is itself the hope in the poem — both of them want the hurt to end, and where that longing survives, the distance between a father and his son is not permanent. The poem's message is therefore double-edged: the generation gap is real, and love alone does not automatically close it — understanding has to be built, patiently, on purpose; but as long as both hands are still held out, "longing for something to forgive", there is still something to build on.

5. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. Who is the speaker in the poem "Father to Son", and about whom is he speaking? 2 marks

Answer:
The speaker in the poem is a father, and he is speaking about his own son. This is made clear from the very first line, "I do not understand this child", and again in the last stanza: "Father and son, we both must live / On the same globe and the same land". The textbook notes that the poem "is autobiographical in nature and describes the relationship between a father and his son", so the father's voice is the poet's own. Throughout the poem the father describes his own feelings — his failure to understand his boy, his silence, his wish to be forgiven and his wish to forgive.

Q2. Why does the father say "I do not understand this child"? 2 marks

Answer:
The father says this because, although he and his son have "lived together now / In the same house for years", he still knows "Nothing of him". The line is the poem's plainest statement of the generation gap — the distance that can open between a father and his son even under the same roof.

Q3. Does the poem talk of an exclusively personal experience or is it fairly universal? 3 marks

Answer:
The poem is personal in its origin but universal in its appeal. It is personal because a particular father speaks in the first person throughout — "I do not understand this child", "I would forgive him too" — and the textbook tells us that the poem "is autobiographical in nature and describes the relationship between a father and his son". It is also universal, because the experience he describes is not his alone: a generation gap between parents and children, the "Silence" that "surrounds" a family, the feeling of "We speak like strangers", and the wish to be forgiven all belong to families everywhere. The closing image in particular — "We each put out an empty hand, / Longing for something to forgive" — could belong to any father and any son.

Q4. How is the father's helplessness brought out in the poem? 3 marks

Answer:
The father's helplessness is brought out through his own confessions, and it grows from one stanza to the next. He cannot understand his son even after "years" in the same house, and the only way he can think of to "build / Up a relationship" is out of his memory of "how / He was when small" — he has nothing more recent to work with. He cannot even decide what went wrong: "Yet have I killed / The seed I spent or sown it where / The land is his and none of mine?" And he cannot share what his son loves — "what he loves I cannot share". Even his will to forgive is helpless, because there is no quarrel he can put right: it is silence, not argument, that "surrounds" them, and in the end both of them simply stand there, each putting out "an empty hand" — a hand that can offer nothing.

Q5. Explain the line "Shaping from sorrow a new love." 3 marks

Answer:
This line shows the father at his most generous. He has just said, "I would forgive him too" — he is willing to forgive his son without being asked; and then he goes further. The word "Shaping" is a maker's word, a craftsman's word: the father does not want his sorrow simply to sit inside him as bitterness. He wants to take that sorrow as raw material and shape it into a "new love" — a fresh relationship made out of the pain of the broken one. So the line means that his grief will not be wasted; it will be turned into the foundation of a new, better bond between father and son. It is the poem's clearest picture of hope.

Q6. Does the poem have a consistent rhyme scheme? 3 marks

Answer:
The poem keeps the same shape throughout — four stanzas of six lines each — and it also keeps a repeated rhyme pattern in every stanza. In each of the four stanzas the second and fifth lines rhyme ("now" / "how", "mine" / "design", "to" / "too", "land" / "hand"), and the fourth and sixth lines rhyme or half-rhyme ("build" / "killed", "air" / "share", "move" / "love", "grief" / "forgive"). The first and third lines are left unrhymed. So the rhyme is quite regular in its pattern, although the rhymes are quiet and gentle and some of them, like "grief" / "forgive", are only near-rhymes. The effect is a calm, speech-like music rather than a tightly locked scheme.

Q7. Identify the phrases and lines that indicate distance between father and son. 4 marks

Answer:
The poem is built out of such phrases, and they fall into three kinds. First, the phrases of not knowing: "I do not understand this child", "I know / Nothing of him", and "Yet what he loves I cannot share" — the father does not know his son's mind or his tastes. Second, the phrases of estrangement and silence: "We speak like strangers, there's no sign / Of understanding in the air", "Silence surrounds us", and the fact that the father has to "build / Up a relationship from how / He was when small" — that is, from a time now long past. Third, the phrases of separate worlds: the father's question about sowing the seed "where / The land is his and none of mine", his fear of watching the boy "make and move / His world", and his wish that the son would be "prodigal, returning to / His father's house" instead. Together these lines mark the whole distance of the generation gap: same house, same land, different worlds.

Q8. Explain the phrase "anger grows from grief" and the closing image of the empty hand. 4 marks

Answer:
"He speaks: I cannot understand / Myself, why anger grows from grief" is the poem's deepest line. The son too is heard here, and he admits he cannot explain his own feelings. The insight is that the anger between father and son is not hatred and not cruelty — it has grown out of grief. It is the sorrow of a love that has gone quiet and cannot express itself, and because it has no words, it comes out as irritation and sharpness. What sounds like anger between them is really sadness. The closing image completes the thought: "We each put out an empty hand, / Longing for something to forgive." Both of them reach out — the gesture of love is made — but the hand is empty, holding nothing and offering nothing, so the offer never becomes contact. Neither can name the wrong he wants forgiven, and so nothing is forgiven. Yet the hands are still stretched out, and the longing to forgive is alive in both — which is why the poem ends in sadness but not in despair.

Q9. "Father to Son" brings out the pain of the generation gap. Discuss with reference to the poem. 6 marks

Answer:
"Father to Son" is a poem about a distance that should not exist — the distance between a man and his own son — and Elizabeth Jennings brings it out entirely through the father's own voice. The poem opens with the plainest statement of that gap: "I do not understand this child / Though we have lived together now / In the same house for years." The father knows "Nothing of him", and the only material he has for building "Up a relationship" is his memory of "how / He was when small". Closeness of space, the poem shows us, is not closeness of heart.

The gap then becomes a matter of words and of worlds. They "speak like strangers", and there is "no sign / Of understanding in the air"; "Silence surrounds us". The son is "built to my design" and yet "what he loves I cannot share" — the same blood, opposite tastes. The father even wonders, helplessly, "Yet have I killed / The seed I spent or sown it where / The land is his and none of mine?", admitting that his boy's world may be a land in which he has no share at all. His wish is not for obedience but for nearness — he would have the son "prodigal, returning to / His father's house, the home he knew" rather than watch him "make and move / His world".

But the poem's greatest strength is that it refuses to make the father a victim or the son a villain. The last stanza lays the two of them side by side — "Father and son, we both must live / On the same globe and the same land" — sharing a planet and still not sharing a life. The son has a voice too, and it says, "I cannot understand / Myself, why anger grows from grief": the anger in this house is really grief, and neither of them knows how it turned hard. And then the picture that sums up the whole poem: "We each put out an empty hand, / Longing for something to forgive." Both want to forgive; neither can find the words or the wrong to forgive, and so the hands stay empty. The pain of the generation gap, then, is precisely this — not hatred, not rebellion, but two people who love each other standing within arm's reach and unable to speak; and the poem's quiet hope is that "Shaping from sorrow a new love" is still possible while both hands are still held out.

Q10. Value-based question: The father in the poem keeps reaching out even though his son does not understand him. What does the poem teach us about love and communication between parents and children? 6 marks

Answer:
The poem teaches that love alone is not enough without communication. The father's love for his son is never in doubt — he would forgive him without being asked, and he wants to take his own sorrow and go on "Shaping from sorrow a new love". And yet that love cannot travel the short distance between them, because they have never learned to speak to each other: they "speak like strangers", and "Silence surrounds us". The first value lesson is therefore that affection has to be expressed; a feeling that is never put into words stays locked inside the person who feels it, and the other person simply experiences the silence — "there's no sign / Of understanding in the air".

The second lesson is about blame. The father spends the whole poem doubting himself — "Yet have I killed / The seed I spent" — while the son admits he "cannot understand / Myself, why anger grows from grief". Both of them are hurt and neither is wicked; the poem shows how easily a family mistakes grief for anger and then answers anger with more anger. Recognising that the sharp words at home are usually sorrow in disguise is the beginning of understanding.

The third and most valuable lesson is the courage of reaching out and of forgiving. Both father and son end the poem with a hand held out — "We each put out an empty hand, / Longing for something to forgive." The hands are empty, and that is the poem's warning: an unspoken offer achieves nothing, and we should not wait for the other person to move first. But the outstretched hands are also its hope, because the wish to forgive is still alive in both of them. So the poem's value message is simple and practical: in a family, say what you feel, do not let silence become the wall, and when there is hurt on both sides, be the one who stretches out the hand and then puts something in it — understanding, an apology, or simply the words that have been missing.
