Snapshots · Class XI · Chapter 4

Ch 4 — Birth (from The Citadel)

Author: A. J. Cronin · Book: Snapshots (Class 11) · Type: Excerpt from the novel The Citadel — a doctor's night of crisis

1. Chapter Summary

Andrew Manson, newly out of medical school, has just begun his medical practice as an assistant to Dr Edward Page in the small Welsh mining town of Blaenelly. He is returning from a disappointing evening with Christine, the girl he loves. It is nearly midnight when he reaches Bryngower, and he finds Joe Morgan waiting for him, walking up and down with short steps between the closed surgery and the entrance to the house. At the sight of Andrew, the burly driller's face expresses relief. "Eh, Doctor, I'm glad to see you. I been back and forward here this last hour. The missus wants ye — before time, too." Joe and his wife have been married nearly twenty years and are expecting their first child.

Andrew tells Morgan to wait, goes into the house for his bag, and the two men set out for Number 12 Blaina Terrace. The night air is cool and deep with quiet mystery. Usually so perceptive, Andrew now feels dull and listless; he has no premonition that this night call will prove unusual, still less that it would influence his whole future in Blaenelly. The men walk in silence until they reach the door of Number 12, and then Joe draws up short. "I'll not come in," he says, his voice showing signs of strain. "But, man, I know ye'll do well for us."

Inside, a narrow stair leads up to a small bedroom, clean but poorly furnished and lit only by an oil lamp. Here Mrs Morgan's mother, a tall, grey-haired woman of nearly seventy, and the stout, elderly midwife wait beside the patient. The old woman quickly offers him tea, and Andrew sees that she, wise in experience, realises there must be a period of waiting — and is afraid he will leave the case, saying he would return later. "Don't fret, mother, I'll not run away," he says. Down in the kitchen he drinks the tea, knowing he could not sleep even an hour, and that the case would demand all his attention. A queer lethargy of spirit comes upon him, and he decides to remain until everything was over.

An hour later he goes upstairs again, notes the progress made, and comes down to sit by the kitchen fire. It is still, except for the rustle of a cinder in the grate and the slow tick-tock of the wall clock — and the beat of Morgan's footsteps as he paces in the street outside. The old woman opposite him sits in her black dress, quite motionless, her eyes strangely alive and wise, probing, never leaving his face. Andrew's thoughts are heavy and muddled. The episode he had witnessed at Cardiff station still obsesses him morbidly. He thinks of Bramwell, foolishly devoted to a woman who deceived him sordidly; of Edward Page, bound to the shrewish Blodwen; of Denny, living unhappily, apart from his wife. His reason tells him all these marriages are dismal failures, but he wishes to consider marriage as an idyllic state, and cannot otherwise consider it with the image of Christine before him. It is the conflict between his level, doubting mind and his overflowing heart which leaves him resentful and confused.

The old woman breaks into his thoughts: "Susan said not to give her the chloroform if it would harm the baby. She's awful set upon this child, Doctor, bach." She adds in a low tone: "Ay, we all are, I fancy." Andrew answers kindly that the anaesthetic will do no harm and that they will be all right. Then the nurse's voice is heard calling from the top landing. Andrew glances at the clock, which now shows half-past three, and goes up to the bedroom; he perceives that he might now begin his work.

An hour elapses. It is a long, harsh struggle. Then, as the first streaks of dawn stray past the broken edges of the blind, the child is born — lifeless. A shiver of horror passes over Andrew; after all that he had promised, his face, heated with his own exertions, chills suddenly. He hesitates, torn between his desire to attempt to resuscitate the child and his obligation towards the mother, who is herself in a desperate state. The dilemma is so urgent that he does not solve it consciously. Blindly, instinctively, he gives the child to the nurse and turns to Susan Morgan, who now lies collapsed, almost pulseless, and not yet out of the ether, upon her side. His haste is desperate, a frantic race against her ebbing strength. It takes him only an instant to smash a glass ampule and inject the medicine. Then he flings down the hypodermic syringe and works unsparingly to restore the flaccid woman. After a few minutes of feverish effort, her heart strengthens and he sees that he may safely leave her.

He swings round, in his shirt sleeves, his hair sticking to his damp brow: "Where's the child?" The midwife makes a frightened gesture — she had placed it beneath the bed. In a flash Andrew kneels down and, fishing amongst the sodden newspapers below the bed, pulls out the child — a boy, perfectly formed. The limp, warm body is white and soft as tallow; the cord, hastily slashed, lies like a broken stem; the head lolls on the thin neck; the limbs seem boneless. The whiteness means only one thing — asphyxia, pallida — and his mind, unnaturally tense, races back to a case he once had seen in the Samaritan, to the treatment that had been used.

Instantly he is on his feet, demanding hot water, cold water and basins. Snatching a blanket, he lays the child upon it and begins the special method of respiration. The basins arrive, the ewer, the big iron kettle. He splashes cold water into one basin and into the other mixes water as hot as his hand can bear; then, like some crazy juggler, he hurries the child between the two, now plunging it into the icy, now into the steaming bath. Fifteen minutes pass. Sweat runs into his eyes, blinding him; one sleeve hangs down, dripping; his breath comes pantingly — but no breath comes from the lax body of the child. A desperate sense of defeat presses on him, a raging hopelessness. The midwife watches him in stark consternation, and the old woman stands pressed back against the wall, her hand to her throat, uttering no sound, her eyes burning upon him. He remembers her longing for a grandchild, as great as had been her daughter's longing for this child — all dashed away now, futile, beyond remedy.

The floor is a draggled mess. Stumbling over a sopping towel, Andrew almost drops the child, now wet and slippery in his hands, like a strange, white fish. "For mercy's sake, Doctor," whimpers the midwife. "It's stillborn." Andrew does not heed her. Beaten, despairing, having laboured in vain for half an hour, he still persists in one last effort, rubbing the child with a rough towel, crushing and releasing the little chest with both his hands, trying to get breath into that limp body. And then, as by a miracle, the pigmy chest gives a short, convulsive heave, another… and another… Andrew turns giddy; the sense of life springing beneath his fingers after all that unavailing striving is so exquisite it almost makes him faint. The child is gasping now, deeper and deeper. A bubble of mucus comes from one tiny nostril, a joyful iridescent bubble. The limbs are no longer boneless, the head no longer lies back spinelessly, the blanched skin is slowly turning pink. Then, exquisitely, comes the child's cry. "Dear Father in heaven," the nurse sobs hysterically. "It's come — it's come alive."

Andrew hands her the child, feeling weak and dazed. About him the room lies in a shuddering litter: blankets, towels, basins, soiled instruments, the hypodermic syringe impaled by its point in the linoleum, the ewer knocked over, the kettle on its side in a puddle of water. Upon the huddled bed the mother still dreams her way quietly through the anaesthetic. The old woman still stands against the wall — but her hands are together and her lips move without sound. She is praying. Mechanically Andrew wrings out his sleeve and pulls on his jacket. "I'll fetch my bag later, nurse."

He goes downstairs, through the kitchen into the scullery, his lips dry, takes a long drink of water, and reaches for his hat and coat. Outside he finds Joe standing on the pavement with a tense, expectant face. "All right, Joe," he says thickly. "Both all right." It is quite light. Nearly five o'clock. A few miners are already in the streets: the first of the night shift moving out. As Andrew walks with them, spent and slow, his footfalls echoing with the others under the morning sky, he keeps thinking blindly, oblivious to all other work he had done in Blaenelly: "I've done something; oh, God! I've done something real at last."

3. Theme

Devotion to duty and the quiet heroism of ordinary medical work. Andrew Manson is tired and distracted — he comes back from a disappointing evening with Christine, feels dull and listless, and is troubled by a queer lethargy of spirit. Yet when Joe Morgan needs him, he goes without hesitation, sits through the long night, refuses to leave the case, and at the end says simply, "Don't fret, mother, I'll not run away." The chapter's heroism is not loud: it is a young doctor staying awake by a kitchen fire, then doing whatever a desperate moment demands. Andrew himself sums it up in his one line of triumph — "I've done something; oh, God! I've done something real at last."

Moral courage and the willingness to take a professional risk. Twice in the night Andrew has to act under crushing pressure. First, when the child is born lifeless and the mother is collapsed, almost pulseless, he must choose between them; the dilemma is so urgent that he "did not solve it consciously" and does not decide with his head but "blindly, instinctively." Second, after he has saved the mother, he takes up a baby the midwife has given up as stillborn and fights on — fifteen minutes, then half an hour of labour "in vain" — until the child breathes. Both are decisions a cautious man would not take. The chapter shows that saving a life sometimes requires risking one's own judgement and reputation.

The value of a human life, and the worth of the long odds. The Morgan household has waited: Joe and Susan have been married nearly twenty years and this is their first child. The mother tells the doctor not even to give her chloroform if it would harm the baby; the grandmother, nearly seventy, longs for a grandchild as much as her daughter longs for this child. Everyone in the room has staked everything on one baby, and the chapter's turning point is the moment when the pigmy chest heaves, the blanched skin turns pink, and the child cries — a life rescued when it seemed "futile, beyond remedy."

The contrast between the doctor's ideals and the poverty and hard living of the town. Blaenelly is a small Welsh mining town; the birth takes place in a small bedroom, "clean but poorly furnished, and lit only by an oil lamp," and the house is reached by a narrow stair, with the child later found among sodden newspapers under the bed. Around the house, Joe paces the street all night, and at dawn "the first of the night shift" moves out — the town's hard life goes on regardless. Andrew, meanwhile, broods over dismal failures of marriage and clutches at an idyllic ideal with the image of Christine before him. The gap between what he hopes life can be and the rough world he actually works in is the quiet ache under the whole chapter.

The difference between textbook medicine and practising medicine. The book teaches theory; the bedside demands nerve. Andrew's knowledge tells him the whiteness means asphyxia, pallida — but the cure comes from a case he had once seen at the Samaritan, and from his own refusal to stop when the midwife says the child is stillborn. He works not by rule but by instinct, improvisation ("like some crazy juggler") and sheer persistence. Textbook medicine can name the condition; only the practising physician can bring a child back.

4. Character Sketch

Andrew Manson

Andrew is a newly qualified doctor, "newly out of medical school," working as an assistant to Dr Edward Page in the small Welsh mining town of Blaenelly. He is young, sensitive and self-doubting: he returns from a disappointing evening with Christine feeling dull and listless, and as he waits he is tormented by "the conflict between his level, doubting mind and his overflowing heart," and by his wish to believe marriage an idyllic state despite the dismal failures he sees around him — Bramwell deceived, Edward Page bound to the shrewish Blodwen, Denny living apart from his wife. But his work reveals his real character. He stays the whole night rather than leaving the case, promising the old woman, "Don't fret, mother, I'll not run away." In the crisis he acts with instinct and nerve: he gives the lifeless baby to the nurse and saves the dying mother first, then takes up the child the midwife has called stillborn and works on "beaten, despairing, having laboured in vain for half an hour" until it breathes. He ends "weak and dazed," too tired even to care about himself — "Mechanically Andrew wrung out his sleeve, pulled on his jacket." His reward is entirely inward: "I've done something; oh, God! I've done something real at last."

Joe Morgan

Joe is the burly driller, a big, strong working man of Blaenelly, married to Susan for nearly twenty years and about to become a father for the first time. He is anxious and utterly devoted — he waits outside Bryngower, "walking up and down with short steps between the closed surgery and the entrance to the house," for "this last hour" before Andrew arrives, and he paces the street outside the house through the whole night, his footsteps heard inside the kitchen. He is also respectful and trusting to the point of humility: he refuses to come into the house, saying only, "I'll not come in… But, man, I know ye'll do well for us." When Andrew comes out at nearly five o'clock he is still there, "with a tense, expectant face," and the two words "Both all right" are enough. He embodies the simple, patient faith of the ordinary man in the doctor.

Susan Morgan

Susan is Joe Morgan's wife, the patient of the story, and she never speaks in the excerpt — she is known entirely through what others say of her and through what is done to her. She is having her first child after nearly twenty years of marriage, and she is wholly selfless about it: "Susan said not to give her the chloroform if it would harm the baby. She's awful set upon this child, Doctor, bach." Her longing for the child is echoed by her mother's longing for a grandchild. She is also desperately ill: after the birth she lies collapsed, almost pulseless, and not yet out of the ether, and it is a "frantic race against her ebbing strength." At the end "the mother still dreamed her way quietly through the anaesthetic" — asleep, unaware of how close she came to death.

Dr Edward Page

Dr Edward Page is the established doctor of Blaenelly and Andrew's employer; Andrew has "just begun his medical practice as an assistant to Dr Edward Page." He is not present in the night's events at all — Andrew is sent out alone — and the source gives only one detail about him: he is "bound to the shrewish Blodwen." That single line matters, because Andrew uses Page's marriage as part of the evidence that marriages are "dismal failures"; Page thus stands quietly in the background as a warning of the life Andrew fears for himself, and his absence also shows how much responsibility the young assistant carries on his own.

Christine

Christine is "the girl he loves." Andrew has spent a disappointing evening with her, and the disappointment follows him into the night. She exists in the story almost entirely as an image in his mind: he cannot think of marriage as anything but idyllic "with the image of Christine before him," and "Her eyes, shining towards him, admitted no other conclusion." He broods about her so long by the kitchen fire that he starts when the old woman suddenly speaks. Her small presence is what makes his night's work more than a medical case — he is a young man with his own hopes at stake, and yet he gives the whole night to a stranger's child.

Q10. "There lies a great difference between textbook medicine and the world of a practising physician." Discuss with reference to the chapter. 6 marks

Answer:
Textbook medicine gives the doctor a name for the problem; practising medicine demands that he do something about it, at once, with no certainty of success. Andrew shows both sides of this. His training lets him look at the child and know instantly that "the whiteness meant only one thing: asphyxia, pallida." But that name saves nobody. What saves the child is something that came to him not from a book but from a case he "once had seen in the Samaritan" — lived experience, remembered and applied on the spot. Textbook medicine also cannot teach a doctor what to do when two lives hang in the balance: Andrew is torn between reviving the child and attending to the mother, and he admits "the dilemma was so urgent he did not solve it consciously"; he acts "blindly, instinctively."

Practising medicine demands endurance, improvisation and nerve — the qualities a classroom does not test. Andrew improvises like "some crazy juggler," hurrying the baby between an icy bath and a steaming one; he works on when the midwife whimpers "It's stillborn"; he goes on "beaten, despairing, having laboured in vain for half an hour." A textbook would have ended the case where the midwife did. The chapter finally shows that the practising physician's hardest subject is not disease but the human situation around it — the old woman praying against the wall, the husband pacing the street, the mother dreaming through the anaesthetic. Knowledge is only the beginning of the doctor's work.

Q11. "I've done something; oh, God! I've done something real at last." Why does Andrew say this, and what does it mean? 6 marks

Answer:
Andrew says these words to himself as he walks home at nearly five o'clock in the morning, "spent and slow," among "the first of the night shift" moving out under the morning sky. He says them because the night has given him something no earlier work in Blaenelly had given him: the plain, undeniable proof that he has saved lives. He had come to the case tired, "dull and listless," with no premonition that "this night call would prove unusual, still less that it would influence his whole future in Blaenelly," and with his mind full of his own troubles about Christine and about marriage. He had left it having pulled a mother back from being "almost pulseless" and having brought a baby the midwife had called stillborn back to breath and to its first cry.

The words also mean that he measures his life by usefulness, not by comfort or success. He is "oblivious to all other work he had done in Blaenelly," which suggests that the routine duties of an assistant doctor had not felt like real achievement to him; this one night does, because it was desperate, hard-won and wholly his own doing. The "oh, God!" in the line is not only relief but wonder and gratitude — the feeling of a young man who has found his purpose. It ties the whole chapter together: the long vigil, the two terrible choices and the final cry of the child all resolve into one sentence, and the chapter's closing image of the doctor walking "with them" — the miners — makes him one of the working people he has just served.

Q12. Value-based: The midwife gives up but the old woman prays and the young doctor does not stop. What does the chapter teach us about faith, hope and perseverance in a crisis? 6 marks

Answer:
The three figures in that room at dawn stand for three ways of facing hopelessness. The midwife, seeing the pallid body, whimpers, "For mercy's sake, Doctor… It's stillborn," and watches him "in stark consternation" — she has done her duty by experience and accepts defeat. The old woman, Mrs Morgan's mother, does neither: she stays "pressed back against the wall where she had all the time remained," her hand at her throat, "uttering no sound, her eyes burning upon him," and at the end "her hands were together, her lips moved without sound. She was praying." Her hope is silent and unshakeable, and Andrew feels its weight — he remembers "her longing for a grandchild, as great as had been her daughter's longing for this child," and how it seemed "all dashed away now; futile, beyond remedy."

Andrew's answer to despair is neither silence nor surrender but work. Though "beaten, despairing, having laboured in vain for half an hour," he "still persisted in one last effort." The chapter's value lesson is direct: in a crisis, giving up is a decision, and so is continuing — and only continuing can produce the moment when "the pigmy chest… gave a short, convulsive heave." Perseverance here is not blind optimism; it is skill, memory of an old case, and refusal to accept a verdict. Paired with the old woman's faith, it shows that hope kept quietly and effort made persistently together can bring a life back "from the brink." It also reminds us that the courage to continue must come from within the doctor himself: no one in that room could help him, and nobody would have blamed him for stopping.
