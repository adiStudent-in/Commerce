Poem 3 — The Voice of the Rain

Poet: Walt Whitman · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: Free-verse poem in which the rain speaks about the cycle of life

1. Stanza-wise Explanation

The poem is in two parts: first the poet asks the rain a question, and then the rain answers in its own voice. The textbook notes on this poem call it "a nature poem celebrating the coming of the rain", and its own exercise asks about "two voices in the poem".

Stanza 1

"And who art thou? said I to the soft-falling shower,\
Which, strange to tell, gave me an answer, as here translated:"

Explanation: This is the question. The poet — the first voice in the poem — speaks directly to the falling rain, which he calls the "soft-falling shower" because it comes down gently. He asks it, in the old-fashioned wording of the poem, "And who art thou?" — that is, "who are you?" The very next line tells us the surprising thing: the shower did answer him. The poet calls this "strange to tell", because rain is a thing of nature and not a person, and yet it gave an answer. Those words also mean the poet himself finds it astonishing to relate. Finally he says the answer is given "as here translated" — meaning he is putting the rain's own speech into human words for us to read. So the whole stanza is the poet setting up his question and promising us the rain's reply.

One-liner: The poet asks the softly falling rain who it is, and the rain gives him an answer, which he now repeats for us in human language.

Stanza 2

"I am the Poem of Earth, said the voice of the rain,\
Eternal I rise impalpable out of the land and the bottomless sea,\
Upward to heaven, whence, vaguely form’d, altogether changed, and yet the same,\
I descend to lave the droughts, atomies, dust-layers of the globe,\
And all that in them without me were seeds only, latent, unborn;\
And forever, by day and night, I give back life to my own origin,\
And make pure and beautify it;\
(For song, issuing from its birth-place, after fulfilment, wandering\
Reck’d or unreck’d, duly with love returns.)"

Explanation: This is the answer, spoken in the rain's own voice — the second voice of the poem. The rain first tells us who it is: "I am the Poem of Earth". The comparison is perfect, because just as a poem comes out of the earth and gives beauty and life back to it, so does the rain.

Then the rain describes its endless journey — the water cycle:

  • "Eternal I rise impalpable out of the land and the bottomless sea" — the rain rises forever, in a form that cannot be touched (impalpable), out of two sources: the land and the bottomless sea. "Eternal" tells us this has gone on and will go on without end.
  • "Upward to heaven, whence, vaguely form’d, altogether changed, and yet the same" — it goes up to the sky, and there it is given a vague (unclear, shapeless) form. It is completely changed — water has become vapour and cloud — and yet it is the same, the same water with the same nature. This one phrase holds the whole mystery of the cycle: the form changes, the identity does not.
  • "I descend to lave the droughts, atomies, dust-layers of the globe" — after rising, it comes back down and washes (lave = wash, bathe) the dry, drought-hit earth, its tiny particles (atomies) and its layers of dust. This is the rain's service to the world.
  • "And all that in them without me were seeds only, latent, unborn" — and without the rain, everything in those drought-stricken, dusty places would have remained only seeds — hidden (latent) and unborn. Rain is what turns a hidden seed into a living plant. This is the clearest statement of the rain as a life-giving force.
  • "And forever, by day and night, I give back life to my own origin, / And make pure and beautify it;" — and forever, day and night, without rest, the rain gives back life to its own origin — to the land and the sea it rose from — and it makes that origin pure and beautiful. So the rain does not merely take from the earth; it returns what it took, and returns it better.

The last two lines, printed in brackets, are the rain's own closing comparison:

  • "(For song, issuing from its birth-place, after fulfilment, wandering / Reck’d or unreck’d, duly with love returns.)" — the rain says it is like a song. A song issues from its birth-place (the heart or mind it came from), is sung, after fulfilment (after it has done its work and been fully expressed), goes wandering through the world, and then — whether it is "Reck’d" (noticed, heeded, valued) or "unreck’d" (ignored, unvalued) — it duly returns with love to the place it was born. In exactly the same way, the rain leaves its birthplace (land and sea), does its work, wanders as cloud, and returns to its origin with love. This is the poem's great simile, and the brackets show that these lines are a separate, reflective aside added to complete the comparison between rain and song / poetry.

One-liner: The rain calls itself the "Poem of Earth", describes its eternal cycle of rising from land and sea, changing form in the sky, descending to wash the dry earth and give life to hidden seeds, and returning with love to its own origin — just as a song returns to its birthplace.

3. Theme

Walt Whitman's "The Voice of the Rain" is a nature poem celebrating the coming of the rain, but it is also a quiet statement about how the cycle of nature and the life of a poem are one and the same thing. The rain speaks in its own voice and calls itself the Poem of Earth, because like a poem it is born out of the earth, gives something back to it, and returns to it. It is eternal and impalpable — forever rising, untouchable, out of the land and the bottomless sea — and it accepts a vague form in the sky, altogether changed, and yet the same: the shape changes, the essence does not. Then it comes down to lave the droughts, the atomies and the dust-layers of the globe, and without it everything in the earth would be seeds only, latent, unborn. This is the rain as a life-giving, selfless and eternal force — it works by day and night, never resting, and it does not merely take from its source: it gives back life to its own origin and makes it pure and beautiful. The closing simile ties everything together: a song, issuing from its birth-place, after fulfilment, wandering, and "Reck’d or unreck’d", duly with love returns — the rain and the song behave alike, both leaving their origin, wandering, giving life and joy, and coming home with love whether or not anyone heeds them. The message is therefore twofold: nature's cycle is a story of giving without expecting reward, and poetry is itself a kind of rain — it rises from life, and returns to life to purify and beautify it.

5. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. There are two voices in the poem. Who do they belong to? Which lines indicate this? 2 marks

Answer:
The first voice belongs to the poet, who asks the rain a question, and the second voice belongs to the rain itself, which answers him. The poet's voice is heard in the line "And who art thou? said I to the soft-falling shower", and from the next line we learn that the shower answered "as here translated". The rain's own voice begins with "I am the Poem of Earth, said the voice of the rain" and continues to the end of the poem, where the rain speaks of itself throughout as "I" — "Eternal I rise", "I descend", "I give back life".

Q2. What does the phrase "strange to tell" mean? 2 marks

Answer:
"Strange to tell" means that something is surprising or astonishing to relate. The poet uses the phrase because the gently falling shower — which is only a thing of nature and not a person — actually answered his question "And who art thou?". It is therefore strange and wonderful to the poet that the rain should have a voice and speak to him, and he draws our attention to this wonder before repeating the rain's reply.

Q3. Why does the rain call itself the "Poem of Earth"? 3 marks

Answer:
The rain calls itself the "Poem of Earth" because its whole life is like that of a poem. A poem is born out of the earth, travels out into the world and finally comes back to the life it came from — and this is exactly the rain's story. The rain tells us it rises "eternal" and "impalpable" out of the land and the bottomless sea, goes up to heaven where it is "vaguely form’d, altogether changed, and yet the same", and then returns. In the same way, it does what a poem does for human beings: it gives life and beauty. It descends to "lave the droughts, atomies, dust-layers of the globe", it makes the hidden seeds grow, and it "gives back life to my own origin, and makes pure and beautify it". The rain is therefore a poem written by the earth itself — a thing of beauty and life that keeps returning to its maker.

Q4. Explain the phrase "altogether changed, and yet the same". 3 marks

Answer:
The phrase describes the water when it has risen "upward to heaven". There the water is given a "vague" form and is "altogether changed" — it is no longer the water of the sea or the land, but has turned into vapour and then into cloud, with no fixed or visible shape of its own. Yet it is "and yet the same", because in spite of this complete change of form, it is still the same water, with the same nature and the same power to give life. The phrase thus captures the very heart of the water cycle — the form changes, the identity does not. It also carries the poem's larger idea that a thing can wander far from its origin, change entirely on the outside, and still remain true to what it was.

The rain calls itself a poem of the earth, and in the bracketed closing lines it openly compares itself to a song. The similarity is complete at every stage: a song issues from its birth-place, just as the rain rises out of the land and the bottomless sea; a song comes to fulfilment and then goes wandering in the world, just as the rain rises to heaven, takes a vague form and moves over the earth; and finally a song returns with love to the place it was born, just as the rain gives back life to its own origin, making it pure and beautiful. Both give life and joy to others, and both return home with love whether they are heeded or not.

Q6. Why are the last two lines put within brackets? 3 marks

Answer:
The last two lines — "(For song, issuing from its birth-place, after fulfilment, wandering / Reck’d or unreck’d, duly with love returns.)" — are put within brackets because they are not part of the rain's account of its own journey, but a separate, reflective aside added at the end. The rest of the rain's speech tells us what the rain does; these two lines tell us what the rain is like, by comparing it to a song. The brackets mark them off as the rain's own concluding thought or afterthought, a quiet comment made aside from the main narration. They also round off the poem, for they complete the comparison between the rain and a song/poem that the rain began when it called itself the "Poem of Earth".

Q7. List the pairs of opposites found in the poem. 3 marks

Answer:
The poem is built on several pairs of opposites, which bring out the movement and balance of nature. The most important are:

1. "Eternal I rise" and "I descend" — rising and coming down, the two halves of the water cycle.
2. "by day and night" — day and night, showing that the rain's work never stops.
3. "altogether changed, and yet the same" — changed and same, the form altered but the essence unchanged.
4. "Reck’d or unreck’d" — noticed and unnoticed (reckoned and unreckoned), the song returning with love whether it is valued or ignored.

In addition, the rain's movement is between the heaven above and the land and the bottomless sea below.

Q8. How is the cyclic movement of rain brought out in the poem? 4 marks

Answer:
The cyclic movement of the rain is brought out step by step, in the rain's own words, and it follows exactly what we learn about the water cycle in science.

First comes the rising: the rain says, "Eternal I rise impalpable out of the land and the bottomless sea". The water of the land and the ocean rises endlessly in an invisible, untouchable form. Next comes the change in the sky: the rain goes "Upward to heaven, whence, vaguely form’d, altogether changed, and yet the same" — it becomes vapour and cloud, taking a vague form, completely changed in appearance but still the same water. Then comes the descent: "I descend to lave the droughts, atomies, dust-layers of the globe" — the water comes back to the earth as rain and washes the dry, dusty surface. And finally comes the return and renewal: "And forever, by day and night, I give back life to my own origin, / And make pure and beautify it". The rain returns life to the very land and sea it rose from, purifies them and beautifies them. Because it does this "forever, by day and night", the cycle never ends — it begins again and again.

Q9. The rain gives itself away endlessly and expects nothing in return. What lesson does the rain's behaviour hold for human beings?
[4 marks] (value-based)

Answer:
The rain is the poem's model of selfless service. It rises "eternal" and "impalpable" and comes down again and again to "lave the droughts, atomies, dust-layers of the globe" — work that nobody asks for and nobody rewards. It works "forever, by day and night", so its giving is not occasional or showy but constant. It also gives more than it takes: it does not simply draw from the land and the sea, it "gives back life to my own origin, and makes pure and beautify it" — it returns to its source and leaves it better than it found it. Most beautifully, it does all this whether it is noticed or not, for the song "Reck’d or unreck’d, duly with love returns".

The lesson for human beings is clear. We should work and give without waiting for praise, honour or reward, and we should give back to the people and places we come from — our family, our community, our country — just as the rain beautifies its own origin. If we all repaid our sources with love and service the way the rain does, the world would be both richer and purer. The rain, in short, teaches us to be diligent, generous and unselfish, and to do good even when no one is watching.

Q10. The poem "The Voice of the Rain" is both a nature poem celebrating the coming of the rain and a poem about poetry itself. Discuss. 6 marks

Answer:
"The Voice of the Rain" works on two levels at once, and the wonder of the poem is that the two levels are really the same story. The textbook's own note calls it "a nature poem celebrating the coming of the rain", and it is certainly that. The rain is given a voice and it answers the poet's question "And who art thou?" by describing its own life — the water cycle — in beautiful, exact detail. The rain rises "Eternal" and "impalpable" out of the "land and the bottomless sea"; it goes "Upward to heaven, whence, vaguely form’d, altogether changed, and yet the same"; it descends "to lave the droughts, atomies, dust-layers of the globe"; and without it, everything in the earth "were seeds only, latent, unborn". In these lines nature itself speaks, and the poem becomes a song of praise for the rain as a life-giving, purifying and ceaselessly generous force of nature.

At the same time, the poem is about poetry. The rain names itself the "Poem of Earth", and the closing bracketed lines openly compare it to a "song, issuing from its birth-place, after fulfilment, wandering / Reck’d or unreck’d, duly with love returns". A poem, like the rain, is born out of the earth, leaves its birthplace, wanders among people, gives them life and beauty, and finally returns to its source with love — and it does so whether or not it is valued, "Reck’d or unreck’d". This is also a picture of the poet's own art: the poet rises from the life around him, transforms it into something new and yet keeps it true, gives it to the world, and finds his way back to it in love.

The two levels meet in the poem's central idea — giving and returning. The rain gives life to the earth and returns to its origin; the song gives joy to the world and returns to its birthplace; the poet gives words to experience and returns to life for his meaning. So the poem celebrates nature, celebrates poetry, and quietly shows that the finest thing either of them does is to give without counting the cost, and then come home with love.
