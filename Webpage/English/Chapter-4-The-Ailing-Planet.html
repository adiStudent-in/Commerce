Hornbill · Class XI · Chapter 4

Ch 4 — The Ailing Planet: the Green Movement's Role

Author: Nani Palkhivala · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: Argumentative/persuasive article (first published in The Indian Express, 24 November 1994)

1. Chapter Summary

Nani Palkhivala opens with the Green Movement, the movement that has "gripped the imagination of the entire human race" more completely and more rapidly than any other movement in world history. It started nearly twenty-five years ago, and in 1972 the world's first nationwide Green party was founded in New Zealand. Since then, the movement has not looked back.

The writer then points to a change in how human beings see the world. We have shifted — one hopes, irrevocably — from the mechanistic view to a holistic and ecological view. He calls this shift in human perceptions as revolutionary as the one introduced by Copernicus, who taught mankind in the sixteenth century that the earth and the other planets revolved round the sun. For the first time in human history there is a growing worldwide consciousness that the earth itself is a living organism — an enormous being of which we are parts — with its own metabolic needs and vital processes that need to be respected and preserved. The earth's vital signs reveal a patient in declining health, and we have begun to realise our ethical obligations to be good stewards of the planet and responsible trustees of the legacy to future generations.

The concept of sustainable development was popularised in 1987 by the World Commission on Environment and Development, which defined it as "Development that meets the needs of the present, without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their needs" — that is, without stripping the natural world of resources future generations would need. To show how wrong our old attitude was, Palkhivala describes the zoo at Lusaka, Zambia, where a cage carries the notice 'The world's most dangerous animal', and inside there is no animal but a mirror. The most dangerous animal is man himself — and he has now realised the wisdom of shifting from a system based on domination to one based on partnership.

Next come the numbers behind the planet's bad health. Scientists have catalogued about 1.4 million living species with which mankind shares the earth, while biologists reckon that about three to a hundred million other living species still languish unnamed in ignominious darkness. One of the early international commissions which dealt, inter alia, with ecology and environment was the Brandt Commission, which had a distinguished Indian, Mr L.K. Jha, as one of its members. The First Brandt Report asked: "Are we to leave our successors a scorched planet of advancing deserts, impoverished landscapes and ailing environment?"

Mr Lester R. Brown, in his book The Global Economic Prospect, points out that the earth's principal biological systems are four — fisheries, forests, grasslands, and croplands — and they form the foundation of the global economic system. Besides supplying our food, these four systems provide virtually all the raw materials for industry except minerals and petroleum-derived synthetics. In large areas of the world human claims on these systems are reaching an unsustainable level, a point where their productivity is being impaired. When this happens, fisheries collapse, forests disappear, grasslands are converted into barren wastelands, and croplands deteriorate. In a protein-conscious and protein-hungry world, over-fishing is common every day. In poor countries, local forests are being decimated to procure firewood for cooking, and in some places firewood has become so expensive that "what goes under the pot now costs more than what goes inside it". Since the tropical forest is, in the words of Dr Myers, "the powerhouse of evolution", several species of life face extinction as a result of its destruction.

Palkhivala then turns to forests and laws. "Forests precede mankind; deserts follow." The world's ancient patrimony of tropical forests is eroding at the rate of forty to fifty million acres a year, and the growing use of dung for burning deprives the soil of an important natural fertiliser. The World Bank estimates that a five-fold increase in the rate of forest planting is needed to cope with the expected fuelwood demand in the year 2000. James Speth, President of the World Resources Institute, said, "We were saying that we are losing the forests at an acre a second, but it is much closer to an acre-and-a-half to a second". Article 48A of the Constitution of India provides that "the State shall endeavour to protect and improve the environment and to safeguard the forests and wildlife of the country" — but what causes endless anguish is that laws are never respected nor enforced in India. (The Constitution says casteism, untouchability and bonded labour shall be abolished, yet they flourish shamelessly even after forty-four years of its operation.) A recent report of Parliament's Estimates Committee has highlighted the near catastrophic depletion of India's forests over the last four decades; India is losing its forests at the rate of 3.7 million acres a year, large areas officially designated as forest land "are already virtually treeless", and the actual loss is estimated to be about eight times the rate shown by government statistics. A three-year study using satellites and aerial photography by the United Nations warns that the environment has deteriorated so badly that it is 'critical' in many of the eighty-eight countries investigated.

Population comes next. The growth of world population is one of the strongest factors distorting the future of human society. It took mankind more than a million years to reach the first billion, which was the world population around the year 1800. By 1900 a second billion was added, and the twentieth century added another 3.7 billion. The present world population is estimated at 5.7 billion, and every four days the world population increases by one million. Fertility falls as incomes rise, education spreads and health improves, so development is the best contraceptive — but development itself may not be possible if the present increase in numbers continues. The rich get richer, and the poor beget children which condemns them to remain poor; more children does not mean more workers, merely more people without work. The author does not suggest that human beings be treated like cattle and compulsorily sterilised; there is no alternative to voluntary family planning without introducing an element of coercion. The real choice is between control of population and perpetuation of poverty. India's population is estimated at 920 million — more than the entire populations of Africa and South America put together — and the hope of the people would die in their hungry hutments unless population control is given topmost priority.

The article closes on hope and responsibility. For the first time in human history we see a transcending concern — the survival not just of the people but of the planet. The environmental problem does not necessarily signal our demise; it is our passport for the future. The emerging new world vision has ushered in the Era of Responsibility — a holistic view, an ecological view, seeing the world as an integrated whole rather than a dissociated collection of parts. Industry has a most crucial role in this era: what a transformation would be effected if more businessmen shared the view of Mr Edgar S. Woolard, Chairman of Du Pont, who five years ago declared himself the Company's "Chief Environmental Officer" and said, "Our continued existence as a leading manufacturer requires that we excel in environmental performance." Finally come two memorable borrowings — Margaret Thatcher's "No generation has a freehold on this earth. All we have is a life tenancy — with a full repairing lease", and Lester Brown's "We have not inherited this earth from our forefathers; we have borrowed it from our children."

3. Theme

The earth is a living organism, not a machine. The chapter's central move is the shift from the mechanistic view, which treated the world as a collection of separate parts to be used, to the holistic and ecological view, which sees it as an integrated whole. The earth is "an enormous being of which we are parts", with its own metabolic needs and vital processes, and "the earth's vital signs reveal a patient in declining health". That image of the planet as a patient is what gives the chapter its title.

The Green Movement and humanity's new awareness. The Green Movement is described as the first movement in world history to have gripped the imagination of the entire human race so completely and so rapidly, with the world's first nationwide Green party founded in New Zealand in 1972. Its achievement is a change in perception — man, the "world's most dangerous animal" of the Lusaka zoo cage, has realised the wisdom of shifting from a system based on domination to one based on partnership.

Sustainable development and the Era of Responsibility. Sustainable development, popularised in 1987 by the World Commission on Environment and Development, insists that we meet present needs "without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their needs". This idea turns into a moral demand: we are stewards of the planet and trustees of a legacy. Both Margaret Thatcher ("a life tenancy — with a full repairing lease") and Lester Brown ("we have borrowed it from our children") make the same point — the earth is not ours to use up.

Our biological systems are being destroyed. The four principal biological systems — fisheries, forests, grasslands and croplands — form the foundation of the global economic system, and human claims on them are reaching an unsustainable level. When that happens, "fisheries collapse, forests disappear, grasslands are converted into barren wastelands, and croplands deteriorate". Forests carry the sharpest warning: "forests precede mankind; deserts follow".

Population growth and the failure of law. The growth of world population is named as "one of the strongest factors distorting the future of human society", and the chapter links it directly to poverty — "the choice is really between control of population and perpetuation of poverty". Alongside this runs a warning about enforcement: Article 48A of the Constitution asks the State to protect the environment, "but what causes endless anguish is the fact that laws are never respected nor enforced in India". Good laws on paper are worthless without the will to enforce them.

4. Character Sketch

Note: This chapter is an expository/persuasive article, not a story — it has no characters in the fictional sense. What it has are voices: the writer himself, and the authorities, reports and public figures he quotes as evidence. These are listed below, because an exam answer often has to name who said what.

Nani Palkhivala — the writer

Palkhivala is the arguing voice of the chapter. He is concerned, well-informed and openly moral: he calls the earth a patient in declining health and insists that we have ethical obligations to be good stewards of the planet. He argues by piling up evidence — international commissions, the United Nations, the World Bank, the Constitution of India and named experts — rather than by telling a story. He can be sharply critical of his own country ("laws are never respected nor enforced in India"), yet he ends not in despair but in hope: the environmental problem "is our passport for the future", and he holds out the Era of Responsibility as a chance to act. His stance is that of a citizen who believes human beings, not nature, are the most dangerous animal — and that they can still choose partnership over domination.

The Brandt Commission and Mr L.K. Jha

The Brandt Commission was one of the early international commissions which dealt, inter alia, with the question of ecology and environment. It had a distinguished Indian, Mr L.K. Jha, as one of its members. Its First Brandt Report supplies the chapter's most haunting question: "Are we to leave our successors a scorched planet of advancing deserts, impoverished landscapes and ailing environment?" It represents the voice of international concern warning about the future we are handing on.

Mr Lester R. Brown

Lester R. Brown, in his book The Global Economic Prospect, is the source of the chapter's central economic argument: the earth's principal biological systems are four — fisheries, forests, grasslands, and croplands — and they form the foundation of the global economic system, providing food and virtually all raw materials for industry except minerals and petroleum-derived synthetics. He is quoted a second time at the end for the line that sums up the whole chapter: "We have not inherited this earth from our forefathers; we have borrowed it from our children." He is the voice of the ecologist-economist, showing that the environment and the economy are the same question.

Dr Myers and James Speth — the scientists and observers

Dr Myers supplies a phrase rather than an argument: the tropical forest is "the powerhouse of evolution", which is why "several species of life face extinction as a result of its destruction". James Speth, President of the World Resources Institute, is the voice of hard, on-the-ground measurement — correcting even the bad news upward: "We were saying that we are losing the forests at an acre a second, but it is much closer to an acre-and-a-half to a second." Together they show that this is not opinion but reported fact.

Mr Edgar S. Woolard — the voice of industry

Woolard, Chairman of Du Pont, is the business leader Palkhivala puts forward as a model for the Era of Responsibility. Five years before the article, he declared himself the Company's "Chief Environmental Officer" and said, "Our continued existence as a leading manufacturer requires that we excel in environmental performance." He shows that industry has "a most crucial role" to play and that profit and environmental care need not be enemies.

Margaret Thatcher

Palkhivala cites the former British Prime Minister not for policy but for a phrase that has "passed decisively into the current coin of English usage": "No generation has a freehold on this earth. All we have is a life tenancy — with a full repairing lease." She is the voice of plain moral statement — we are only temporary holders of the earth, and we must leave it in repair.

Q12. Value-based / higher-order: "We have not inherited this earth from our forefathers; we have borrowed it from our children." What values does this statement demand of us, and how does the chapter support them elsewhere? 6 marks

Answer:
The words are Lester Brown's, quoted at the end of the chapter, and they reverse our usual way of thinking. If the earth is not an inheritance but a loan, then we are not owners but borrowers — and a borrower must return what he has taken in good condition. This demands three values. First, responsibility towards future generations: the idea of sustainable development, popularised in 1987 by the World Commission on Environment and Development, states exactly this — "Development that meets the needs of the present, without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their needs". Palkhivala's own words for the same value are that we are "good stewards of the planet and responsible trustees of the legacy to future generations". Second, restraint and partnership rather than domination: man, described in the cage at Lusaka, Zambia, as "the world's most dangerous animal", has realised the wisdom of shifting from a system based on domination to one based on partnership. Third, honesty and the will to enforce what we promise: the chapter notes that Article 48A of the Constitution asks the State to protect and improve the environment, yet laws "are never respected nor enforced in India" — a reminder that declared values mean nothing without action. The statement is supported elsewhere in the chapter by Margaret Thatcher's "No generation has a freehold on this earth. All we have is a life tenancy — with a full repairing lease", which says the same thing in legal language, and by the chapter's closing vision of the Era of Responsibility, where the environmental problem is not our demise but our "passport for the future". Accepting the loan means, in practical terms, doing what the author urges — protecting forests, controlling population, respecting the four biological systems, and making industry answer for its environmental performance, as Edgar S. Woolard of Du Pont did. The value the chapter finally teaches is gratitude turned into care: we hold the earth briefly, in trust.
