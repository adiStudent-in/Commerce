Hornbill · Class XI · Poem 1

Poem 1 — A Photograph

Poet: Shirley Toulson · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: Reflective poem on time, memory and loss

1. Stanza-wise Explanation

Stanza 1

"The cardboard shows me how it was
When the two girl cousins went paddling,
Each one holding one of my mother’s hands,
And she the big girl — some twelve years or so.
All three stood still to smile through their hair
At the uncle with the camera. A sweet face,
My mother’s, that was before I was born.
And the sea, which appears to have changed less,
Washed their terribly transient feet."

Explanation: The poet looks at an old photograph mounted on cardboard and it takes her back to a day at the beach. In it her mother is only a girl of about twelve years or so — "the big girl" — and on either side of her are the two girl cousins, Betty and Dolly, each holding one of her hands. The three of them had gone paddling in the water and then stood still, smiling through their hair at the uncle with the camera. Looking at that sweet face of her mother, the poet remembers that this was a time before she was born — her mother had a whole life and childhood of her own that the poet never saw. The last two lines hold the deep image of the poem: the sea looks as if it has changed less over the years, while it washed their terribly transient feet — the happy moment is frozen forever in the picture, but the people in it were only passing, brief visitors on the shore. The sea stays; the human beings do not.

One-liner: An old cardboard photograph brings back a beach holiday from before the poet was born, where the unchanging sea washed the "terribly transient feet" of three laughing girls.

Stanza 2

"Some twenty — thirty — years later
She’d laugh at the snapshot. “See Betty
And Dolly,” she’d say, “and look how they
Dressed us for the beach.” The sea holiday
Was her past, mine is her laughter. Both wry
With the laboured ease of loss."

Explanation: Time moves ahead. Some twenty — thirty — years later the poet's mother would look at that snapshot and laugh. She would point out the two girls — "See Betty / And Dolly" — and joke about how they were dressed for the beach, laughing at the old-fashioned clothes of that young girl who was once her. The poem then draws a fine line between the two women: the sea holiday was her past (something the mother had actually lived), while for the poet her mother's laughter is her past, because that is the memory the poet now holds. Both are wry — a smile that is a little twisted with sadness — and both carry the "laboured ease of loss": the ache of what has been lost, carried so long and so often that it now sits on them easily. The mother has lost her girlhood; the poet has lost the mother's young laughter.

One-liner: Years later the mother laughed at the same photograph, and both her lost girlhood and the poet's lost memory of her laughter are "wry with the laboured ease of loss".

Stanza 3

"Now she’s been dead nearly as many years
As that girl lived. And of this circumstance
There is nothing to say at all.
Its silence silences."

Explanation: The poem's last stanza brings the loss home. The poet's mother has now been dead for nearly as many years as that girl lived — that is, the little girl in the photograph lived only about twelve years, and the mother has already been gone for roughly that long. Faced with this fact — "this circumstance" — the poet finds there is nothing to say at all. There is no explanation, no consolation, no speech that can fill the emptiness of the mother's death. The poem closes on the famous line "Its silence silences." — the silence of the photograph, and of the loss, is so complete that it silences the poet herself. The very stillness of the picture, which once held happiness, now holds only an absence.

One-liner: With the mother dead almost as long as the girl in the photo lived, the poet has nothing left to say — "Its silence silences."

3. Theme

A Photograph is a quiet poem about time, memory and loss, and it is built on the simple fact that a cardboard photograph can hold a moment forever while the people inside it cannot. In the first stanza the young mother and her two cousins, Betty and Dolly, stand paddling at the beach, smiling through their hair at the uncle with the camera; the picture freezes them exactly as they were, and against that stillness the poet sets the sea, which "appears to have changed less" and washed their "terribly transient feet" — the puny, passing human lives that nature outlasts. In the second stanza the same photograph returns some twenty — thirty — years later, and the mother laughs at the snapshot and at how they were dressed. But the poet sees what lies under the joke: the sea holiday was the mother's past and the mother's laughter is the poet's past, and the laughter itself is wry — carrying the "laboured ease of loss", the ache of vanished girlhood and vanished time made bearable only because it has been carried so long. The third stanza completes the loss: the mother has now been dead nearly as many years as that girl lived, and of "this circumstance" there is "nothing to say at all" — "Its silence silences." The poem's message is that photographs keep what life surrenders, that memory gives us a loved one twice and takes her away twice, and that the deepest grief often has no words at all — it simply falls silent.

5. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. What does the word 'cardboard' denote in the poem? Why has this word been used? 2 marks

Answer:
"Cardboard" denotes the stiff board on which the old photograph of the poet's mother is mounted. The poet has used this word deliberately to show that the picture is now an old, flat, brittle object — a thing of paper and paste, not of living flesh. It is the lifeless cardboard that "shows" her how it was, and the contrast between the dry board and the warm, laughing girls it holds gives the poem its quiet sadness.

Q3. What has not changed over the years? Does this suggest something to you? 3 marks

Answer:
The sea has not changed over the years — it "appears to have changed less" than the people who played in it. The same waves still wash the same shore, while the girls in the photograph have grown up, aged and, in the mother's case, died. This suggests a clear truth about life: nature is permanent and endless, but human beings are terribly transient, here only for a short while. The photograph preserves the girls exactly as they were, but the sea is the only thing in the picture that is still truly alive and unchanged.

Q4. The poet's mother laughed at the snapshot. What did this laugh indicate? 3 marks

Answer:
When the mother laughed at the snapshot she was laughing at the young girl she once was — at the way she and her cousins Betty and Dolly had been dressed for the beach, and at how they had posed. The laugh indicates both amusement and affection: she enjoyed looking back at that happy, carefree beach holiday of her girlhood. But the poet calls it wry, so the laugh also hides a pang — the girl in the picture is gone forever, and the mother is looking at her own lost youth. What was once a lived holiday has become only a snapshot to laugh at.

Q5. What is the meaning of the line "Both wry with the laboured ease of loss."? 3 marks

Answer:
In this line the poet means that both she and her mother wear the same expression of half-painful, half-amused acceptance. "Wry" describes a smile twisted slightly with sorrow. The sea holiday was the mother's loss — her girlhood and youth are gone — and the mother's laughter is the poet's loss, because the mother herself is now dead. "The laboured ease of loss" means that carrying this loss was once hard labour, but after so many years it has become almost easy to bear. The grief is still there, only it now sits lightly.

Q7. The three stanzas depict three different phases. What are they? 4 marks

Answer:
The three stanzas move through three phases of the poet's understanding of loss. The first phase is the photograph itself: three girls, the poet's young mother among them, paddling at the beach and smiling through their hair at the uncle with the camera — a moment of happy, living girlhood, with the unchanging sea washing their "terribly transient feet". The second phase comes some twenty — thirty — years later, when the mother laughs at the same snapshot and jokes about Betty and Dolly and how they were dressed; here the sea holiday is her past and the laughter is the poet's, and both are wry with the laboured ease of loss. The third phase is the present: the mother has been dead nearly as many years as that girl lived, and of this there is nothing to say at all — "Its silence silences." So the poem moves from a living moment, to a memory looked back on with mixed laughter and pain, to a loss that is total and wordless.

Q8. How does the poet use the sea as a symbol in the poem? 4 marks

Answer:
The sea in the poem stands for permanence and continuity, in contrast to human life, which is brief. In the first stanza the poet notes that the sea "appears to have changed less" and yet it washed their terribly transient feet — the sea is still there, doing the same work, while the feet it touched belong to people who have grown, changed and died. This makes the sea a silent witness to time: it sees generation after generation pass, and it stays. The word transient carries the sting — the girls' joy was real but short. The sea, then, is the poem's measure of how small and short a human life is when set beside nature.

Q9. "A Photograph" is about time, memory and loss. Discuss with reference to the poem. 6 marks

Answer:
"A Photograph" is built on the simplest of objects — a photograph mounted on cardboard — and from it Shirley Toulson draws out her thoughts on time, memory and loss. The photograph freezes a moment that can never return: the poet's mother as a girl of "some twelve years or so", paddling in the sea with cousins Betty and Dolly, each holding one of her hands, all three standing still to smile through their hair at the uncle with the camera. Time is at work here from the very beginning, because the poet reminds us that this was "before I was born" — the picture shows a life that existed without her.

Memory then gives the moment a second life. Some twenty — thirty — years later the mother laughs at the snapshot, pointing out Betty and Dolly and joking about their beach clothes. But the poet sees what lies under the joke: the sea holiday was the mother's past and the mother's laughter is her past, so memory hands each of them a happiness that is already gone. The laughter is wry — "Both wry / With the laboured ease of loss" — amused on the surface, aching underneath, a loss carried so long that it has become almost easy.

Finally there is loss in its full, silent form. The mother has now been "dead nearly as many years / As that girl lived", and of "this circumstance" there is "nothing to say at all". The poem ends, not with a cry or a consolation, but with "Its silence silences" — the photograph's stillness now mirrors the stillness of death, and the poet's grief goes beyond words. Throughout, the sea "which appears to have changed less" has washed their "terribly transient feet", reminding us that nature stays while people do not. So the poem says that a photograph can keep a moment forever, but it cannot keep the people in it; time takes them, memory gives them back changed, and loss ends in silence.

Q10. Value-based question: The poet says that of her mother's death "there is nothing to say at all." What does the poem teach us about dealing with the loss of someone we love? 6 marks

Answer:
The poem teaches that some losses are too deep for words, and that this silence is itself a form of grief rather than a failure of feeling. The poet does not console herself with explanations or with the memory alone; she simply records that her mother has been dead "nearly as many years / As that girl lived" and then says, "There is nothing to say at all." The last line, "Its silence silences", shows that at a certain point grief stops speaking and simply stands still — and the poem accepts that stillness rather than fighting it.

At the same time, the poem shows the value of keeping the memory that we do have. The cardboard photograph has held the mother's girlhood safe for decades, and the memory of her laughter has become the poet's own past. Even the "laboured ease of loss" — the phrase in which the heavy work of grieving finally becomes a quiet, familiar endurance — suggests that sorrow, honestly carried over years, becomes bearable without ever disappearing. The value lesson is therefore double: it is human and right to hold on to the happy moments that a camera or a memory has preserved, and it is also human and right to admit that when the loss is complete, silence is an honest answer. We should treasure time with those we love now, because the sea "which appears to have changed less" will outlast us all.
