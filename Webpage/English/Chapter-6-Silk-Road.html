Hornbill · Class XI · Chapter 6

Ch 6 — Silk Road

Author: Nick Middleton · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: Travelogue / first-person travel narrative

1. Chapter Summary

The chapter opens on the morning the narrator leaves Ravu. A flawless half-moon floats in a perfect blue sky, and extended banks of cloud "like long French loaves" glow pink as the sun emerges and splashes the distant mountain tops with a rose-tinted blush. Now that he is leaving, Lhamo says she wants to give him a farewell present. One evening he had told her, through Daniel, that he was heading towards Mount Kailash to complete the kora, and she had said he ought to get some warmer clothes. After ducking back into her tent, she emerges carrying one of the long-sleeved sheepskin coats that all the men wore. Tsetan sizes him up as they clamber into his car: "Ah, yes," he declares, "drokba, sir."

They take a short cut to get off the Changtang, a route south-west that runs almost directly towards Mount Kailash and involves crossing several fairly high mountain passes. Tsetan is confident — "But no problem, sir… if there is no snow" — though he admits he will not know about the snow "until we get there." From the gently rolling hills of Ravu the route crosses vast open plains where a few gazelles look up from nibbling the arid pastures and bound away into the void. Where the plains turn stony, a great herd of wild ass comes into view; Tsetan calls them "kyang" and points to a far-off pall of dust long before they appear. The herd gallops en masse, wheeling and turning in tight formation "as if they were practising manoeuvres on some predetermined course", and plumes of dust billow into the crisp, clean air.

As hills push up again from the rocky wilderness they pass solitary drokbas — sometimes men, sometimes women — tending their flocks, well-wrapped figures who pause, stare and occasionally wave. Sheep take evasive action as the vehicle passes. They also pass nomads' dark tents pitched in splendid isolation, usually guarded by a huge black Tibetan mastiff. These "shaggy monsters, blacker than the darkest night" wear bright red collars, cock their great heads, and then explode into action, speeding at the car "like a bullet from a gun". Completely fearless, they shoot straight into the vehicle's path, forcing Tsetan to brake and swerve, and chase for a hundred metres or so before easing off. The narrator understands why ferocious Tibetan mastiffs became popular in China's imperial courts as hunting dogs, brought along the Silk Road in ancient times as tribute from Tibet.

Snow-capped mountains gather on the horizon. They enter a valley where the river is wide and mostly clogged with ice, brilliant white and glinting in the sunshine; the trail hugs its bank and twists with the meanders as they gain height and the valley sides close in. The turns sharpen, the ride grows bumpier, and Tsetan changes to third gear. The track leaves the icy river and labours through steeper slopes sporting big rocks daubed with patches of bright orange lichen, with hunks of snow clinging on beneath them in near-permanent shade. The narrator feels pressure building in his ears, holds his nose, snorts and clears them. Rounding a tight bend, Tsetan stops and jumps out: "Snow," says Daniel, who also exits.

A swathe of snow lies across the track, stretching for maybe fifteen metres before it peters out and the dirt trail reappears. The bank on the upslope side is too steep to scale, so there is no way round. Tsetan steps onto the encrusted snow and slithers forward, stamping his foot to test how sturdy it is. The narrator's wristwatch reads 5,210 metres above sea level. Daniel explains that the danger is not the depth so much as the icy top layer — "If we slip off, the car could turn over." Tsetan flings handfuls of dirt across the frozen surface, Daniel and the narrator pitch in, and then the two of them stay out of the vehicle to lighten the load while Tsetan eases the car across. Ten minutes later they stop at another blockage; this time Tsetan negotiates the steep, rock-studded slope, his four-wheel drive lurching from one obstacle to the next, and cuts off one of the hairpin bends to regain the trail further up where the snow has not drifted.

As they climb past 5,400 metres the narrator's head begins to throb horribly and he takes gulps from his water bottle, which is supposed to help a rapid ascent. They reach the top of the pass at 5,515 metres, marked by a large cairn of rocks festooned with white silk scarves and ragged prayer flags. Everyone takes a turn round the cairn in a clockwise direction, as is the tradition, and Tsetan checks the tyres. At the petrol tank he partially unscrews the top, which emits a loud hiss: the lower atmospheric pressure is allowing the fuel to expand. It sounds dangerous, and Tsetan laughs — "Maybe, sir… but no smoking."

The headache clears as they career down the other side. At two o'clock they stop for lunch and eat hot noodles inside a long canvas tent, part of a workcamp erected beside a dry salt lake. The plateau, the narrator explains, is pockmarked with salt flats and brackish lakes, vestiges of the Tethys Ocean which bordered Tibet before the great continental collision lifted it skyward. This particular lake is a hive of activity: men with pickaxes and shovels trudge back and forth in long sheepskin coats and salt-encrusted boots, all wearing sunglasses against the glare, while a steady stream of blue trucks emerges from the blindingly white lake laden with piles of salt.

By late afternoon they reach the small town of Hor, back on the main east-west highway that follows the old trade route from Lhasa to Kashmir. Daniel, who is returning to Lhasa, finds a ride in a truck and is bade farewell outside a tyre-repair shop; they had suffered two punctures in quick succession on the drive down from the salt lake, and the replacement tyre was "as smooth as my bald head". Hor itself is a grim, miserable place — no vegetation whatsoever, just dust and rocks liberally scattered with years of accumulated refuse, which is unfortunate because the town sits on the shore of Lake Manasarovar, Tibet's most venerated stretch of water. Ancient Hindu and Buddhist cosmology pinpoints Manasarovar as the source of four great Indian rivers — the Indus, the Ganges, the Sutlej and the Brahmaputra — though actually only the Sutlej flows from the lake; the headwaters of the others rise nearby on the flanks of Mount Kailash.

The narrator is eager to forge ahead, but he has to wait. Tsetan sends him to drink tea in Hor's only cafe, built like the town's other buildings from badly painted concrete and fitted with three broken windows — the good view of the lake through one of them compensating for the draught. He is served by a Chinese youth in military uniform who spreads the grease around on the table with a filthy rag before bringing him a glass and a thermos of tea. Half an hour later Tsetan relieves him and they drive past more rocks and rubbish westwards out of town. His experience in Hor comes as a stark contrast to the accounts he had read of earlier travellers' first encounters with Lake Manasarovar: Ekai Kawaguchi, a Japanese monk who arrived in 1900, was so moved by the sanctity of the lake that he burst into tears, and a couple of years later the hallowed waters had a similar effect on Sven Hedin, a Swede who wasn't prone to sentimental outbursts.

It is dark by the time they leave, and after 10.30 p.m. they draw up outside a guest house in Darchen for another troubled night. Kicking around in the open-air rubbish dump that passed for the town of Hor had set off his cold once more, and one nostril is blocked again. His watch tells him he is at 4,760 metres — not much higher than Ravu, where he had been gasping for oxygen several times every night. He starts breathing through his mouth, then switches to "single-nostril power", but each time he is on the point of sleep he wakes abruptly with a strangely heavy chest. Sitting up clears his nasal passages and relieves the feeling at once, and as soon as he lies down again his sinuses fill. He props himself upright against the wall but cannot relax; a little voice inside tells him that if he sleeps he might never wake up again. So he stays awake all night.

The following morning Tsetan takes him to the Darchen medical college, which is new and looks like a monastery from the outside, with a very solid door leading into a large courtyard. The consulting room is dark and cold, and the Tibetan doctor wears none of the paraphernalia the narrator expected — no white coat, just a thick pullover and a woolly hat, looking like any other Tibetan. He shoots a few questions while feeling the veins in the narrator's wrist, then says through Tsetan: "It's a cold… a cold and the effects of altitude. I'll give you something for it." Asked whether he will recover enough to do the kora, the doctor says, "Oh yes… you'll be fine."

The narrator walks out clutching a brown envelope stuffed with fifteen screws of paper — a five-day course of Tibetan medicine which he starts right away. The after-breakfast package contains a brown powder to be taken with hot water that tastes just like cinnamon; the lunchtime and bedtime packages contain small, spherical brown pellets that look suspiciously like sheep dung. He takes them all the same, and after his first full day's course he sleeps very soundly — "Like a log, not a dead man." Once he sees the narrator is going to live, Tsetan leaves to return to Lhasa, remarking that as a Buddhist he knew it didn't really matter if the narrator passed away, but he thought it would be bad for business.

Darchen doesn't look so horrible after a good night's sleep. It is still dusty, partially derelict and punctuated by heaps of rubble and refuse, but the sun shines brilliantly in a clear blue sky and the outlook across the plain to the south gives a vision of the Himalayas, commanded by a huge, snow-capped mountain, Gurla Mandhata, with just a wisp of cloud over its summit. The town has a couple of rudimentary general stores selling Chinese cigarettes, soap and other basic provisions, as well as strings of prayer flags; in front of one, men gather in the afternoon for a game of pool on a battered table that looks supremely incongruous in the open air, while nearby women wash their long hair in the icy water of a narrow brook that babbles past the guest house. Darchen feels relaxed and unhurried, but it comes with a significant drawback for the narrator: there are no pilgrims. He had timed his arrival for the beginning of the pilgrimage season, but he was too early — at the height of the season the town would be bustling with visitors who brought their own accommodation and set up tents that spilled down onto the plain.

Sitting over a glass of tea in Darchen's only cafe, he concludes that his options are severely limited: he can only wait. The pilgrimage trail is well-trodden but he doesn't fancy doing it alone; the kora is seasonal because parts of the route are liable to blockage by snow; he has no idea whether the snow has cleared, and the chunks of dirty ice still clinging to the banks of Darchen's brook do not encourage him. Since Tsetan left, he has not met anyone in Darchen with enough English to answer even this most basic question — until he meets Norbu.

The cafe is small, dark and cavernous, with a long metal stove running down the middle and walls and ceiling wreathed in sheets of multi-coloured striped plastic of the kind made into stout, voluminous shopping bags sold all over China and in many other countries of Asia and Europe — plastic, the narrator notes wryly, must rate as one of China's most successful exports along the Silk Road today. Norbu sees the narrator's book, gestures to ask if he may sit at the rickety table, and asks, "You English?" He is wearing a windcheater and metal-rimmed spectacles of a Western style, and he tells the narrator that he is Tibetan but works in Beijing at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, in the Institute of Ethnic Literature. The narrator assumes he is on fieldwork. "Yes and no," says Norbu — "I have come to do the kora." Norbu has been writing academic papers about the Kailash kora and its importance in various works of Buddhist literature for many years, but has never actually done it himself. When the narrator explains what brought him to Darchen, Norbu's eyes light up: "We could be a team… two academics who have escaped from the library." The narrator feels his positive-thinking strategy may be working after all.

His relief is tempered by the realisation that Norbu is almost as ill-equipped as he is: Norbu keeps telling him how fat he is and how hard the walk will be — "Very high up… so tiresome to walk" — and he is not really a practising Buddhist, though he has enthusiasm and is, of course, Tibetan. The narrator had originally envisaged making the trek in the company of devout believers, but on reflection decides that Norbu may turn out to be the ideal companion. Norbu suggests they hire some yaks to carry their luggage, which the narrator reads as a good sign, and he has no intention of prostrating himself all round the mountain — "Not possible," he cries, collapsing across the table in hysterical laughter. It isn't his style, and anyway his tummy is too big.

Last-minute revision hook: Farewell at Ravu and Lhamo's sheepskin coat → the short cut off the Changtang with the kyang and the mastiffs → the snow patches at 5,210 metres and the pass at 5,515 metres → the salt lake workcamp and grim Hor → sleepless night at Darchen (4,760 metres) → the Tibetan doctor and his five-day course of medicine → Norbu, and the plan to do the kora together.

3. Theme

The gap between legend and reality. The chapter's sharpest idea is that a place famous in legend can meet you as something plain and disappointing. Lake Manasarovar is holy water, the source, in ancient Hindu and Buddhist cosmology, of four great Indian rivers, and earlier travellers were so overcome before it that the monk Ekai Kawaguchi burst into tears in 1900 and even the unsentimental Sven Hedin was moved. Yet the town on its shore, Hor, is a grim, miserable place with no vegetation, only dust, rocks and years of accumulated refuse. The narrator's own first experience of the lake is a greasy table wiped with a filthy rag and a thermos of tea. Reality and legend simply do not match, and the honest travelogue records both.

Travel as endurance, not just scenery. This is a journey of real physical hardship: mountain passes at 5,210 metres and 5,515 metres, a track blocked by snow, an icy top layer that could turn the car over, a throbbing head at 5,400 metres, two punctures, a cold that never quite goes away, and a night at 4,760 metres in Darchen where the narrator is afraid to fall asleep in case he never wakes up. Beauty and difficulty sit side by side — the flawless half-moon and the rose-tinted mountain tops belong to the same day as the snow patch and the aching head. The message is that such journeys are worth making, but they are not comfortable.

Faith, pilgrimage and the kora. The whole journey has a purpose: to reach Mount Kailash and complete the kora, the ritual circumambulation. The narrator takes a turn round the cairn at the top of the pass clockwise, as is the tradition; the kora itself is seasonal because snow blocks parts of the route; and pilgrims come in such numbers in season that the town swells with their tents. Faith is what draws people across this hard land — though the chapter is honest that the two companions who finally set out to do it, the narrator and Norbu, are "two academics who have escaped from the library", one of them not even a practising Buddhist.

Human kindness along the road. The narrator is constantly carried by other people's goodwill: Lhamo gives him the sheepskin coat she thinks he will need, Tsetan drives, mends, waits, teases and finally takes him to a doctor, the Tibetan doctor gives him medicine and reassurance, and Norbu offers companionship exactly when he is stuck. This is the "sensitive behaviour of hill-folk" — people who live hard lives and share what they have with a stranger.

Curiosity and adaptability are what make a traveller. The narrator copes because he stays curious and flexible. He asks about everything — "Kyang", "drokba", why the petrol tank hisses — accepts pellets that look like sheep dung, keeps notes, and revises his plan when his first idea (walking with devout believers) does not work out. His "self-help programme on positive thinking" becomes a running joke, but in the end the right companion does appear.

4. Character Sketch

Nick Middleton (the narrator and traveller)

He is a curious, observant and self-aware traveller, and this is his own first-person account. He notices everything — the cloud "like long French loaves", the herd turning "as if they were practising manoeuvres", the patches of bright orange lichen, the pink of the rock cairn's silk scarves — and records the exact facts of the journey, checking his wristwatch for altitude at each stage. He is honest about his weaknesses: he is ill-equipped, his cold never quite disappears, he suffers headaches and gasping for oxygen, and at Darchen he is genuinely frightened — "a little voice inside me was saying that if I did I might never wake up again. So I stayed awake all night." He has a dry, self-deprecating humour: the replaced tyre is "as smooth as my bald head", his medicine tastes "just like cinnamon" until it looks "suspiciously like sheep dung", and he jokes about his "self-help programme on positive thinking" and his limited success with it. He respects what he meets rather than judging it: the mastiffs are ferocious but he understands why China wanted them; Hor disappoints him but he still records the lake's holiness and the earlier travellers' tears. Above all he is determined — he wants the kora, he is "eager to forge ahead", and he keeps looking for a way to do it, agreeing to team up with Norbu when the chance comes.

Tsetan

Tsetan is the Tibetan driver and guide who carries the narrator across the plateau. He is practical, calm and expert: he knows a short cut off the Changtang that runs south-west almost directly towards Mount Kailash; he drives in third gear on the steep climbs; he tests the snow by stamping on it and flinging handfuls of dirt across the icy surface; he negotiates a steep, rock-studded slope and cuts off a hairpin bend when the track is blocked; he checks his tyres at the top of the pass. He is also honest about uncertainty rather than over-confident — asked about the likelihood of snow, he answers only, "Not knowing, sir, until we get there", and when the second blockage appears he simply says, "Not good, sir." His cheerfulness never falters: when the petrol tank hisses with the lower atmospheric pressure, he laughs, "Maybe, sir… but no smoking." He uses a distinct, simple English, addressing the narrator as "sir" and "drokba, sir". Most of all he is kind and responsible — he sends the narrator into Hor's cafe to wait in safety, takes him to the Darchen medical college, and stays until he is sure the narrator will live. His parting line — that as a Buddhist it didn't really matter if the narrator passed away, but it would be bad for business — shows a dry sense of humour wrapped around real affection.

Lhamo

Lhamo appears only at the very start, in the farewell scene at Ravu, but she sets the tone of the chapter. She is warm, thoughtful and generous — she hears through Daniel that the narrator is heading towards Mount Kailash to complete the kora, and she immediately tells him he ought to get some warmer clothes. When the day of departure comes she announces her farewell present, disappears into her tent and comes back carrying one of the long-sleeved sheepskin coats that all the men wore — a gift of real value in that cold, high place, and one chosen for his practical need rather than for show. Her silence about the gift until the moment of parting suggests a modest, undemonstrative kindness: she simply does what a host ought to do. Tsetan's approving "Ah, yes… drokba, sir" as the narrator puts the coat on shows how completely her gift makes him belong to the landscape.

Norbu

Norbu is the Tibetan academic the narrator meets in Darchen's only cafe, and his arrival turns the chapter's mood around. He is Tibetan, but he works in Beijing at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, in the Institute of Ethnic Literature, and he wears a windcheater and metal-rimmed spectacles of a Western style, which is why the narrator at first takes him for someone from elsewhere. He is a scholar of the pilgrimage he has never made: for many years he has been "writing academic papers about the Kailash kora and its importance in various works of Buddhist literature" without ever doing it himself — a lovely irony, and a sign of genuine curiosity and enthusiasm when he says, "Yes and no… I have come to do the kora." He is friendly and forward-going, gesturing to share a rickety table, striking up conversation, and instantly proposing, "We could be a team… two academics who have escaped from the library." He is also honest and self-deprecating — he keeps saying how fat he is and how hard the walk is going to be, "Very high up… so tiresome to walk", and collapses across the table in hysterical laughter at the very idea of prostrating himself all round the mountain. He is not really a practising Buddhist, but he brings enthusiasm, he is Tibetan, and he sensibly suggests hiring yaks to carry their luggage — which is exactly why the narrator comes to think he may be "the ideal companion."

5. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. Why has the article been titled 'Silk Road'? 2 marks

Answer:
The narrator's whole journey follows the great corridor of the ancient Silk Road. The small town of Hor, where he and Tsetan stop, stands on the main east-west highway that follows the old trade route from Lhasa to Kashmir, and the travellers are heading across Tibet towards Mount Kailash — the same ground once crossed by Silk Road traffic. The chapter itself keeps pointing to that older trade: the ferocious Tibetan mastiffs became popular in China's imperial courts as hunting dogs, brought along the Silk Road in ancient times as tribute from Tibet, and the multi-coloured striped plastic of the Darchen cafe is described as one of China's most successful exports along the Silk Road today. The title therefore names both the historic route the narrator is travelling and the long story of goods and ideas moving along it.

She chose it because of what she knew about the narrator's plans: one evening he had told her, through Daniel, that he was heading towards Mount Kailash to complete the kora, and she had said that he ought to get some warmer clothes. Such a ferocious and powerful dog made an ideal hunting companion, and they were brought along the Silk Road in ancient times as tribute from Tibet.

Q6. What was the author's physical condition in Darchen? 3 marks

Answer:
By the time the travellers reached Darchen the narrator was tired and hungry and his cold had come back. Kicking around in the open-air rubbish dump that passed for the town of Hor had set it off once more, though he admits it had never quite disappeared with his herbal tea. One of his nostrils was blocked again, and he was not convinced the other would give him enough oxygen. His watch put him at 4,760 metres, not much higher than Ravu, and yet at Ravu he had been gasping for oxygen several times every night — disturbances he had grown accustomed to but which still scared him. On the climb earlier he had suffered badly too: his head began to throb horribly past 5,400 metres, and he had taken gulps from his water bottle because it is supposed to help a rapid ascent. Worst of all was the sleepless night that followed. He began breathing through his mouth, then "single-nostril power", but every time he was on the point of sleep his chest felt strangely heavy and he woke abruptly; sitting up cleared it at once, and lying down again filled his sinuses. He propped himself upright against the wall, could not relax, and was afraid to go to sleep in case he never woke up. So he stayed awake all night.

Q7. Why did the author's experience at Hor come as a stark contrast to earlier accounts of the place? 3 marks

Answer:
Hor is a grim, miserable place with no vegetation whatsoever — just dust and rocks, liberally scattered with years of accumulated refuse — and yet the town sits on the shore of Lake Manasarovar, Tibet's most venerated stretch of water. Earlier travellers had approached that lake as a holy place and been overwhelmed by it: Ekai Kawaguchi, a Japanese monk who arrived there in 1900, was so moved by the sanctity of the lake that he burst into tears, and a couple of years later the hallowed waters had a similar effect on Sven Hedin, a Swede who wasn't prone to sentimental outbursts. The narrator's own first encounter could hardly be further from that. He waited in Hor's only cafe, a building of badly painted concrete with three broken windows, and was served by a Chinese youth in military uniform who spread the grease around on his table with a filthy rag before bringing him a glass and a thermos of tea. Where the others found sanctity and tears, he found grease, draught and refuse — the legend of the place and the reality of the town did not match at all.

Q8. Why was the author disappointed with Darchen? 4 marks

Answer:
The narrator was disappointed with Darchen because, for all its charm, it lacked the one thing he had come for: there were no pilgrims. He had been told that at the height of the pilgrimage season the town was bustling with visitors, many of whom brought their own accommodation and enlarged the settlement round its edges as they set up tents that spilled down on to the plain. He had timed his arrival for the beginning of the season, but it seemed he was too early. This mattered because he could not simply set off on his own. The kora was seasonal, since parts of the route were liable to blockage by snow; he had no idea whether the snow had cleared, and he was not encouraged by the chunks of dirty ice still clinging to the banks of Darchen's brook. Since Tsetan had left, he had met no one in Darchen with enough English to answer even this most basic question. Sitting over a glass of tea in Darchen's only cafe, he concluded that his options were severely limited and that he could only wait — the pilgrimage trail was well-trodden, but he didn't fancy doing it alone. The town itself also disappointed in appearance: it was still dusty, partially derelict and punctuated by heaps of rubble and refuse, though after a good night's sleep and with the sun shining brilliantly he began to see its better side — the rudimentary general stores, the men playing pool on a battered table in the open air, and the women washing their long hair in the icy brook.

Q9. How did the Tibetan doctor treat the author, and what did the author think of the treatment? 4 marks

Answer:
Tsetan took the narrator to the Darchen medical college the morning after his sleepless night. The college was new and looked like a monastery from the outside, with a very solid door leading into a large courtyard; inside, the consulting room was dark and cold. The Tibetan doctor wore none of the paraphernalia the narrator had expected — no white coat, only a thick pullover and a woolly hat, so that he looked like any other Tibetan. He shot the narrator a few questions while feeling the veins in his wrist, and then said, through Tsetan: "It's a cold… a cold and the effects of altitude. I'll give you something for it." Asked whether he would recover enough to do the kora, the doctor answered reassuringly, "Oh yes… you'll be fine."

The narrator walked out clutching a brown envelope stuffed with fifteen screws of paper — a five-day course of Tibetan medicine, which he began right away. He found it strange but took it faithfully. The after-breakfast package held a brown powder to be taken with hot water which "tasted just like cinnamon"; the lunchtime and bedtime packages held small, spherical brown pellets which "looked suspiciously like sheep dung", but he took them all the same. The result was complete success: after his first full day's course he slept very soundly — "Like a log, not a dead man."

Q10. How did the author meet Norbu, and why did the two decide to do the kora together? 4 marks

Answer:
The narrator met Norbu in Darchen's only cafe, where he had taken up position beside the single window so that he could see the pages of his notebook, and where he had brought a novel to help pass the time. Norbu saw the book when he came in and asked with a gesture whether he could sit opposite him at the rickety table; after ordering tea he asked, "You English?", and they struck up a conversation. The narrator had not thought he was from those parts, because he wore a windcheater and metal-rimmed spectacles of a Western style. Norbu told him he was Tibetan but worked in Beijing at the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences, in the Institute of Ethnic Literature. The narrator assumed he was on some sort of fieldwork. "Yes and no," Norbu said — "I have come to do the kora." He had been writing academic papers about the Kailash kora and its importance in various works of Buddhist literature for many years, but had never actually done it himself.

When the narrator told him what had brought him to Darchen, Norbu's eyes lit up: "We could be a team… two academics who have escaped from the library." It was the perfect solution to the narrator's problem, since he had been stuck in Darchen with no pilgrims, no English speakers to ask about the snow, and no wish to walk the trail alone. His initial relief was tempered by the realisation that Norbu was almost as ill-equipped as he was — Norbu kept saying how fat he was and how hard it was going to be, and reminded him, "Very high up… so tiresome to walk." Nor was Norbu really a practising Buddhist. But the narrator, who had originally envisaged making the trek in the company of devout believers, decided on reflection that Norbu might be the ideal companion: he had enthusiasm, he was Tibetan, he sensibly suggested hiring yaks to carry their luggage, and he had no intention of prostrating himself all round the mountain — "Not possible," he cried, collapsing across the table in hysterical laughter.

Q11. The narrator depended on the kindness of people he met on the way — Lhamo, Tsetan, the doctor and Norbu. What does the chapter teach us about the "sensitive behaviour of hill-folk"? 6 marks

Answer:
Throughout the chapter the narrator survives not because he is well prepared — he is ill-equipped, he catches a cold and he cannot sleep at 4,760 metres — but because the people of the plateau look after him. The first act of the chapter is Lhamo, at Ravu, giving him one of the long-sleeved sheepskin coats that all the men wore when she learns he is going to Mount Kailash to complete the kora, because she had told him he ought to get warmer clothes. She gives what is needed, not what is showy, and gives it without fuss.

Tsetan, the driver, carries that care across the whole journey. He refuses to pretend certainty about the snow — "Not knowing, sir, until we get there" — and protects the narrator at every stage: testing the frozen snow with his foot, flinging dirt across it, driving round the second blockage over the rocks, checking his tyres at the top of the pass, and making a joke even of danger — "Maybe, sir… but no smoking" when the petrol tank hisses in the low pressure. When the narrator cannot sleep at Darchen, Tsetan takes him to the Darchen medical college, waits while the Tibetan doctor — a man in a thick pullover and a woolly hat, not a white coat — feels the veins in his wrist, diagnoses "a cold and the effects of altitude" and hands over a five-day course of Tibetan medicine. Tsetan stays until he sees the narrator "was going to live", and even his joke about leaving — that as a Buddhist he knew it didn't really matter if the narrator passed away, but it would be bad for business — comes from a man who has in fact taken every care of him.

The lesson is clearest in what these people do without being asked. Nobody demands payment for reassurance; nobody treats the stranger as a nuisance. The nomads and drokbas wave as the car passes; the doctor answers the anxious question about the kora with "Oh yes… you'll be fine"; and finally Norbu, a stranger in a cafe, offers companionship at the exact moment the narrator is stranded — "We could be a team… two academics who have escaped from the library."

At the same time the chapter is honest that this kindness is practical, not sentimental. The hill-folk live hard lives in dust, snow and thin air, and their generosity takes the form of useful things: a coat, a lift, a stretch of repaired road, a parcel of medicine, a shared walk. The narrator's own experience at Hor — a grim town of refuse that nevertheless sits on Tibet's most venerated lake — reminds us that these are ordinary people in an extraordinary, unforgiving place. What the chapter finally teaches is that in such country human warmth is the real survival equipment, and that a traveller's best preparation is a willingness to accept help, and to be curious and courteous in return.

Q12. "The Silk Road gives a panoramic view of the journey to Mount Kailash." Describe the experiences of the narrator on this journey, showing how the beauty of the landscape and the hardship of travel go together. 6 marks

Answer:
The chapter is a travelogue, and its power comes from holding two things together at once: a landscape of great beauty and a journey of real physical hardship. It opens with beauty — a flawless half-moon floating in a perfect blue sky and banks of cloud "like long French loaves" glowing pink as the sun splashes the mountain tops with a rose-tinted blush — and moves immediately into a hard drive across the Changtang on a short cut that involves crossing several fairly high mountain passes. Across the vast open plains there is almost nothing but a few gazelles and a great herd of wild ass, the "kyang", galloping en masse and wheeling in tight formation as if practising manoeuvres, with plumes of dust billowing into the crisp, clean air. They pass solitary drokbas and nomads' dark tents, each guarded by a Tibetan mastiff — shaggy monsters blacker than the darkest night that shoot into the car's path and force Tsetan to brake and swerve.

The hardship then becomes personal. The road climbs into a valley where the river is wide and mostly clogged with ice, the trail twisting with the meanders, the turns sharper and the ride bumpier, Tsetan driving in third gear past rocks daubed with bright orange lichen. A swathe of snow lies across the track at 5,210 metres, and the danger is its icy top layer — "If we slip off, the car could turn over." After the dirt-throwing and the careful crossing, a second blockage forces Tsetan over a steep, rock-studded slope to cut off a hairpin bend. As they creep past 5,400 metres the narrator's head begins to throb horribly; at the top of the pass, 5,515 metres, they walk clockwise round a large cairn of rocks festooned with white silk scarves and ragged prayer flags, and the petrol tank hisses because the lower atmospheric pressure is letting the fuel expand.

Relief comes at two o'clock, with hot noodles in a long canvas tent beside a dry salt lake — a workcamp where men in sheepskin coats and salt-encrusted boots load blue trucks with salt from the blindingly white lake; the narrator explains that the plateau is pockmarked with salt flats and brackish lakes, vestiges of the Tethys Ocean. By late afternoon they reach Hor, on the old trade route from Lhasa to Kashmir, where Daniel leaves for Lhasa and where the narrator's experience of the place — grease on a table, a thermos of tea, a rubbish-strewn town on the shore of Lake Manasarovar — contrasts starkly with the tears of Ekai Kawaguchi in 1900 and the emotion of Sven Hedin. They reach Darchen after 10.30 p.m., and the narrator spends the night at 4,760 metres unable to sleep, afraid that if he did he might never wake up.

Even the illness brings the two sides of the journey together. The Tibetan doctor at the Darchen medical college — no white coat, thick pullover and woolly hat, feeling the veins in the narrator's wrist — says simply, "It's a cold… a cold and the effects of altitude", gives him a five-day course of medicine in a brown envelope of fifteen screws of paper, and assures him he will be able to do the kora. After the cinnamon-tasting powder and the brown pellets that look like sheep dung, the narrator sleeps "like a log, not a dead man". The next morning Darchen itself looks better: dusty and partially derelict still, but under a brilliant sun, with Gurla Mandhata commanding the plain to the south, the general stores, the pool players and the women washing their long hair in the icy brook. His remaining problem is that there are no pilgrims — he is too early — but in the cafe he meets Norbu, and the two "academics who have escaped from the library" agree to do the kora together.
