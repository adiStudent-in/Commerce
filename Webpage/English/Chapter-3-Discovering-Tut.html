Hornbill · Class XI · Chapter 3

Ch 3 — Discovering Tut: the Saga Continues

Author: A. R. Williams · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: Informative/expository article on an archaeological find

1. Chapter Summary

King Tut was only a teenager when he died. He was the last heir of a powerful family that had ruled Egypt and its empire for centuries, was buried laden with gold, and was eventually forgotten. Since the discovery of his tomb in 1922, the modern world has speculated about what happened to him, with murder being "the most extreme possibility". Now, leaving his tomb for the first time in almost 80 years, the boyish pharaoh underwent a CT scan that offered new clues about his life and death — and provided precise data for an accurate forensic reconstruction of him.

An angry wind stirred up ghostly dust devils as King Tut was taken from his resting place in the ancient Egyptian cemetery known as the Valley of the Kings. Dark-bellied clouds had scudded across the desert sky all day and now were veiling the stars in casket grey. It was 6 p.m. on 5 January 2005. The world's most famous mummy glided head first into a CT scanner, brought there to probe the lingering medical mysteries of a little-understood young ruler who died more than 3,300 years ago. All afternoon the usual line of tourists from around the world had descended into the cramped, rock-cut tomb some 26 feet underground to pay their respects. They gazed at the murals on the walls of the burial chamber and peered at Tut's gilded face, the most striking feature of his mummy-shaped outer coffin lid. Some read from guidebooks in a whisper; others stood silently, pondering Tut's untimely death in his late teens, or wondering with a shiver whether the pharaoh's curse — death or misfortune falling upon those who disturbed him — was really true.

"The mummy is in very bad condition because of what Carter did in the 1920s," said Zahi Hawass, Secretary General of Egypt's Supreme Council of Antiquities, as he leaned over the body for a long first look. Howard Carter was the British archaeologist who, in 1922, discovered Tut's tomb after years of futile searching. Its contents, though hastily ransacked in antiquity, were surprisingly complete — they remain the richest royal collection ever found and have become part of the pharaoh's legend. The stunning artefacts in gold, whose eternal brilliance was meant to guarantee resurrection, caused a sensation at the time of the discovery and still get the most attention. But Tut was also buried with everyday things he would want in the afterlife: board games, a bronze razor, linen undergarments, cases of food and wine.

After months of carefully recording the pharaoh's funerary treasures, Carter began investigating his three nested coffins. Opening the first, he found a shroud adorned with garlands of willow and olive leaves, wild celery, lotus petals and cornflowers — the faded evidence of a burial in March or April. When he finally reached the mummy, he ran into trouble: the ritual resins had hardened, cementing Tut to the bottom of his solid gold coffin. "No amount of legitimate force could move them," Carter wrote. He set the mummy outside in blazing sunshine that heated it to 149 degrees Fahrenheit, but nothing budged, and he reported with scientific detachment that "the consolidated material had to be chiselled away from beneath the limbs and trunk before it was possible to raise the king's remains." In his defence, Carter really had little choice — if he had not cut the mummy free, thieves would most certainly have circumvented the guards and ripped it apart to remove the gold. In Tut's time the royals were fabulously wealthy and thought, or hoped, they could take their riches with them. For his journey to the great beyond, Tut was lavished with glittering goods: precious collars, inlaid necklaces and bracelets, rings, amulets, a ceremonial apron, sandals, sheaths for his fingers and toes, and the now iconic inner coffin and mask — all of pure gold. To separate Tut from his adornments, Carter's men removed the mummy's head and severed nearly every major joint, then reassembled the remains on a layer of sand in a wooden box with padding that concealed the damage — the bed where Tut now rests.

King Tut's demise was a big event, even by royal standards: he was the last of his family's line, and his funeral was the death rattle of a dynasty. Amenhotep III — Tut's father or grandfather — was a powerful pharaoh who ruled for almost four decades at the height of the eighteenth dynasty's golden age. His son Amenhotep IV succeeded him and initiated one of the strangest periods in the history of ancient Egypt: he promoted the worship of the Aten, the sun disk, changed his name to Akhenaten ("servant of the Aten"), moved the religious capital from the old city of Thebes to the new city of Akhetaten, known now as Amarna, and shocked the country by attacking Amun, a major god, smashing his images and closing his temples. "It must have been a horrific time," said Ray Johnson, director of the University of Chicago's research centre in Luxor, the site of ancient Thebes. "The family that had ruled for centuries was coming to an end, and then Akhenaten went a little wacky." After Akhenaten's death, a mysterious ruler named Smenkhkare appeared briefly and exited with hardly a trace. Then a very young Tutankhaten took the throne — King Tut as he is widely known today. The boy king soon changed his name to Tutankhamun, "living image of Amun", and oversaw a restoration of the old ways. He reigned for about nine years — and then died unexpectedly.

Archaeology had changed substantially in the intervening decades, focusing less on treasure and more on the fascinating details of life and intriguing mysteries of death, and using more sophisticated tools, including medical technology. In 1968, more than 40 years after Carter's discovery, an anatomy professor X-rayed the mummy and revealed a startling fact: beneath the resin that cakes his chest, his breast-bone and front ribs are missing. Today diagnostic imaging can be done with computed tomography, or CT, by which hundreds of X-rays in cross section are put together like slices of bread to create a three-dimensional virtual body. Two of the biggest questions still lingered about Tut: how did he die, and how old was he at the time of his death?

Regardless of his fame, Tut is one mummy among many in Egypt. The Egyptian Mummy Project, which began an inventory in late 2003, had recorded almost 600 so far and was still counting. Its next phase was to scan the mummies with a portable CT machine donated by the National Geographic Society and Siemens, its manufacturer — and King Tut was one of the first mummies to be scanned, "in death, as in life, moving regally ahead of his countrymen". The machine scanned the mummy head to toe, creating 1,700 digital X-ray images in cross section, and Tut's head, scanned in 0.62 millimetre slices to register its intricate structures, takes on eerie detail in the resulting image. A team of specialists in radiology, forensics and anatomy began to probe the secrets that the winged goddesses of a gilded burial shrine had protected for so long.

On the night of the scan, workmen carried Tut from the tomb in his box. Like pallbearers they climbed a ramp and a flight of stairs into the swirling sand outside, then rose on a hydraulic lift into the trailer that held the scanner. Twenty minutes later two men emerged, sprinted for an office nearby and returned with a pair of white plastic fans — the million-dollar scanner had quit because of sand in a cooler fan. "Curse of the pharaoh," joked a guard nervously. Eventually the substitute fans worked well enough to finish the procedure, and after checking that no data had been lost the technicians turned Tut over to the workmen, who carried him back to his tomb. Less than three hours after he was removed from his coffin, the pharaoh again rested in peace where the funerary priests had laid him so long ago.

Back in the trailer a technician pulled up astonishing images of Tut on a computer screen. A grey head took shape from a scattering of pixels, and the technician spun and tilted it in every direction. Neck vertebrae appeared as clearly as in an anatomy class. Other images revealed a hand, several views of the rib cage, and a transection of the skull. Sitting back in his chair, Zahi Hawass smiled, visibly relieved that nothing had gone seriously wrong. "I didn't sleep last night, not for a second," he said. "I was so worried. But now I think I will go and sleep." By the time the author left the trailer, descending metal stairs to the sandy ground, the wind had stopped. The winter air lay cold and still, like death itself, in this valley of the departed. Just above the entrance to Tut's tomb stood Orion — the constellation the ancient Egyptians knew as the soul of Osiris, the god of the afterlife — watching over the boy king.

Last-minute revision hook: Teenage Tut, forgotten for 3,300 years → 1922, Carter finds the tomb → gold, hardened resins and a chiselled mummy → 1968 X-ray, missing ribs → 5 January 2005, the CT scan and its eerie images → the saga continues under Orion.

3. Theme

The pull of an unsolved mystery. Tut died a teenager more than 3,300 years ago, and the modern world has speculated about him ever since his tomb was found in 1922, "with murder being the most extreme possibility". Two questions hang over the whole chapter — how did he die, and how old was he? The chapter keeps the mystery open rather than closing it, which is exactly why the title says the saga continues.

Science and technology as the new key to the past. Archaeology has changed: it now focuses "less on treasure and more on the fascinating details of life and intriguing mysteries of death". The 1968 X-ray and the 2005 CT scan show the shift from digging for gold to reading bones — a portable CT machine that turned a 3,300-year-old body into a three-dimensional virtual one, giving precise data for a forensic reconstruction.

The ethics of disturbing the dead. Carter had to chisel hardened resin off the body, remove the mummy's head and sever nearly every major joint, and then conceal the damage under padding; yet, as the chapter admits, if he had not cut the mummy free, thieves would have circumvented the guards and ripped it apart. The tourists who whisper in the 26-feet-deep tomb and the guard who nervously jokes about the curse both keep the moral question alive: how far may curiosity go?

Legend versus evidence. The pharaoh's curse is present from the first page to the night of the scan, but the chapter treats it as a human belief, not a fact — a guard's nervous joke when the million-dollar scanner quits because of sand in a cooler fan. The real danger to Tut came not from a curse but from robbers, resins and sand.

Time, mortality and continuity. From Tut's untimely death "in his late teens" to the winter air lying "cold and still, like death itself", the chapter keeps death in view. And yet the last image is hopeful and continuous: Orion, "the constellation that the ancient Egyptians knew as the soul of Osiris", standing above the tomb and watching over the boy king, while new technology keeps re-opening his story.

4. Character Sketch

King Tutankhamun

The boy king around whom the whole chapter turns. He was the last heir of a powerful family, died a teenager more than 3,300 years ago, was buried laden with gold, and was then forgotten until 1922. He began life on the throne as Tutankhaten, changed his name to Tutankhamun, "living image of Amun", restored the old ways after Akhenaten's religious revolution, reigned about nine years and "died unexpectedly". He does not act in the chapter — he is the silent centre of it. Everything else is other people's response to him: the tourists pondering his untimely death, Carter chiselling him free, Hawass leaning over him "for a long first look". Even in death he leads his countrymen, as one of the first mummies to be scanned, "moving regally ahead" of the rest.

Howard Carter

The British archaeologist who in 1922 discovered Tut's tomb after years of futile searching. He is patient and methodical — he spent months carefully recording the pharaoh's funerary treasures before he even began investigating the three nested coffins. He is also practical and unemotional: when the hardened ritual resins refused to move, he used the desert sun, then chiselled, and reported it all with scientific detachment. He is not cruel: he had little choice, since thieves would have circumvented the guards and destroyed the body for its gold, and afterwards he had the remains reassembled on sand in a padded wooden box that concealed the damage. Still, his work is resented — "the mummy is in very bad condition because of what Carter did in the 1920s."

Zahi Hawass

Secretary General of Egypt's Supreme Council of Antiquities and the man in charge on the night of the scan. He is blunt and unafraid to criticise the past, saying plainly that the mummy is in very bad condition because of Carter. But he is also deeply anxious about the mummy's safety: "I didn't sleep last night, not for a second... I was so worried." When the scan succeeds he sits back, smiles, is "visibly relieved that nothing had gone seriously wrong", and jokes about finally going to sleep. He is the human face of modern, protective archaeology.

Ray Johnson

Director of the University of Chicago's research centre in Luxor, the site of ancient Thebes. He appears only in one quotation, but he gives the chapter its historical judgement: "It must have been a horrific time... The family that had ruled for centuries was coming to an end, and then Akhenaten went a little wacky." His blunt, everyday phrasing ("a little wacky") turns distant Egyptian politics into something a reader can feel.

The modern scientists and technicians

The unnamed team of specialists in radiology, forensics and anatomy who probed the secrets that "the winged goddesses of a gilded burial shrine protected for so long". They work gently and without greed: the workmen carry Tut "like pallbearers", two men sprint off for white plastic fans when the million-dollar scanner quits, the technicians check that no data had been lost before handing him back. In the trailer, a technician pulls up astonishing images and spins the grey head in every direction. Their curiosity — how did he die, and how old was he? — is the new face of archaeology. Behind them stands the now-anonymous anatomy professor who X-rayed the mummy in 1968 and found that the breast-bone and front ribs were missing.

Q9. List the advances in technology that have improved forensic analysis. 4 marks

Answer:
Earlier, all that could be done was to X-ray the mummy — in 1968 an anatomy professor did so and discovered that, beneath the resin that cakes his chest, the breast-bone and front ribs are missing. Today diagnostic imaging can be done with computed tomography, or CT, in which hundreds of X-rays in cross section are put together "like slices of bread" to create a three-dimensional virtual body. The 2005 scan used a portable CT machine donated by the National Geographic Society and Siemens, its manufacturer, and produced 1,700 digital X-ray images, with the head recorded in 0.62 millimetre slices. A team of specialists in radiology, forensics and anatomy then studied these images, giving precise data for a forensic reconstruction. Archaeology has thus moved from a hunt for treasure to a scientific study of the details of life and the mysteries of death.

Q10. Explain the statement, "King Tut is one of the first mummies to be scanned — in death, as in life, moving regally ahead of his countrymen." 4 marks

Answer:
The Egyptian Mummy Project began an inventory of Egypt's mummies in late 2003, had recorded almost 600 and was still counting. Its next phase was to scan them with the portable CT machine donated by the National Geographic Society and Siemens, and King Tut was among the first mummies to be scanned. The statement points to a gentle irony. In life, Tut was a pharaoh, a royal who stood above his countrymen; and even in death he is again ahead of them, the first to be examined with the newest technology while hundreds of other mummies still wait their turn. It also hints at the chapter's larger point — that the boy king's story is not closed but keeps being reopened by each new scientific advance.

Q12. Value-based: Scientific investigation gave the world new knowledge about Tut, but it also damaged his body. What, in your view, matters more — knowledge or respect for the dead? Discuss with evidence from the chapter. 6 marks

Answer:
The chapter makes a strong case for knowledge. Tut died more than 3,300 years ago and the world has speculated about him since 1922, "with murder being the most extreme possibility". Only scientific investigation could answer the two questions that still linger — how did he die, and how old was he? The CT scan of 5 January 2005 produced 1,700 digital X-ray images, including the head in 0.62 millimetre slices, and gave "precise data for an accurate forensic reconstruction of the boyish pharaoh"; the 1968 X-ray had already revealed that his breast-bone and front ribs were missing. Without such work, these facts would have stayed buried with him.

Yet the chapter is equally honest about the cost of disrespect. The ritual resins had cemented Tut to his solid gold coffin, and to free him Carter's men removed the mummy's head and severed nearly every major joint, afterwards concealing the damage with padding. That is why Zahi Hawass says plainly that "the mummy is in very bad condition because of what Carter did in the 1920s."

In my view the two aims need not be enemies; what matters is the method and the motive. Carter worked in an age when archaeology was a hunt for treasure, but the chapter defends the cut: he really had little choice, since thieves would otherwise have circumvented the guards and ripped the body apart to remove the gold. Modern researchers work differently: the scanner was used non-destructively, the mummy was out of its tomb for less than three hours and was then laid back "in peace where the funerary priests had laid him so long ago". Knowledge earned with care, and shared with the world, honours the dead rather than robbing them — which is exactly the note the chapter ends on, with the wind stopped, the winter air lying "cold and still, like death itself", and Orion keeping watch over the boy king.
