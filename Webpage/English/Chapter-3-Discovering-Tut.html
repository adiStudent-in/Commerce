Ch 3 — Discovering Tut: The Saga Continues (+ 2 Poems)

Author: A. R. Williams · Poems: The Laburnum Top (Ted Hughes), The Voice of the Rain (Walt Whitman) · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: Science-meets-history account + nature poems

One line to remember: King Tut — a teenage pharaoh dead 3,300 years — leaves his tomb for a CT scan that offers clues to his life and death; then a silent laburnum tree bursts alive when the goldfinch arrives, and the rain calls itself the "Poem of Earth", rising eternally to give life back to its origin.

1. The Boy King & His Tomb

He was just a teenager when he died — the last heir of a powerful family that had ruled Egypt and its empire for centuries. Laid to rest laden with gold, he was eventually forgotten; since the discovery of his tomb in 1922, the modern world has speculated about what happened to him, with murder being the most extreme possibility. At 6 p.m. on 5 January 2005, in the ancient cemetery called the Valley of the Kings, an angry wind stirred up ghostly dust devils as dark-bellied clouds veiled the stars in casket grey: the world's most famous mummy glided head first into a CT scanner — leaving his tomb for the first time in almost 80 years — to probe the lingering medical mysteries of this little-understood young ruler dead more than 3,300 years ago. All afternoon tourists had descended into the cramped rock-cut tomb some 26 feet underground, gazing at the murals and at Tut's gilded face on the outer coffin lid, some whispering from guidebooks, others shivering at the thought of the pharaoh's curse — death or misfortune falling upon those who disturbed him.

2. Carter & the Ransacked Mummy

"The mummy is in very bad condition because of what Carter did in the 1920s," said Zahi Hawass, Secretary General of Egypt's Supreme Council of Antiquities. Howard Carter, the British archaeologist, had discovered Tut's tomb in 1922 after years of futile searching — its contents hastily ransacked in antiquity yet surprisingly complete: the richest royal collection ever found. Stunning gold artefacts, their eternal brilliance meant to guarantee resurrection, sat beside everyday things Tut would want in the afterlife — board games, a bronze razor, linen undergarments, cases of food and wine. After months of recording the funerary treasures, Carter investigated the three nested coffins: inside lay a shroud adorned with garlands of willow and olive leaves, wild celery, lotus petals and cornflowers — faded evidence of a burial in March or April. Then trouble: ritual resins had hardened, cementing Tut to the bottom of his solid gold coffin. Carter baked the mummy in blazing sunshine at 149 degrees Fahrenheit for hours — nothing budged — and finally reported with scientific detachment that the consolidated material "had to be chiselled away from beneath the limbs and trunk." In his defence, he had little choice: otherwise thieves would certainly have circumvented the guards and ripped the mummy apart for its gold. Still, to separate Tut from his adornments — precious collars, inlaid necklaces, bracelets, rings, amulets, a ceremonial apron, sandals, finger-and-toe sheaths, the iconic inner coffin and mask, all pure gold — Carter's men removed the mummy's head and severed nearly every major joint, reassembling the remains on a layer of sand in a padded wooden box, the bed where Tut now rests.

3. New Science, Old Mystery

Archaeology has changed substantially since Carter's days — focusing less on treasure and more on the fascinating details of life and intriguing mysteries of death, using sophisticated tools including medical technology. In 1968, more than 40 years after the discovery, an anatomy professor X-rayed the mummy and revealed a startling fact: beneath the resin caking his chest, Tut's breast-bone and front ribs are missing. Today diagnostic imaging uses computed tomography (CT) — hundreds of cross-section X-rays put together like slices of bread to create a three-dimensional virtual body. Could it answer the two biggest lingering questions: how did Tut die, and how old was he? His demise was a big event even by royal standards — he was the last of his family's line, and his funeral was the death rattle of a dynasty — yet the particulars of his passing remain unclear.

4. The Royal Family Drama

Amenhotep III — Tut's father or grandfather — ruled almost four decades at the height of the eighteenth dynasty's golden age. His son Amenhotep IV succeeded him and initiated one of the strangest periods in ancient Egypt's history: he promoted the worship of the Aten, the sun disk, changed his name to Akhenaten ("servant of the Aten"), moved the religious capital from Thebes to the new city of Akhetaten (now Amarna), and shocked the country by attacking Amun, a major god — smashing his images and closing his temples. "It must have been a horrific time," said Ray Johnson, director of the University of Chicago's research centre in Luxor; "the family that had ruled for centuries was coming to an end, and then Akhenaten went a little wacky." After Akhenaten died, a mysterious ruler, Smenkhkare, appeared briefly and exited with hardly a trace. Then a very young Tutankhaten took the throne — soon renaming himself Tutankhamun ("living image of Amun") and overseeing a restoration of the old ways. He reigned about nine years — and died unexpectedly.

5. The CT Scan Night

Tut is one mummy among many: the Egyptian Mummy Project, which began its inventory in late 2003, has recorded almost 600 so far and is still counting, now scanning them with a portable CT machine donated by the National Geographic Society and Siemens. King Tut is one of the first mummies to be scanned — in death, as in life, moving regally ahead of his countrymen. That night workmen carried Tut from his tomb like pallbearers, up a ramp and stairs into the swirling sand, then by hydraulic lift into the scanner trailer. Twenty minutes later two men sprinted out and returned with white plastic fans — the million-dollar scanner had quit because sand clogged a cooler fan. "Curse of the pharaoh," joked a guard nervously. Substitute fans worked well enough, and the procedure finished: 1,700 digital X-ray images in cross section, Tut's head scanned in 0.62 millimetre slices taking on eerie detail. Less than three hours after removal, the pharaoh again rested in peace where the funerary priests had laid him so long ago. In the trailer, astonishing images bloomed on screen — a grey head spun and tilted from scattered pixels, neck vertebrae clear as in an anatomy class, plus views of a hand, rib cage and skull transection. Zahi Hawass smiled, visibly relieved: "I didn't sleep last night, not for a second… but now I think I will go and sleep." Outside, the wind had stopped; the winter air lay cold and still, like death itself, in this valley of the departed — and just above Tut's tomb entrance stood Orion, the constellation the Egyptians knew as the soul of Osiris, the god of the afterlife, watching over the boy king.

✒️ Poem 1 — The Laburnum Top (Ted Hughes) 🌳

(Separate poem printed with this chapter — not part of the prose.)

One line to remember: A silent, still laburnum tree bursts into life when a goldfinch arrives to feed her chicks, then flies away — and the tree subsides to empty silence again.

Stanza 1

"The Laburnum top is silent, quite still / In the afternoon yellow September sunlight, / A few leaves yellowing, all its seeds fallen. // Till the goldfinch comes, with a twitching chirrup / A suddenness, a startlement, at a branch end."

Explanation: The poem opens with complete stillness: the laburnum (a short tree with hanging branches, yellow flowers and poisonous seeds) stands silent in the yellow September afternoon light, its leaves yellowing and all its seeds fallen — an image of quiet, approaching barrenness. This silence breaks only when a goldfinch (a small singing bird with yellow feathers on its wings) arrives with a twitching chirrup, bringing suddenness and startlement to the branch-end.

Best-answer point: beginning = silence/stillness; the bird's arrival is sudden and startling against it.

Stanza 2

"Then sleek as a lizard, and alert, and abrupt, / She enters the thickness, and a machine starts up / Of chitterings, and a tremor of wings, and trillings — / The whole tree trembles and thrills. / It is the engine of her family. / She stokes it full, then flirts out to a branch-end / Showing her barred face identity mask"

Explanation: Sleek, alert and abrupt like a lizard, the bird slips into the thick foliage where her nest hides — and instantly a "machine" starts up: chitterings, a tremor of wings, trillings; the whole tree trembles and thrills. The tree-with-nest is the engine of her family: she "stokes it full" (feeds her young), then flirts out to a branch-end showing her barred face identity mask — the stripes on her face that mark her identity.

Best-answer point: movement compared to a lizard (basis: sleek, smooth, alert, abrupt motion); the "machine/engine" image shows the tree as the energy-source of her family life.

Stanza 3

"Then with eerie delicate whistle-chirrup whisperings / She launches away, towards the infinite / And the laburnum subsides to empty."

Explanation: With delicate, eerie whistle-chirrup whisperings the bird launches away "towards the infinite" — the open sky — and the laburnum subsides to empty silence once more. The poem thus moves in a circle: silence → sudden life and energy → silence again.

Best-answer point: beginning and ending are both silent/still — the bird's visit is the brief burst of life between them; dominant colour = yellow (sunlight, leaves, flowers, bird's feathers).

✒️ Poem 2 — The Voice of the Rain (Walt Whitman) 🌧️

(Separate poem printed with this chapter — not part of the prose.)

One line to remember: The rain tells the poet it is the "Poem of Earth" — eternally rising as vapour, descending to wash the droughts and give life to hidden seeds, just as a song returns duly with love to its birth-place.

Part 1 — The Question

"And who art thou? said I to the soft-falling shower, / Which, strange to tell, gave me an answer, as here translated:"

Explanation: The poet asks the softly falling rain who it is — and, "strange to tell," the shower replies (its answer given here in translation). Two voices speak in the poem: the poet's and the rain's.

Best-answer point: "strange to tell" = surprisingly, unbelievably — rain never speaks, yet here it answers.

Part 2 — The Rain's Reply

"I am the Poem of Earth, said the voice of the rain, / Eternal I rise impalpable out of the land and the bottomless sea, / Upward to heaven, whence, vaguely form'd, altogether changed, and yet the same, / I descend to lave the droughts, atomies, dust-layers of the globe, / And all that in them without me were seeds only, latent, unborn; / And forever, by day and night, I give back life to my own origin, / And make pure and beautify it;"

Explanation: The rain calls itself the "Poem of Earth" — nature's own song. It rises eternally, impalpable (untouchable, as vapour) from the land and bottomless sea toward heaven; changed in form yet somehow the same, it descends to lave (wash) the droughts, atomies (tiny particles) and dust-layers of the globe, awakening seeds that without it would stay latent (hidden) and unborn. By day and night, forever, it gives back life to its own origin and purifies and beautifies it — the water cycle described poetically, exactly as science explains it: evaporation → cloud formation → rainfall → life on earth.

Best-answer point: cyclic movement = evaporation from sea/land → clouds ("vaguely form'd") → rain washing the earth → reviving seeds; matches the scientific water cycle.

Part 3 — The Song Parallel

"(For song, issuing from its birth-place, after fulfilment, wandering / Reck'd or unreck'd, duly with love returns.)"

Explanation: In these bracketed lines the poet draws the parallel between rain and music/poetry: a song, born at its source and fulfilled in the world, wanders — heeded (reck'd) or ignored (unreck'd) — but duly returns "with love" to its origin, just as rain returns to the earth. Both are eternal cycles of giving. The brackets show this is a general truth added as an aside.

Best-answer point: opposites in the poem — rise/fall, land/sea, day/night, reck'd/unreck'd, form'd formless; rain : earth :: song : poet's heart.

6. Exam Corner 📝✅

Prose — Long Answer (Exam-Ready)

"How has modern science and technology helped unravel the mysteries surrounding King Tut's life and death?"

King Tut, the last heir of a powerful eighteenth-dynasty family, died mysteriously in his late teens and lay forgotten in the Valley of the Kings until Howard Carter discovered his treasure-filled tomb in 1922. Carter's investigation, however, was destructive: to free the mummy cemented by hardened ritual resins to its gold coffin, his men removed its head and severed nearly every major joint — though had he not done so, thieves would surely have ripped the body apart for gold.

Archaeology later turned from treasure to truth. An X-ray in 1968 revealed that Tut's breast-bone and front ribs were missing beneath the chest resin, deepening the mystery of his untimely death. The real breakthrough came on 5 January 2005, when the mummy underwent a CT scan — hundreds of cross-section X-rays combined into a three-dimensional virtual body — producing 1,700 digital images and precise data for an accurate forensic reconstruction of the boyish pharaoh. Despite a sand-blocked cooler fan that briefly revived jokes of the "pharaoh's curse," the scan went smoothly, and Tut returned to his tomb within three hours.

The story also traces the historical backdrop: Akhenaten's "wacky" religious revolution — worshipping the Aten, shifting the capital, smashing Amun's images — and young Tutankhamun's restoration of the old ways before his unexpected death ended the dynasty. Thus, from Carter's chisel to the CT scanner, each generation's tools have brought science closer to answering the twin questions: how did Tut die, and how old was he?

(From these paragraphs you can frame short answers on Carter's investigation, the family drama, the CT scan or the curse anecdote — pick the relevant part.)

Prose — Expected Questions & Pointers ✅

Q1. Give reasons:
→ (i) Repeated scrutiny of Tut's body: he died prematurely in his late teens amid murder rumours; the 1968 X-ray found his breast-bone and front ribs missing; the 2005 CT scan sought clues about his age and cause of death for forensic reconstruction.
→ (ii) Carter's investigation was resented: to separate the mummy from its gold adornments his men removed the head and severed nearly every major joint, damaging the body badly — though otherwise thieves would have ripped it apart.
→ (iii) Resins had to be chiselled away: ritual resins had hardened, cementing Tut to the solid gold coffin; even 149°F sunshine could not loosen them, and "no amount of legitimate force could move them."
→ (iv) Gilded treasures buried with him: royals were fabulously wealthy and hoped to take their riches to the great beyond; the gold's eternal brilliance was meant to guarantee resurrection.
→ (v) Name change: Tutankhaten ("living image of the Aten") became Tutankhamun ("living image of Amun") to restore the old ways — worship of Amun — after Akhenaten's Aten revolution.

Q2. List the deeds that led Ray Johnson to call Akhenaten "wacky".
→ Promoted worship of the Aten (sun disk); changed his name to Akhenaten; moved the capital from Thebes to Akhetaten (Amarna); attacked the major god Amun — smashing his images and closing his temples.

Q3. What were the results of the CT scan?
→ 1,700 digital cross-section X-rays creating a 3-D virtual body; head scanned in 0.62 mm slices showing eerie detail; clear images of neck vertebrae, hand, rib cage and skull; precise data for an accurate forensic reconstruction and new clues about Tut's age and death.

Q4. List the advances in technology that improved forensic analysis.
→ X-ray (1968 discovery of missing breast-bone and ribs); computed tomography (CT) building 3-D virtual bodies from slice-images; portable CT machines (National Geographic Society + Siemens); teams combining radiology, forensics and anatomy.

Q5. Explain: "King Tut is one of the first mummies to be scanned — in death, as in life…"
→ In life Tut led his people as pharaoh; in death he again moves "regally ahead" — among the first of nearly 600 mummies inventoried by the Egyptian Mummy Project to be CT-scanned.

Q6. What does the ending with Orion suggest?
→ As the cold still air lies "like death itself," the constellation Orion — the soul of Osiris, god of the afterlife — stands watch above the tomb: myth and science meet, and the boy king rests watched over and at peace.

Poem Pointers ✅

P1. What do you notice about the beginning and ending of 'The Laburnum Top'?
→ Both are silent and still — the tree "subsides to empty" exactly as it began; the goldfinch's visit is a brief, vibrant interruption of silence.

P2. To what is the bird's movement compared? On what basis?
→ To a lizard — "sleek as a lizard, and alert, and abrupt": both move quickly, smoothly and silently.

P3. Why is the image of the engine evoked?
→ The tree-with-nest is the "engine of her family": her arrival starts it (chitterings, trillings), she "stokes it full" by feeding the chicks — the engine image captures the energy of family life she powers.

P4. What does "her barred face identity mask" mean?
→ The stripe-like bars on the goldfinch's face act like a mask that reveals — not hides — her identity as she peeks out from the branch-end.

P5. Who are the two voices in 'The Voice of the Rain', and what does "strange to tell" mean?
→ The poet's voice and the rain's voice; "strange to tell" = surprisingly, the soft-falling shower actually answered the poet's question.

P6. Why are the last two lines of the poem put in brackets?
→ They state a general truth beyond the rain: any song (or poem), after fulfilment, returns "duly with love" to its birth-place whether heeded or not — the bracket marks it as a universal aside applied to music and art.

8. Theme & Message 💡

  • Science completes history — from Carter's chisel to the CT scanner, each era's tools reveal truths older methods destroyed or missed.
  • Respect the dead, seek the truth — curiosity must walk with care; the "pharaoh's curse" joke reminds us to handle heritage humbly.
  • Power fades, mystery remains — the richest royal collection could not save a dynasty, nor explain a teenager's death.
  • (Laburnum Top) Interdependence and motherhood — the silent tree comes alive only with the bird; arrival brings energy, departure brings emptiness.
  • (Voice of the Rain) Selfless eternal service — rain gives life back to its origin, purified; art, like rain, returns with love to its source whether noticed or not.
