Hornbill · Class XI · Chapter 1

Ch 1 — The Portrait of a Lady (+ A Photograph)

Author: Khushwant Singh · Poem: A Photograph by Shirley Toulson · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: Real autobiographical account + memory poem

One line to remember: A grandson traces his bond with his grandmother through three phases — village closeness, city distance, university separation — ending in a prayerful death that even thousands of silent sparrows mourned; then a poem on an old photograph that freezes a dead mother's youth against the silence of loss.

1. The Grandmother's Portrait

For the twenty years the author had known her, his grandmother had simply always been old. People said she had once been young and pretty and had even had a husband, but he found this hard to believe — the very thought felt almost revolting. Her face was a criss-cross of wrinkles running from everywhere to everywhere; she was short, fat and slightly bent, yet she had stayed exactly the same age for two decades. She could never have been pretty, the author admits, but she was always beautiful: hobbled about the house in spotless white, one hand resting on her waist to balance her stoop, the other telling the beads of her rosary, silver locks scattered untidily over her pale puckered face, lips constantly moving in inaudible prayer. She was like the winter landscape in the mountains — an expanse of pure white serenity breathing peace and contentment. Even the grandfather's portrait above the mantelpiece — big turban, loose clothes, long white beard, looking at least a hundred years old — seemed like a man who could only have lots of grandchildren, never a wife or a youth of his own.

2. The Three Phases

2.1 Village Childhood — Constant Companions

His parents left him with her when they went to live in the city, and grandmother and grandson were constantly together. Every morning she woke him and got him ready for school, singing her morning prayer in a monotonous sing-song while bathing and dressing him — hoping he would learn it by heart; he listened only because he loved her voice. She handed him his wooden slate washed and plastered with yellow chalk, a tiny earthen ink-pot and a red pen tied into a bundle, and after a breakfast of a thick stale chapatti with butter and sugar, they walked to school together. The school was attached to the temple: while children sat in rows in the verandah singing the alphabet and the morning prayer in chorus under the priest, the grandmother sat inside reading the scriptures. On the way back, the village dogs met them at the temple door, growling and fighting for the stale chapattis she threw to them.

2.2 Turning Point — Summoned to the City

When his parents were comfortably settled in the city, they sent for them. This was the turning-point in their friendship. Though they still shared the same room, everything else began quietly changing — and the beautiful bond started loosening without either of them noticing it.

2.3 City School — Distance Begins

In the city he went to an English school in a motor bus, so she could not walk him there or sit beside him in a temple classroom any more. There were no street dogs in the city, so she took to feeding sparrows in the courtyard instead. As the years rolled by they saw less of each other. When he returned from school and told her English words and little things of Western science — the law of gravity, Archimedes' Principle, the world being round — she grew unhappy: she could not help him with his lessons, did not believe what the English school taught, and was distressed that there was no teaching about God and the scriptures. Then came the real shock: he announced they were being given music lessons. To her, music had lewd associations — it was the monopoly of harlots and beggars, not meant for gentlefolk. She said nothing, but her silence meant disapproval, and she rarely talked to him after that.

2.4 University — The Link Snapped

When he went up to University he was given a room of his own, and the common link of their friendship was snapped. His grandmother accepted her seclusion with resignation. She rarely left her spinning-wheel to talk to anyone: from sunrise to sunset she sat by the wheel spinning and reciting prayers. Only in the afternoon did she relax — breaking bread into little bits in the verandah while hundreds of little birds collected around her, creating a veritable bedlam of chirrupings. Some perched on her legs, some on her shoulders, some even on her head; she smiled but never shooed them away. It was the happiest half-hour of her day.

3. Goodbye, Return & Peaceful Death

3.1 Going Abroad — The Calm Goodbye and the Homecoming

When he decided to go abroad for five years, he was sure she would be upset — at her age one could never tell. But she could: she was not even sentimental. At the railway station she did not talk or show any emotion; her lips moved in prayer, her fingers stayed busy telling the beads, and silently she kissed his forehead. He cherished that moist imprint as perhaps the last sign of physical contact between them. But it was not so. After five years she met him at the station, not looking a day older, clasping him in her arms while reciting prayers — and spent her happiest moments feeding her sparrows longer than ever, with frivolous rebukes. That evening a change came over her: for the first time since he had known her, she did not pray. She collected the women of the neighbourhood, thumped the sagging skins of a dilapidated old drum for hours and sang of the home-coming of warriors, until they persuaded her to stop to avoid overstraining.

3.2 Her Peaceful Death

The next morning she was taken ill — a mild fever the doctor said would go. But she thought differently and declared her end was near: since she had omitted to pray just before the close of the last chapter of her life, she was not going to waste any more time talking to them. Ignoring all protests, she lay peacefully in bed praying and telling her beads — until her lips stopped moving and the rosary fell from her lifeless fingers. A peaceful pallor spread on her face. As is customary, the family laid her on the ground and covered her with a red shroud.

3.3 The Sparrows' Mourning

That evening, as the setting sun lit her room and verandah with a blaze of golden light, the family stopped half-way in the courtyard: thousands of sparrows sat scattered on the floor, all over the verandah and right up to where she lay dead and stiff in the red shroud. There was no chirruping. Feeling sorry for the birds, the author's mother broke bread into little crumbs, the way grandmother used to, and threw it to them — but the sparrows took no notice of the bread at all. When the corpse was carried off, they flew away quietly, and the next morning the sweeper swept the untouched crumbs into the dustbin. Nature itself observed the silence of mourning.

✒️ Poem — A Photograph (Shirley Toulson) 📷

(Separate poem printed with this chapter in Hornbill — not a continuation of the story.)

One line to remember: An old beach photograph captures the poet's mother as a happy twelve-year-old; years later she laughs at it, and after her death only silence remains — nature (the sea) outlives man, who is terribly transient.

Stanza 1

"The cardboard shows me how it was / When the two girl cousins went paddling, / Each one holding one of my mother's hands, / And she the big girl — some twelve years or so."

Explanation: The poet looks at an old cardboard photograph that shows a sea-beach scene of long ago. Two girl cousins (Betty and Dolly) are paddling — standing in shallow water — each holding one hand of the poet's mother, who being the eldest, about twelve, walks beside them like a responsible "big girl". The word cardboard (the stiff backing of the photo frame) suggests how flimsy and temporary the picture is, yet it faithfully preserves a moment from before the poet was even born.

Best-answer point: cardboard = the photograph itself; it shows the mother's childhood, which the poet can know only through the picture.

Stanza 2

"All three stood still to smile through their hair / At the uncle with the camera. A sweet face, / My mother's, that was before I was born. / And the sea, which appears to have changed less, / Washed their terribly transient feet."

Explanation: All three girls stood still, smiling through their wind-blown hair at the uncle holding the camera. The poet lovingly calls his mother's face in the photo "a sweet face" — a face from a time before he existed. Then comes the contrast at the heart of the poem: the sea has hardly changed over all these years, but the feet it washed belong to humans whose lives are terribly transient — short-lived and quickly gone. Nature is permanent; man is momentary.

Best-answer point: "transient" = short-lived; the line contrasts the changeless sea with the briefness of human life.

Stanza 3

"Some twenty — thirty — years later / She'd laugh at the snapshot. 'See Betty / And Dolly,' she'd say, 'and look how they / Dressed us for the beach.' The sea holiday / Was her past, mine is her laughter. Both wry / With the laboured ease of loss. // Now she's been dead nearly as many years / As that girl lived. And of this circumstance / There is nothing to say at all. / Its silence silences."

Explanation: Twenty–thirty years later, the grown-up mother would laugh at the same snapshot, teasing "See Betty and Dolly — look how they dressed us for the beach!" For the mother, that sea holiday had become her past; for the poet, only her laughter about it is now his past. Both feelings are wry — twisted between smile and sorrow — a "laboured ease of loss": grief accepted with effort, wearing a half-smile. In the final turn, the mother has now been dead almost as many years as the twelve-year-old girl in the photo actually lived. About death itself there is truly nothing to say — and that silence of death silences the poet completely: speech fails where loss is total.

Best-answer point: three phases — the captured moment → the mother laughing at her past → the mother dead and the poet's speechless acceptance; final line means death's silence leaves no words.

4. Exam Corner 📝✅

Long Answer (Exam-Ready)

"Describe the changing relationship between the author and his grandmother. Did their feelings for each other change?"

In his village childhood, the author and his grandmother were the best of friends. Left in her care by his city-going parents, he woke each morning to her sing-song prayers, was bathed, dressed and packed off to the temple school with his slate, ink-pot and red pen, while she sat inside reading the scriptures; on the way home they fed stale chapattis to the village dogs together.

The turning point came when his parents called them to the city. He now travelled to an English school by motor bus without her, and a triple distance grew: she could not help him with English words and Western science like gravity and Archimedes' Principle; she was pained that his education had no teaching about God and the scriptures; and she silently disapproved of the music lessons, which she associated with harlots and beggars. When he went to University and got a room of his own, the common link snapped. She accepted her seclusion with resignation, spinning at her wheel from sunrise to sunset, reciting prayers, and finding her happiest half-hour feeding the sparrows.

Yet their feelings for each other never changed — only its expression did. She saw him off abroad without a tear, kissing his forehead in silent prayer, and welcomed him back five years later looking not a day older, celebrating his homecoming by singing songs of warriors to the beat of a dusty drum. The next morning she took ill, refused to "waste time" talking, and died peacefully praying, the rosary falling from her lifeless fingers — mourned by thousands of sparrows who sat in silence and refused the bread crumbs. Distance separated their lives, but love, faith and quiet affection bound them till the end.

Expected Questions & Pointers ✅

Q1. Mention the three phases of the author's relationship with his grandmother before he left the country to study abroad.
→ ① Village childhood — constant companions; she ran his whole morning routine and attended the temple school with him. ② City English school — turning point; motor bus, English/Western science, no God in studies, music lessons — she rarely talked to him. ③ University — separate room, link snapped; she accepted seclusion with the spinning-wheel and the sparrows.

Q2. Three reasons why the grandmother was disturbed when he started going to the city school.
→ ① She couldn't help him — lessons were in English (Western science, gravity, Archimedes' Principle). ② No teaching about God and the scriptures in that education. ③ Music lessons — lewd associations; monopoly of harlots and beggars, not gentlefolk.

Q3. Three ways in which the grandmother spent her days after he grew up.
→ ① Sat by her spinning-wheel from sunrise to sunset, reciting prayers. ② Rarely talked to anyone — accepted seclusion with resignation. ③ Fed the sparrows in the afternoon — the happiest half-hour of her day.

Q4. The odd way in which the grandmother behaved just before she died.
→ Mild fever, yet declared her end was near; refused to talk to family ("not going to waste any more time"); lay peacefully praying and telling beads until the rosary fell from her lifeless fingers. (Odder still: the previous evening she had not prayed — she sang of warriors' homecoming instead.)

Q5. How did the sparrows express their sorrow when the grandmother died?
→ Thousands sat scattered silently in the verandah and room, no chirruping; ignored the bread crumbs broken the way grandmother used to; flew away quietly when the corpse was carried off; crumbs swept into the dustbin untouched next morning.

Q6. Why was it hard for the author to believe his grandmother had ever been young and pretty?
→ For twenty years she looked exactly the same — wrinkled, short, fat, slightly bent, in spotless white with rosary in hand; the thought seemed revolting, her childhood games sounded like fables of the Prophets; yet "she could never have been pretty, but she was always beautiful."

Q7. (Poem) What are the three phases depicted in the three stanzas of 'A Photograph'?
→ ① The beach photo — mother at ~12 with cousins Betty and Dolly, before the poet was born. ② Twenty–thirty years later — the mother laughing at the snapshot of her past. ③ Now — the mother dead almost as many years as the girl lived; "its silence silences."

Q8. (Poem) What does the word 'cardboard' denote, and why is it used?
→ The stiff card backing of the photo frame — it shows how flimsy and temporary the physical picture is, standing against permanent loss; the camera has captured a sweet face "that was before I was born."

6. Theme & Message 💡

  • Unconditional intergenerational love — the bond survives every phase of distance; only its form changes.
  • Tradition vs modernity — English education, Western science and music widen the gap between the scripture-reading grandmother and the modern boy.
  • Faith gives dignity — prayer structures her whole life and makes even her death calm, chosen and peaceful.
  • Nature mourns the pure — the silent sparrows' refusal of food is the truest tribute to a woman who fed them daily.
  • (Poem) Time, memory and loss — a photograph freezes the living moment, but human life is transient; ultimate loss leaves "nothing to say at all — its silence silences."
