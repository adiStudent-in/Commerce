Hornbill · Class XI · Chapter 1

Ch 1 — The Portrait of a Lady

Author: Khushwant Singh · Book: Hornbill (Class 11) · Type: Autobiographical reminiscence

1. Chapter Summary

The author begins by saying that his grandmother, like everybody's grandmother, was an old woman. She had been old and wrinkled for the twenty years that he had known her. People said she had once been young and pretty and had even had a husband, but that was hard to believe. Above the mantelpiece in the drawing room hung the portrait of his grandfather — a man in a big turban and loose-fitting clothes, with a long, white beard covering the best part of his chest, looking at least a hundred years old. He did not look the sort of person who would have a wife or children; he looked as if he could only have lots and lots of grandchildren. To the author, the thought of the grandmother being young and pretty was almost revolting, and her stories of the games she used to play as a child seemed absurd and undignified — the children treated them like the fables of the Prophets she used to tell.

She had always been short and fat and slightly bent, her face a criss-cross of wrinkles running from everywhere to everywhere. The author and his family were certain she had always been as they had known her — old, so terribly old that she could not have grown older, and had stayed at the same age for twenty years. She could never have been pretty, but she was always beautiful. She hobbled about the house in spotless white, one hand resting on her waist to balance her stoop and the other telling the beads of her rosary. Her silver locks were scattered untidily over her pale, puckered face, and her lips constantly moved in inaudible prayer. She was like the winter landscape in the mountains — an expanse of pure white serenity breathing peace and contentment.

The author and his grandmother were good friends. When his parents left him with her and went to live in the city, the two were constantly together. She used to wake him up in the morning and get him ready for school, saying her morning prayer in a monotonous sing-song while she bathed and dressed him, hoping he would listen and learn it by heart; he listened because he loved her voice but never bothered to learn it. Then she would fetch his wooden slate, already washed and plastered with yellow chalk, a tiny earthen ink-pot and a red pen, tie them all in a bundle and hand it to him. After a breakfast of a thick, stale chapatti with a little butter and sugar spread on it, they went to school — and she carried several stale chapattis with her for the village dogs. The school was attached to the temple, so she always went with him. The priest taught them the alphabet and the morning prayer; while the children sat in rows on either side of the verandah singing the alphabet or the prayer in a chorus, the grandmother sat inside reading the scriptures. When both had finished, they walked back together, and the village dogs met them at the temple door, following them home growling and fighting with each other for the chapattis thrown to them. That was the first phase of their friendship — daily, close and shared.

When his parents were comfortably settled in the city, they sent for the two of them. That was a turning-point in the friendship. Although they shared the same room, the grandmother no longer came to school with him. He now went to an English school in a motor bus. There were no dogs in the streets, so she took to feeding sparrows in the courtyard of the city house. As the years rolled by they saw less of each other. For some time she continued to wake him and get him ready for school, and when he came back she would ask what the teacher had taught him. He would tell her English words and little things of western science and learning — the law of gravity, Archimedes' Principle, the world being round. This made her unhappy. She could not help him with his lessons, she did not believe in the things they taught at the English school, and she was distressed that there was no teaching about God and the scriptures. One day he announced that they were being given music lessons. She was very disturbed: to her, music had lewd associations — it was the monopoly of harlots and beggars and not meant for gentlefolk. She said nothing, but her silence meant disapproval, and she rarely talked to him after that.

When he went up to University, he was given a room of his own, and the common link of friendship was snapped. His grandmother accepted her seclusion with resignation. She rarely left her spinning-wheel to talk to anyone. From sunrise to sunset she sat by her wheel spinning and reciting prayers, and only in the afternoon did she relax for a while to feed the sparrows. Sitting in the verandah breaking bread into little bits, she drew hundreds of little birds around her, creating a veritable bedlam of chirrupings; some came and perched on her legs, others on her shoulders, and some even sat on her head. She smiled but never shooed them away. It used to be the happiest half-hour of the day for her.

When the author decided to go abroad for further studies — he would be away for five years — he was sure she would be upset, for at her age one could never tell. But she was not even sentimental. She came to leave him at the railway station but did not talk or show any emotion; her lips moved in prayer, her mind was lost in prayer, and her fingers were busy telling the beads of her rosary. Silently she kissed his forehead, and he cherished the moist imprint as perhaps the last sign of physical contact between them. But that was not so. After five years he came back home and was met by her at the station. She did not look a day older. She still had no time for words, and while she clasped him in her arms he could hear her reciting her prayers. Even on the first day of his arrival her happiest moments were with her sparrows, whom she fed longer and with frivolous rebukes. In the evening, however, a change came over her — she did not pray. She collected the women of the neighbourhood, got an old drum and started to sing. For several hours she thumped the sagging skins of the dilapidated drum and sang of the home-coming of warriors. They had to persuade her to stop to avoid overstraining. That was the first time since he had known her that she did not pray.

The next morning she was taken ill. It was a mild fever and the doctor told them it would go, but the grandmother thought differently — she told them that her end was near. She said that since only a few hours before the close of the last chapter of her life she had omitted to pray, she was not going to waste any more time talking to them. They protested, but she ignored their protests. She lay peacefully in bed praying and telling her beads. Even before they could suspect, her lips stopped moving and the rosary fell from her lifeless fingers. A peaceful pallor spread on her face, and they knew that she was dead. They lifted her off the bed and, as is customary, laid her on the ground and covered her with a red shroud. After a few hours of mourning they left her alone to make arrangements for her funeral, and in the evening they went to her room with a crude stretcher to take her to be cremated. The sun was setting and had lit her room and verandah with a blaze of golden light. They stopped half-way in the courtyard: all over the verandah and in her room, right up to where she lay dead and stiff wrapped in the red shroud, thousands of sparrows sat scattered on the floor. There was no chirruping. They felt sorry for the birds, and the author's mother fetched some bread, broke it into little crumbs the way the grandmother used to, and threw it to them — but the sparrows took no notice of the bread. When they carried the corpse off, the sparrows flew away quietly. Next morning the sweeper swept the bread crumbs into the dustbin.

3. Theme

1. The changing relationship between the author and his grandmother.
The chapter is the story of a friendship that moves through clear phases — the close village years when they were constantly together, the city years when they shared the same room but she no longer came to school, the University years when the common link of friendship was snapped, and finally the reunion after five years. The outward closeness changes as the author grows into a world of English words, western science and music lessons that she cannot share. And yet the love itself never dies — she kisses his forehead at the station, clasps him in her arms when he returns, and her death leaves a silence that the whole house feels.

2. The generation gap between old faith and modern education.
The grandmother lives in a world of the scriptures, the morning prayer and the rosary; the author learns the law of gravity, Archimedes' Principle and the world being round. She is unhappy because she cannot help him with his lessons and is distressed that there was no teaching about God and the scriptures in the English school. The final break comes with the music lessons, which to her had lewd associations and were the monopoly of harlots and beggars. The chapter shows gently that education and old beliefs often pull a family in two directions.

3. Old age with dignity, self-discipline and deep faith.
The grandmother is the picture of an expanse of pure white serenity — spotless white clothes, a stoop balanced by one hand, lips moving in inaudible prayer. She never complains. When she loses her grandson's company she accepted her seclusion with resignation and fills her day with the spinning-wheel and prayer from sunrise to sunset. Old age here is not pitiable but beautiful: she could never have been pretty; but she was always beautiful.

4. Love that is shown through actions, not words.
The grandmother hardly ever speaks of her feelings. She shows love by waking him up, by washing his slate and plastering it with yellow chalk, by tying up his ink-pot and red pen, by feeding him stale chapatti with butter and sugar, and by her frivolous rebukes to the sparrows. At the railway station she does not talk or show emotion — she only kisses his forehead, and he treasures the moist imprint. The chapter's lesson is that the deepest affection can be completely silent.

5. Death as a peaceful fulfilment, and nature's silent mourning.
The grandmother foretells her own end, saying she is near the close of the last chapter of her life. She dies only when her lips stopped moving and the rosary fell from her lifeless fingers. The most moving proof of her goodness is not spoken by any human being: thousands of sparrows sit scattered on the floor around her body, there is no chirruping, and they take no notice of the bread offered to them — they mourn in silence and then fly away quietly.

4. Character Sketch

The Grandmother

She is the centre of the chapter and the person the title points to. When the author first knew her she had been old and wrinkled for twenty years, short and fat and slightly bent, with a face that was a criss-cross of wrinkles. She dressed always in spotless white and hobbled about the house with one hand on her waist to balance her stoop and the other telling the beads of her rosary. Her silver locks lay untidily over her pale, puckered face and her lips constantly moved in inaudible prayer. The author says she could never have been pretty, but she was always beautiful — she was like the winter landscape in the mountains, an expanse of pure white serenity breathing peace and contentment.

She is deeply religious. She says her morning prayer in a monotonous sing-song while bathing and dressing the boy, reads the scriptures inside the temple while he learns his alphabet, and after he grows up sits at her spinning-wheel from sunrise to sunset spinning and reciting prayers. She was distressed that there was no teaching about God and the scriptures at the English school, and she was disturbed by the music lessons, since to her music had lewd associations and was the monopoly of harlots and beggars, not meant for gentlefolk.

She is also full of quiet, practical love. She keeps the boy with her when his parents go to the city, wakes him, prepares his wooden slate, earthen ink-pot and red pen, and feeds him stale chapatti with butter and sugar. She carries stale chapattis for the village dogs, and in the city, where there are no dogs in the streets, she switches to feeding sparrows. She has great compassion for small creatures — she smiled but never shooed them away when sparrows sat on her legs, her shoulders and even her head, and that happiest half-hour of the day was hers.

Her greatest strength is dignity and self-control. When the author goes up to University and the common link of friendship was snapped, she does not complain — she accepted her seclusion with resignation. At the railway station, when he leaves for five years, she was not even sentimental; she does not talk or show emotion but silently kisses his forehead. After five years she does not look a day older and, clasping him in her arms, meets him while reciting her prayers. On the day before her death she does something she had never done — she does not pray, gathers the women of the neighbourhood, beats an old drum with the sagging skins of the dilapidated drum, and sings of the home-coming of warriors. Then, calm and unafraid, she says her end was near, lies peacefully in bed praying and telling her beads, and dies with a peaceful pallor spreading on her face. She is, in short, a woman strong in character — faithful, loving, self-disciplined and fearless.

The Author as a Boy and Young Man

As a small boy in the village he is his grandmother's constant companion. He loved her voice and listened to her prayer every morning, though he never bothered to learn it. He accepted her simple routine without question — the wooden slate, the yellow chalk, the earthen ink-pot, the red pen, the stale chapatti and the walk to the temple school. He also shared the family's amusement about her past: the idea that she had once been young and pretty was almost revolting to him, and her childhood games seemed absurd and undignified.

As he grows up he becomes the reason the relationship changes. He goes to an English school in a motor bus and comes home full of English words and western science — the law of gravity, Archimedes' Principle, the world being round — without realising how much it hurt her that she could not help him. When he goes to University he gets a room of his own, and the friendship is quietly broken. Yet he is a sensitive and observant narrator: he notices her silence after the music lessons, notices that she does not look a day older after five years, and cherishes the moist imprint of her kiss as perhaps the last sign of physical contact between them. It is his eyes that record the thousands of sparrows sitting silently around her body, and his memory that keeps the chapter alive.

The Author's Parents

They appear only briefly, but they set the whole story in motion. They left the author with his grandmother when they went to live in the city, making the boy and the old woman constantly together. When they were comfortably settled in the city, they sent for the two of them — and that move became a turning-point in the author's friendship with his grandmother, because in the city he joined an English school and she was left with nothing to do but feed the sparrows in the courtyard. They are not unkind — the author's mother, after the grandmother's death, fetches bread and breaks it into little crumbs exactly the way my grandmother used to and throws it to the mourning sparrows. This small act of imitation shows that the grandmother's gentle habits were noticed and remembered by the whole family.

The Grandfather (the Portrait)

He never appears alive in the chapter; he exists only as a portrait hanging above the mantelpiece in the drawing room. He wears a big turban and loose-fitting clothes, his long, white beard covers the best part of his chest, and he looks at least a hundred years old. He does not look the sort of person who would have a wife or children — he looks as if he could only have lots and lots of grandchildren. His portrait is important because it is the doorway to the chapter's first idea: that the grandmother, like the grandfather, was once young, and that fact is now impossible for the children to believe.

Q11. Would you agree that the author's grandmother was a person strong in character? Give instances from the text to support your answer. 6 marks

Answer:
Yes, the grandmother is unmistakably a person strong in character, and the text gives instance after instance. Her appearance itself speaks of quiet dignity: she hobbled about in spotless white, one hand resting on her waist to balance her stoop and the other telling the beads of her rosary, and the author calls her an expanse of pure white serenity breathing peace and contentment.

Her self-discipline is remarkable. From sunrise to sunset she sat by her spinning-wheel, spinning and reciting prayers, leaving it only for the happiest half-hour of the day with her sparrows. Her faith never wavers: she says her morning prayer in a monotonous sing-song, reads the scriptures in the temple, is distressed that there was no teaching about God and the scriptures at the English school, and disapproves so firmly of the music lessons that her silence alone conveys her judgement.

Above all she has the strength to accept loss without bitterness and death without fear. When the author goes to University and the common link of friendship was snapped, she does not protest — she accepted her seclusion with resignation. At the railway station, she was not even sentimental; she showed no emotion and simply kissed his forehead. When her end comes she announces it herself, saying that since she had omitted to pray a few hours before the close of the last chapter of her life she would not waste time talking to them. She ignores their protests, lies peacefully in bed praying and telling her beads, and dies with a peaceful pallor on her face. A woman who can live with such discipline and die with such calm is certainly strong in character.

Q12. (Value-based) What values does the life of the author's grandmother teach us? Answer with reference to the chapter. 6 marks

Answer:
The grandmother's life is a quiet lesson in several values. The first is faith and devotion: her rosary, her inaudible prayer and her reading of the scriptures fill her whole day, and even on her deathbed she lies praying and telling her beads. The second is selfless love shown through action rather than words — she wakes the author, washes his wooden slate, ties up his ink-pot and red pen, feeds him stale chapatti with butter and sugar, and shows her feelings at the railway station only through a silent kiss that leaves a moist imprint on his forehead.

The third is dignity, self-respect and patient acceptance — when the author grows up and the common link of friendship was snapped, she accepted her seclusion with resignation instead of complaining, and gave herself to her spinning-wheel and her prayers. The fourth is compassion for all living beings: she carried stale chapattis for the village dogs, and in the city she fed the sparrows, letting them sit on her legs, her shoulders and even her head, and she smiled but never shooed them away.

The fifth is courage and fearlessness in the face of death. She tells her family plainly that her end was near, ignores their protests, and dies calmly, her lips stopping only at the very end. The sixth is simplicity — spotless white clothes, plain food, and the small daily joy of the happiest half-hour of the day spent feeding birds. The most beautiful confirmation of these values is the mourning of the thousands of sparrows, who sat in complete silence around her body and took no notice of the bread offered to them. A life lived with such faith, kindness and calm is a value lesson in itself.
