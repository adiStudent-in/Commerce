Ch 5 — The Tale of Melon City

Poet: Vikram Seth · Book: Snapshots (Class 11) · Type: Narrative comic poem (after Idries Shah) satirising governance and the mob

1. Stanza-wise Explanation

Stanza 1

"In the city of which I sing
There was a just and placid King.
The King proclaimed an arch should be
Constructed, that triumphally
Would span the major thoroughfare
To edify spectators there.
The workmen went and built the thing.
They did so since he was the King.
The King rode down the thoroughfare
To edify spectators there."

Explanation: The poem opens like an old tale being sung. The poet sets the scene in a city where there lived a just and placid King — a ruler who is fair, calm and never loses his temper. This King proclaimed that an arch should be constructed, one that would triumphally span the major thoroughfare — that is, it would stretch grandly across the main road — in order to edify spectators there, meaning to impress, uplift and instruct the people who watched. The workmen simply "went and built the thing" — they had no say, no plans of their own, and no objection. The reason is stated flatly: "They did so since he was the King." Then the King himself "rode down the thoroughfare", again "To edify spectators there." Notice the quiet irony — the King builds a grand public monument for his own glory, and the poet repeats the same phrase like a refrain. Notice also that the King's proclamation is planted here early — it becomes the key that unlocks the whole chain of blame later.

One-liner: A "just and placid" King orders a grand arch over the main road for the people's edification, and the workmen build it simply because he is the King.

Stanza 2

"Under the arch he lost his crown.
The arch was built too low. A frown
Appeared upon his placid face.
The King said, 'This is a disgrace.
The chief of builders will be hanged.'
The rope and gallows were arranged.
The chief of builders was led out.
He passed the King. He gave a shout,
'O King, it was the workmen's fault'
'Oh!' said the King, and called a halt"

Explanation: The King rides under the arch — and "The arch was built too low", so "Under the arch he lost his crown." The arch does not kill him and does not even injure him; it only knocks his crown off. But that is enough to break his calm: "A frown / Appeared upon his placid face." and he declared, "'This is a disgrace. / The chief of builders will be hanged.'" His instant reaction is not repair or inquiry but punishment. The machinery of the state moves at once: "The rope and gallows were arranged." and "The chief of builders was led out." But as he "passed the King", he "gave a shout" that shifts the blame: "'O King, it was the workmen's fault'". The King's response — "'Oh!' said the King," — is almost comic in its mildness, and he "called a halt" to the hanging. The comedy of a king who thinks death is the obvious answer to a low arch begins here.

One-liner: The too-low arch knocks off the King's crown, he orders the chief of builders hanged for the "disgrace", and the builder saves himself by blaming the workmen.

Stanza 3

"To the proceedings. Being just
(And placider now) he said, 'I must
Have all the workmen hanged instead.'
The workmen looked surprised, and said,
'O King, you do not realise
The bricks were made of the wrong size.'
'Summon the masons!' said the King.
The masons stood there quivering.
'It was the architect...', they said,
The architect was summoned.
'Well, architect,' said His Majesty.
'I do ordain that you shall be
Hanged.' Said the architect, 'O King,
You have forgotten one small thing.
You made certain amendments to
The plans when I showed them to you.'"

Explanation: The King resumes the proceedings and, "Being just / (And placider now)" — the poet's sly parenthesis tells us he is calmer only because someone else has been blamed — decides, "'I must / Have all the workmen hanged instead.'" "The workmen looked surprised" and passed the blame a step lower: "'O King, you do not realise / The bricks were made of the wrong size.'" So the King calls "'Summon the masons!'", and "The masons stood there quivering" (trembling with fear). They too push the blame onward: "'It was the architect...', they said," and "The architect was summoned." "'Well, architect,' said His Majesty", before announcing, "'I do ordain that you shall be / Hanged.'" But the architect delivers the line that blows the whole case open: "'O King, / You have forgotten one small thing. / You made certain amendments to / The plans when I showed them to you.'" The blame has travelled builder → workmen → masons → architect, and now it points straight at the King himself, because he changed the plans with his own hand. This is the turning point of the poem's satire.

One-liner: Blame travels down the chain — workmen, masons, architect — until the architect points out that the King himself amended the plans.

Stanza 4

"The King heard this. The King saw red.
In fact he nearly lost his head;
But being a just and placid King
He said, 'This is a tricky thing.
I need some counsel. Bring to me
The wisest man in this country.'
The wisest man was found and brought,
Nay, carried, to the Royal Court."

Explanation: Hearing that he is himself the cause, "The King heard this. The King saw red." — he flew into a rage — and "In fact he nearly lost his head;", a deliberately double-edged phrase meaning both that he nearly lost his temper completely and that he, like others before him, nearly lost his head on the gallows. But "being a just and placid King" he controls himself (the poet repeats the opening description with heavy irony) and admits, "'This is a tricky thing. / I need some counsel. Bring to me / The wisest man in this country.'" So "The wisest man was found and brought, / Nay, carried, to the Royal Court." The word "carried" is the first hint that this famously wise man is extremely old and helpless, and the poet's correction of his own sentence ("found and brought, / Nay, carried, to the Royal Court") is part of the poem's dry humour.

One-liner: Furious at being implicated himself, the King calms down, calls the whole matter "tricky", and has the country's wisest man carried into court.

Stanza 5

"He could not walk and could not see,
So old (and therefore wise) was he —
But in a quavering voice he said,
'The culprit must be punished.
Truly, the arch it was that banged
The crown off, and it must be hanged'.
To the scaffold the arch was led
When suddenly a Councillor said —
'How can we hang so shamefully
What touched your head, Your Majesty?'
'True,' mused the King. By now the crowd,
Restless, was muttering aloud."

Explanation: The wisest man "could not walk and could not see, / So old (and therefore wise) was he" — the parenthesis mocks the common assumption that age automatically equals wisdom. In a quavering voice (trembling with age — the source's footnote explains quavering as trembling) he delivers his judgement: "'The culprit must be punished. / Truly, the arch it was that banged / The crown off, and it must be hanged'." His logic is absurd but perfectly consistent — the crown was knocked off by the arch, so the arch is the culprit, and the punishment for culprits is hanging. Accordingly, "To the scaffold the arch was led" — the source's footnote explains scaffold as a platform for the execution of criminals. At this point "a Councillor said", objecting on grounds of reverence for the throne: "'How can we hang so shamefully / What touched your head, Your Majesty?'" The King, unbothered by the absurdity and bothered only by protocol, "mused", "'True,'". Meanwhile "the crowd, / Restless, was muttering aloud." — the mob is beginning to make itself heard, and that noise will decide everything.

One-liner: The ancient "wisest man" declares the arch itself the culprit and has the arch led to the scaffold, a Councillor objects on the King's honour, and the restless crowd begins to mutter.

Stanza 6

"The King perceived their mood and trembled
And said to all who were assembled —
'Let us postpone consideration
Of finer points like guilt. The nation
Wants a hanging. Hanged must be
Someone, and that immediately.'
The noose was set up somewhat high.
Each man was measured by and by.
But only one man was so tall
He fitted. One man. That was all.
He was the King. His Majesty
Was therefore hanged by Royal Decree."

Explanation: "The King perceived their mood and trembled" — the just and placid King is now frightened of his own people. To satisfy them he abandons justice altogether: "'Let us postpone consideration / Of finer points like guilt. The nation / Wants a hanging. Hanged must be / Someone, and that immediately.'" This is the poem's sharpest stroke — the question of guilt is openly set aside as a "finer point", because what the nation (the mob) actually wants is not justice but a spectacle. So "The noose was set up somewhat high. / Each man was measured by and by." The grim joke lands in the next lines: "But only one man was so tall / He fitted. One man. That was all. / He was the King." The King's own height makes him the only possible candidate for his own gallows, and "His Majesty / Was therefore hanged by Royal Decree." The absurd chain of blame, the "justice" that hunted for a victim instead of a culprit, and the King's own proclamation about the arch, all end with the King hanging from the rope he had arranged for someone else.

One-liner: Frightened by the mob and caring only for a hanging, the King orders guilt set aside — and since he alone fits the high noose, he is hanged by Royal Decree.

Stanza 7

"'Thank Goodness we found someone,' said
The Ministers, 'for if instead
We had not, the unruly town
Might well have turned against the Crown.'
'Long live the King!' the Ministers said.
'Long live the King! The King is dead.'
They pondered the dilemma; then,
Being practical-minded men,
Sent out the heralds to proclaim
(In His [former] Majesty's name):
'The next to pass the City Gate
Will choose the ruler of our state,
As is our custom. This will be
Enforced with due ceremony.'"

Explanation: The Ministers are relieved, and they say so with brutal frankness: "'Thank Goodness we found someone,' said / The Ministers, 'for if instead / We had not, the unruly town / Might well have turned against the Crown.'" A dead King, in other words, is a useful shield against the mob. Their loyalty is entirely to the institution, not the man, and they prove it by shouting the contradictory pair in one breath — "'Long live the King!'" and "'Long live the King! The King is dead.'" The dead King is gone; "the King" as a post must live on. So "They pondered the dilemma; then, / Being practical-minded men, / Sent out the heralds to proclaim / (In His [former] Majesty's name):" that "'The next to pass the City Gate / Will choose the ruler of our state, / As is our custom. This will be / Enforced with due ceremony.'" The matter of who rules is settled by an ancient custom applied blindly, without a single thought about wisdom or ability. Satire on government could hardly be sharper.

One-liner: The Ministers, relieved to have someone else to blame, declare "The King is dead" and fall back on the old custom that whoever next passes the City Gate shall choose the ruler.

Stanza 8

"A man passed by the City Gate.
An idiot. The guards cried, 'Wait!
Who is to be the King? Decide!'
'A melon,' the idiot replied.
This was his standard answer to
All questions. (He liked melons.) 'You
Are now our King,' the Ministers said,
Crowning a melon. Then they led
(Carried) the Melon to the throne
And reverently set it down."

Explanation: "A man passed by the City Gate. / An idiot." The guards seize the moment: "'Wait! / Who is to be the King? Decide!'" His answer is "'A melon,' the idiot replied." And the poet immediately explains, in the most deadpan parenthesis in the poem, "This was his standard answer to / All questions. (He liked melons.)" The whole future of the state now rests on a man's one-track reply. The Ministers accept it without a murmur of doubt — "'You / Are now our King,'" they say, "Crowning a melon." Then "they led / (Carried) the Melon to the throne / And reverently set it down." The word "reverently" is the final touch of mockery: the state treats a fruit with the same solemn ceremony it gave a King, because the custom — not sense, not merit — has been obeyed.

One-liner: The first person through the City Gate is an idiot who answers "A melon" to every question, and the Ministers solemnly crown a melon and set it reverently on the throne.

Stanza 9

"This happened years and years ago.
When now you ask the people, 'So —
Your King appears to be a melon.
How did this happen?', they say, 'Well, on
Account of customary choice.
If His Majesty rejoice
In being a melon, that's OK
With us, for who are we to say
What he should be as long as he
Leaves us in Peace and Liberty?'
The principles of laissez faire
Seem to be well-established there."

Explanation: The poem steps back in time — "This happened years and years ago." — and turns to the present-day people of that city. When you ask them how their King came to be a melon, they reply patiently that it was "'Well, on / Account of customary choice. / If His Majesty rejoice / In being a melon, that's OK / With us, for who are we to say / What he should be as long as he / Leaves us in Peace and Liberty?'" Their one condition on a ruler is that he leave them alone. The poem closes with the formal-sounding couplet, "The principles of laissez faire / Seem to be well-established there." — laissez faire being the policy of letting things take their own course without interference. This is the final joke: the citizens, the Ministers, the King and the crowd have each simply passed the problem on, and the state has drifted into being ruled by a melon, which suits everyone because a melon asks nothing of anyone.

One-liner: Years later the people explain that a melon rules them only by "customary choice", and that they are content as long as they are left in peace and liberty — so laissez faire is well-established there.

3. Theme

Vikram Seth's The Tale of Melon City is a satire on law, governance and the mob, told in the flat, cheerful voice of an old folk tale. It begins with a just and placid King who orders an arch over the major thoroughfare "To edify spectators there", simply because he is the King and the workmen do as they are told. When the too-low arch knocks off his crown, the King's justice turns out to be nothing but the search for a neck: the chief of builders is to be hanged, then the workmen, then the masons, then the architect — a chain of blame in which each man saves himself by naming the man below him, until the architect reveals that the King himself "made certain amendments to / The plans when I showed them to you". The state, in other words, is a machine for passing responsibility downwards and never for finding the truth; the King's own proclamation starts the whole business, and no step in the blame game is ever an honest search for guilt. Panicked into consulting the wisest man in this country — who is "So old (and therefore wise) was he" that he must be carried, blind and unable to walk — the King receives the answer that the arch is the culprit and "must be hanged". When a Councillor objects that the thing which touched the King's head cannot be hanged so shamefully, the King cuts through everything with the poem's most damning line — "Let us postpone consideration / Of finer points like guilt. The nation / Wants a hanging." Here the satire names the real ruler of Melon City: mob rule. The mob does not want justice, only a spectacle, and a government that lives by appeasing the crowd must produce a victim, not a verdict — so, because "only one man was so tall / He fitted", the King is "hanged by Royal Decree." The Ministers are delighted ("Thank Goodness we found someone"), mourn and celebrate in a single breath ("Long live the King! The King is dead."), and fall back on custom — the next person to pass the City Gate shall choose the ruler — so the crown passes to an idiot whose "standard answer" is "'A melon,'" and accordingly a melon is crowned. That melon sits on the throne, and the people are content because it "Leaves us in Peace and Liberty?"; laissez faire, the poem concludes drily, "Seem to be well-established there." The message is layered and bleakly funny: kingship is hollow because its authority rests on proclamation and ceremony rather than wisdom; justice without honesty becomes persecution; custom, obeyed without thought, produces rulers nobody chose and nobody can defend; and a society that asks nothing of its government except to be left alone gets exactly the government it deserves — one that does nothing at all. The poem's weapon throughout is the calm couplet, the rhyme that makes horror sound orderly, and the gentle refrain — the very ordinariness of the language is what makes the absurdity cut.

4. Reference to Context (RTC) Questions

RTC 1

"In the city of which I sing
There was a just and placid King.
The King proclaimed an arch should be
Constructed, that triumphally
Would span the major thoroughfare
To edify spectators there." (Stanza 1)

(a) Where is the poem set, and how is its King described? (b) Why was the arch to be built, and what do the words "triumphally" and "To edify spectators there" tell us about the King's purpose? (c) Who built the arch and why, according to the poet?

Answers (a) The poem is set in the city "of which I sing" — the city that comes to be ruled by a melon. Its ruler is described as a just and placid King — apparently fair and calm, a description the poem will go on to mock. (b) The King proclaimed that an arch should be constructed to span the major thoroughfare — to stretch across the main road — so that it would do so triumphally, that is grandly and in a processional, victorious style, in order "To edify spectators there", to impress, instruct and morally uplift the onlookers. It shows that the monument was built for the King's own glory and public display, not for any practical need. (c) "The workmen went and built the thing. / They did so since he was the King." — they built it not out of conviction but out of unquestioning obedience to royal authority.

RTC 2

"Under the arch he lost his crown.
The arch was built too low. A frown
Appeared upon his placid face.
The King said, 'This is a disgrace.
The chief of builders will be hanged.'" (Stanza 2)

(a) What happened to the King under the arch, and what was the cause? (b) Why is the frown on his "placid face" significant? (c) What does his order "'This is a disgrace. / The chief of builders will be hanged.'" reveal about his idea of justice?

Answers (a) "Under the arch he lost his crown. / The arch was built too low." The arch did not kill or injure the King — it only knocked his crown off, because it had been built too low for him to pass under it comfortably. (b) The just and placid King was famous for his calm; the very fact that "A frown / Appeared upon his placid face." shows how deeply a mere knocked-off crown stung his pride. The public humiliation, not any real harm, is what upsets him. (c) It reveals a justice that is instant, angry and disproportionate. He does not ask how the arch came to be too low or order an inquiry; he pronounces "'This is a disgrace. / The chief of builders will be hanged.'" and immediately reaches for the extreme punishment. The poem's satire is that this "justice" is really wounded vanity.

RTC 3

"'I do ordain that you shall be
Hanged.' Said the architect, 'O King,
You have forgotten one small thing.
You made certain amendments to
The plans when I showed them to you.'" (Stanza 3)

(a) Trace the chain of blame that led to the architect being summoned. (b) What is the "one small thing" the architect reminds the King of? (c) Why are these lines the turning point of the poem's satire?

Answers (a) The blame moved steadily downwards and then sideways: the chief of builders was to be hanged, but he shouted that it was the workmen's fault; the workmen said "The bricks were made of the wrong size."; the King summoned the masons, who "stood there quivering" and said "It was the architect..."; so "The architect was summoned." and the King told him, "'I do ordain that you shall be / Hanged.'" (b) The architect reminds him that the King himself "made certain amendments to / The plans when I showed them to you." The man who is about to be hanged for a faulty building points out that the fault lies in his royal master's own interference with the design. (c) Until this moment the search for a culprit had been moving away from the throne. Now it points directly at it, and the King has no one left to blame — which is why he "saw red", "nearly lost his head", and had to fall back on asking for counsel. The satire turns here from who built it wrong? to who really rules and who is really answerable?

RTC 4

"He could not walk and could not see,
So old (and therefore wise) was he —
But in a quavering voice he said,
'The culprit must be punished.
Truly, the arch it was that banged
The crown off, and it must be hanged'." (Stanza 5)

(a) Who is "he", and how is he brought before the King? (b) What does the parenthesis "So old (and therefore wise) was he" suggest? (c) What is the old man's judgement, and what is illogical about it?

Answers (a) He is "The wisest man in this country", whom the King ordered to be brought to him for counsel. The poem says "The wisest man was found and brought, / Nay, carried, to the Royal Court." — he had to be "carried" because, as these lines say, "He could not walk and could not see,". (b) The parenthesis mocks the common assumption that age automatically brings wisdom. The poem treats the old man's extreme age — blind and unable to walk — as if it were proof of his wisdom, which is exactly the kind of unthinking logic that runs this state. His voice is quavering (trembling, as the source's footnote tells us), which hardly suggests clear judgement. (c) He declares, "'The culprit must be punished. / Truly, the arch it was that banged / The crown off, and it must be hanged'." His conclusion follows a childishly literal logic: the arch knocked off the crown, therefore the arch is the culprit, therefore the arch must be hanged. What is illogical is that an inanimate object cannot be a culprit, cannot be punished, and cannot be hanged — and that the real question of who designed and approved a too-low arch is quietly dropped.

RTC 5

"'Let us postpone consideration
Of finer points like guilt. The nation
Wants a hanging. Hanged must be
Someone, and that immediately.'
The noose was set up somewhat high.
Each man was measured by and by.
But only one man was so tall
He fitted. One man. That was all.
He was the King. His Majesty
Was therefore hanged by Royal Decree." (Stanza 6)

(a) Whom does the King say this to, and why does he say it? (b) What does the phrase "finer points like guilt" reveal about the King's justice? (c) How does the poem make his death the outcome of this very order?

Answers (a) He says it "to all who were assembled", after he had "perceived their mood and trembled" — that is, after noticing that "the crowd, / Restless, was muttering aloud." He speaks to satisfy the mob, having already conceded the absurdity of hanging the arch because a Councillor objected that it had touched the King's head. (b) It is the poem's most damning phrase. The King openly proposes to postpone consideration of guilt itself — to treat the question of who is actually guilty as a "finer point", a mere technicality, because "The nation / Wants a hanging." and a victim must be produced "immediately." This is not justice at all; it is the sacrifice of one person to the mood of the crowd. (c) His own order makes the hanging inevitable and then selects him. "The noose was set up somewhat high. / Each man was measured by and by.", and the grim arithmetic of the poem gives us the result: "But only one man was so tall / He fitted. One man. That was all. / He was the King." The King who insisted that someone must hang at once is the only man who fits his own noose — "His Majesty / Was therefore hanged by Royal Decree."

5. Important Questions & Answers

Q1. Who was the King of the city, and what did he proclaim? 2 marks

Answer:
The poem opens by telling us that in the city the poet sings of, "There was a just and placid King" — a ruler who was supposedly fair and calm. This King "proclaimed an arch should be / Constructed, that triumphally / Would span the major thoroughfare / To edify spectators there." — that is, he ordered a grand arch to be built across the main road, so that it would impress and morally uplift the people who saw it. "The workmen went and built the thing. / They did so since he was the King." — out of simple obedience to royal authority.

Q2. What happened when the King rode down the thoroughfare, and how did he react? 2 marks

Answer:
As the King rode down the thoroughfare, "Under the arch he lost his crown. / The arch was built too low." The arch did not kill or hurt him — it knocked his crown off. But this public humiliation shattered his calm: "A frown / Appeared upon his placid face." and he declared, "'This is a disgrace. / The chief of builders will be hanged.'" So "The rope and gallows were arranged." and the chief of builders was led out to be hanged. His reaction shows a justice that punishes instantly and out of wounded pride.

Q3. Trace the chain of blame in the poem. How does it finally turn against the King? 3 marks

Answer:
When the chief of builders was led out, he gave a shout — "'O King, it was the workmen's fault'" — and the King "called a halt" to the proceedings and ordered all the workmen hanged instead. The workmen were surprised and said, "'O King, you do not realise / The bricks were made of the wrong size.'" The King then called "'Summon the masons!'", and "The masons stood there quivering" and said, "'It was the architect...'". "The architect was summoned", and the King told him, "'I do ordain that you shall be / Hanged.'" But the architect answered, "'O King, / You have forgotten one small thing. / You made certain amendments to / The plans when I showed them to you.'" With that, the blame that had moved builder → workmen → masons → architect landed on the King himself, because the fault lay in his own changes to the plans. "The King heard this. The King saw red." and "nearly lost his head;", then admitted, "'This is a tricky thing. / I need some counsel. Bring to me / The wisest man in this country.'" and asked for counsel.

Q4. Why does the poem call the wisest man's old age proof of his wisdom, and what does his advice lead to? 3 marks

Answer:
The poem says of the wisest man, "He could not walk and could not see, / So old (and therefore wise) was he". The parenthesis is sarcastic: the state assumes that extreme age must mean extreme wisdom, even though the man is blind, helpless and has to be carried to the Royal Court. In a quavering voice he gives his verdict: "'The culprit must be punished. / Truly, the arch it was that banged / The crown off, and it must be hanged'." His advice leads to the absurd scene of "To the scaffold the arch was led" — an inanimate arch being taken to be executed. It is then that "a Councillor said", "'How can we hang so shamefully / What touched your head, Your Majesty?'" and the King "mused", "'True,'". The attack on the "wisdom" of age is part of the poem's wider satire on mindless authority.

Q5. How does the King's own order bring about his death? 4 marks

Answer:
Once the Councillor's objection made it impossible to hang the arch, and once the King "perceived their mood and trembled" at the "Restless" crowd that was "muttering aloud", the King gave up all pretence of justice. He said to "all who were assembled": "'Let us postpone consideration / Of finer points like guilt. The nation / Wants a hanging. Hanged must be / Someone, and that immediately.'" He thus openly set aside the question of guilt, because the crowd wanted a spectacle and wanted it at once. The poem then applies his own order with grim arithmetic: "The noose was set up somewhat high. / Each man was measured by and by. / But only one man was so tall / He fitted. One man. That was all." And that one man was the King himself: "He was the King. His Majesty / Was therefore hanged by Royal Decree." The ruler who insisted that somebody must hang, immediately and regardless of guilt, was the only man in the city tall enough to fit the noose he had ordered to be set up — so his own demand for a hanging executed him. The irony is complete: the King is condemned by the very logic he used to condemn others.

Q6. What do the Ministers do after the King's death, and what does their behaviour reveal? 4 marks

Answer:
The Ministers' first reaction is relief, and they say so with shocking frankness: "'Thank Goodness we found someone,' said / The Ministers, 'for if instead / We had not, the unruly town / Might well have turned against the Crown.'" Their concern is not the dead King but the safety of the institution. In the very next breath they produce the poem's famous contradiction, "'Long live the King!' the Ministers said. 'Long live the King! The King is dead.'" — loyalty to the post, complete indifference to the person. They then "pondered the dilemma" and, "Being practical-minded men,", "Sent out the heralds to proclaim / (In His [former] Majesty's name):" that "'The next to pass the City Gate / Will choose the ruler of our state, / As is our custom. This will be / Enforced with due ceremony.'" Their behaviour shows a government with no principles and no plan: it does not ask who would rule well, it simply obeys an old custom and dresses the decision up as ceremony. It is exactly this blind practicality that gives the city a melon for a King.

Q7. How does the poem use humour and irony to make its serious points? 6 marks

Answer:
Humour and irony are the poem's main weapons, and they work together throughout. The humour is largely deadpan — the poet tells an outrageous story in calm, tidy rhyming couplets, so that horror sounds orderly. When the King orders the chief of builders hanged and the builder shouts that it was the workmen's fault, the King's entire reply is "'Oh!' said the King, and called a halt" — comedy in a single syllable. When the workmen are to be hanged instead, they merely "looked surprised" and remark that "The bricks were made of the wrong size.", as though that were a sufficient defence. "The masons stood there quivering" and pass the blame to the architect, who calmly points out that the King himself "made certain amendments to / The plans".

The irony is sharper still. The King is called "a just and placid King" at the start and "Being just / (And placider now)" in the middle — "placider" precisely when he is ordering more men to be hanged, so the praised virtue is exposed as a mask for violence. "The wisest man in this country" is blind, cannot walk, and is "carried" into court, yet his verdict is that an arch — a thing of stone — is the "culprit" and "must be hanged". The King's own guilt is revealed by the architect's reminder about the plans, and then the King solves the problem by declaring that "finer points like guilt" should be postponed because "The nation / Wants a hanging.". The grimmest joke of all is the arithmetic of the gallows: "The noose was set up somewhat high. / Each man was measured by and by. / But only one man was so tall / He fitted. One man. That was all." — and that man is the King, so "His Majesty / Was therefore hanged by Royal Decree." The Ministers then supply the perfect ironic double line, "'Long live the King! The King is dead.'" Finally, a melon is crowned and set "reverently" on the throne, and the closing couplet about laissez faire turns the whole tale into a joke about a government that rules best by doing nothing.

The point of all this is serious: by making us laugh at the chain of blame, the hanging of an arch and a melon on the throne, the poem shows how easily justice becomes the search for a victim, how hollow royal authority is, and how a crowd, a Minister and a custom can together produce a ruler no one ever truly chose.

Q8. "The Tale of Melon City" has been narrated in verse form. How does this style lend charm to an ancient tale? 6 marks

Answer:
The poem is written as a narrative in rhyming verse, and it reads like an old story being sung, exactly as the opening words suggest: "In the city of which I sing / There was a just and placid King." The poet openly presents himself as a singer of a tale, and the paired rhymes carry the story forward easily from event to event — the arch is proclaimed and built, the crown is lost, the builder shouts, the masons quiver, the arch is led to the scaffold, the melon is crowned — so that the reader is carried along almost like a listener at a recitation.

This verse form is particularly well suited to the poem's comic purpose. The neat, unfailing rhyme and steady rhythm make the most shocking statements sound ordinary and reasonable: "So old (and therefore wise) was he", "Each man was measured by and by. / But only one man was so tall / He fitted. One man. That was all." The form itself becomes a joke, because the calm, orderly music of the couplets keeps pace with the growing absurdity of the events. The short, flat lines also allow the poet to drop in dry asides — "Nay, carried, to the Royal Court.", "This was his standard answer to / All questions. (He liked melons.)" — which would be clumsy in prose but land perfectly in verse.

The style also gives the tale the feel of folklore and oral tradition, which suits both its note that it is written "(After Idries Shah)" and its closing lines, where the poet speaks of what happened "years and years ago" and reports how the people still answer the question today. The repeated phrases and refrains — "To edify spectators there", "being a just and placid King", "Long live the King" — work like the repeated lines of a folk song, making the story memorable and giving the satire the weight of something long told and well known. In short, the verse form supplies the tale's charm (music, ease, memorability, the flavour of folk tradition) and simultaneously supplies the satire's sting, because its very orderliness makes the absurdity of the city stand out.

Q9. (Value-based) "for who are we to say / What he should be as long as he / Leaves us in Peace and Liberty?" What attitude to government do these words show, and what do you think a citizen's duty is in a state? 6 marks

Answer:
These words are spoken by the people of the city when they are asked how their King came to be a melon. Their full answer is "'Well, on / Account of customary choice. / If His Majesty rejoice / In being a melon, that's OK / With us, for who are we to say / What he should be as long as he / Leaves us in Peace and Liberty?'" The poem then closes with its dry summary: "The principles of laissez faire / Seem to be well-established there."

The attitude these lines show is complete indifference to how a state is governed, combined with one strong demand — that it leave them alone. These citizens do not ask whether their ruler is wise, just, capable or even human; they ask only that he not disturb their "Peace and Liberty". Their words sound tolerant, almost generous — for who are we to say / What he should be — but the tolerance is really laziness and self-interest. It is exactly this attitude that has allowed every absurdity in the poem: the King who orders hangings to protect his pride, the Ministers who proclaim a custom rather than choose a capable ruler, the crowd that "Wants a hanging.", the blind "wisest man" whose advice sends an arch to the scaffold, and finally an idiot's answer that crowns a melon. Nobody in that city ever asks who is fit to rule, so the state drifts — and drift is where it lands.

The poem's own verdict is in its closing couplet. Laissez faire may sound like a clever policy of non-interference, but here it means that no one is responsible for anything, and a fruit sits "reverently" on the throne. The value lesson is the opposite of the citizens' attitude. A citizen's duty is not blind obedience and not indifferent tolerance, but active, informed participation: to ask who is fit to govern, to insist that guilt — not the crowd's mood — decide who is punished, to question custom when custom stops making sense, and to take responsibility for the choice of rulers instead of leaving it to whoever happens to pass the City Gate. A society that takes no interest in its government, the poem suggests, should not be surprised when it is governed by a melon.
