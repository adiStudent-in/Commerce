Session 1

Health and Safety Requirements

1. Meaning of Health and Safety

In a retail store, it is the retailer's responsibility to ensure, as far as is reasonably practicable, the health and safety of workers and anyone else who could be put at risk by the business — customers, visitors, children and young people, and the general public.

Health (WHO definition): "a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity."

Safety: the state of being "safe" — the condition of being protected from harm or other non-desirable outcomes.

Risk assessment: regular risk assessments should be conducted at retail stores to identify health and safety problems and start the necessary measures to eliminate or mitigate them as far as possible.

Hazards in a retail environment are often thought to be low risk, but accidents remain common and can add significant extra costs to a business.

2. Health and Safety Requirements Laid Down by the Retailer and the Law

A sales associate is expected to identify and follow the health and safety needs laid down by the retailer and the law. The factors to keep in mind are:

# Factor What it means
1 Abiding the law The retailer must show that all safety practices are being followed in the store
2 Risk assessments Examine hazardous conditions at the workplace to identify risks and implement measures to prevent or reduce them
3 Safety readiness from expected perils As per health and safety legislation, health and safety rules must be followed
4 Ergonomics The science of matching a retail store's requirements to the retailer's capabilities. A weak person lifting very heavy boxes for hours has a higher chance of injury from poor ergonomics
5 Air quality Without proper ventilation, air collects mould, fungus, bacteria or odours. The law recommends machines that cycle fresh outdoor air and circulate it through the store
6 Visual inspection of premises Inspect the premises to ensure no hazards are visible — uneven flooring, spills and misplaced boxes
7 Crime Install surveillance cameras in different parts of the store; hiring a guard may also help monitor the store
8 Training Employees should be trained to tackle any situation that invites danger
9 Insurance The retailer must get the retail store insured

3. Encouraging Colleagues to Follow Health and Safety Norms

A health and safety programme is a process for managing the prevention of work-related injuries and diseases at the workplace.

Way What to do
Create a plan Identify hazards, take steps to minimise them, develop a safety plan and tell colleagues what is expected of them
Workplace inspection Check equipment and tools and inspect the workplace regularly; colleagues then take safety practices seriously
Training Train all employees properly and encourage them to follow health and safety practices
Supervision Ensure colleagues work properly and safely; they should know that not following instructions endangers their safety and makes them liable
Informal communication Meet staff regularly and encourage them to share health and safety ideas
Discharge responsibilities Integrate health and safety into all work processes and provide guidelines defining measures, action and responsibility, with the vision of an injury-free organisation

Session 2

Equipment and Materials

1. Risks in a Retail Store

Risk — an unforeseen situation that poses a potential threat to the workplace and its workers. If not handled properly, a risk can turn into an emergency.

Emergencies can be natural or manmade and include climbing accidents, electrocution, road and fire accidents, and machine injuries. An emergency can cause physical or environmental damage and disrupt retail business operations.

Two main types of potential risk:

Risk Cause Possible injuries
Musculoskeletal injuries (sprains and strains) Lifting, carrying, pushing or pulling heavy loads — especially when the load is too heavy, difficult to grasp or too large, the effort is too strenuous, or the worker bends and twists while handling it Muscle sprains and strains; injuries to muscles, ligaments, intervertebral discs and other back structures; injuries to soft tissues such as nerves, ligaments and tendons in wrists, arms, shoulders, neck or legs
Power tools and equipment Using power tools improperly, for example in a hardware store or a bicycle or furniture shop where the job involves assembling products Abrasions, cuts, lacerations, amputations, burns, electrocution and broken bones

Other risks that can turn into emergencies:

  • gas leaks
  • explosions
  • snake bite or poisoning
  • electrocution
  • equipment failure
  • emergencies requiring evacuation
  • hazardous substances and chemical spills
  • internal emergencies
  • serious injury events or medical emergencies
  • bomb threats
  • hostage situations or terrorism

Risk assessment is the keystone to effective management of health and safety.

2. Types of Equipment and Materials Used for Health and Safety

Equipment Purpose and details
First-aid kit The basic requirement when an injury occurs to an employee
Fire extinguishers Prevent and extinguish small fires. Fires can start from heating systems, discarded cigarettes, electric appliances, poor housekeeping or improper storage of chemicals. Quick action can stop a small fire becoming uncontrollable. Staff must be trained to use them, and they must suit the hazard: Type A for combustibles, Type B for flammable or combustible liquids, Type C for electrical equipment, Type ABC for general fires
Smoke detector Senses smoke, typically as an indicator of fire. Commercial devices signal a fire alarm control panel as part of a fire alarm system
Floor markers Visual cues such as lines, shapes and signs on floors that make a space easier to navigate. They divide spaces, highlight hazards, outline workstations and storage locations, and convey safety information. Often part of a system that includes wall signs and labels such as "Caution Wet Floor"
Personal protective equipment (PPE) Protects the user against health or safety risks at work: safety helmets, gloves, eye protection, high-visibility clothing, safety footwear, safety harnesses, and respiratory protective equipment (RPE)

3. Dealing with Risks at Retail Outlets

Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) is the area concerned with protecting the safety, health and welfare of people engaged in work or employment.

Action How to do it
Training Train employees on how to use personal protective equipment
Written certification Given as proof after formal training. It contains the name of the employee trained, date(s) of training and subject of the certification
First-aid Give the injured person immediate attention and first-aid before medical help arrives
Avoiding electrical hazards Inspect the wiring; cover all electric boxes; label all switch boxes and circuits
Avoiding musculoskeletal injuries Avoid long periods of repetitive movement; take micro-breaks and change positions; avoid awkward positions; use an adjustable chair
Avoiding lifting risks Lift smaller loads; plan and adjust weight distribution ahead of time
Avoiding slipping Wear well-fitting, non-slippery footwear; keep walkways and work areas clear of boxes and material; report any spill to the person responsible for cleaning it and place a floor marker until it is cleaned
Avoiding falling Use a stepladder rather than a crate to reach high items
Avoiding power tool risks Do not walk near a tool that is operating until it comes to a complete stop
Avoiding shoplifting and robbery Put security and surveillance equipment in place and display notices; keep the store well lit; watch anyone loitering in or around the store; keep a security guard
Safety data sheets Hand them to staff or put them on the notice board

4. Use of Equipment as per the Manufacturer's Instructions

It is mandatory that equipment is used as per the manufacturer's instructions, so that its operations are understood. Reasons:

  • The instructions provide information about the controls and about wearing protective equipment against hazards associated with the machine.
  • Reading the instructions and information can save the retailer money.

Good health and safety performance ensures an accident-free environment, which is important for the smooth and effective functioning of a retail business.

Session 3

Dealing with Accidents and Emergencies

1. Reasons for Accidents in Retail Stores

  • leaky ceilings that cause wet spots on the floor
  • narrow walkways
  • uneven floor conditions
  • unexpected fire, or accidents due to power short-circuits

Retail stores are prone to accidents, so retailers must take precautions.

2. Meaning of Emergency

Emergency — a dangerous or serious situation, such as an accident, that happens and needs immediate action. An accident may lead to an emergency, and the retailer is expected to take remedial steps.

An emergency is also described as an unplanned critical event that could cause serious injury, illness, death and property damage.

3. Emergency Conditions in a Retail Store

  • explosion
  • insect bite
  • rat menace
  • electrocution
  • slip down
  • equipment failure
  • extreme environmental conditions — summers, winters, rainy, windy, lighting
  • hazardous substances and chemical or acid spills
  • loss of power or water supply, structural collapse
  • bomb threats
  • robberies and shootings

These can arise from manmade mistakes or natural causes, and need immediate attention and remedial action from the retailer.

4. Dealing with Accidents

If an accident occurs, the incident must be recorded in the accident book, if required by the store's rules. Management may check these records to grant sick or accident pay.

Accidents can be dealt with by:

  • developing an initial plan
  • calling for help
  • contacting the doctor
  • changing the layout of the facility or store
  • updating emergency procedures

5. What to Do During an Emergency

A retailer or employee must not panic but:

  1. remain calm
  2. check for any other danger
  3. protect themselves first, and protect the injured person from fire, explosions or other hazards
  4. treat the most life-threatening problems first — bleeding or shock
  5. check for broken bones; if someone has injured their spine, do not move the person
  6. call emergency services such as the fire brigade or hospital, if required

6. Emergency Action Plan

Every retail store should have emergency response procedures based on its OHS policies, and every staff member must be aware of the plan. A hazard assessment must be conducted while developing the plan.

The plan must communicate:

# Must state
1 Procedures for emergency evacuation, including the type of evacuation and exit route assignments
2 Procedures for employees who remain to operate critical operations before they evacuate
3 Procedures to account for all employees after evacuation
4 Procedures for employees performing rescue or medical duties
5 Means of reporting fires or other emergencies
6 The name or job title of every employee who may be contacted for more information about the plan or an explanation of duties under it

7. Recognising When Evacuation Starts

Keep these points in mind while developing and following the plan:

  • conditions in mass departure
  • a clear chain of command in the store
  • an evacuation warden at many places, to assist others and account for personnel
  • specific evacuation procedures, with routes and exits posted where all employees can easily access them
  • procedures for assisting staff with disabilities
  • retail staff must be capable of recognising when to evacuate themselves

8. Following the Retailer's Evacuation Procedure

  • During an emergency, local officials order the evacuation of the premises. They may instruct employees to shut off water, gas and electricity.
  • If there is an alarm, listen attentively and follow the official orders received.
  • In other cases, a specially designated person in the store is responsible for deciding to evacuate or shut down operations.
  • Protect the health and safety of retail staff and customers.
  • In case of fire, evacuate immediately to a predetermined area.
  • Assess the situation to determine whether an emergency exists.
  • Supervise all efforts, including evacuating personnel.
  • Coordinate with emergency services such as medical and fire departments, and keep them informed.
  • Shut down plant operations directly.

Session 4

Reporting Accidents and Emergencies

1. Meaning of Reporting

Reporting and recording of accidents and emergencies are legal requirements for a retail store. A store must notify the relevant local authority of work-related deaths or certain work-related injuries.

Reporting involves:

  1. instructing employees to call police, fire or medical emergency services
  2. listing emergency phone numbers and asking employees to report all non-emergency issues as well
  3. posting emergency phone numbers on the notice board
  4. information about accidents and ill health
  5. developing solutions for potential risks

2. Legal Policies and Procedures

Retail stores must plan for emergencies, and retailers must formulate special procedures in addition to the relevant laws. Emergencies must be attended to immediately. Quick and effective action can ease the situation and reduce or avoid loss of property and life, so staff must be well trained and competent.

3. Procedure to Follow When an Accident or Emergency Occurs

# Step
1 Make first-aid available to reduce the seriousness of injury
2 Notify the accident or emergency to the store supervisor
3 Restrict entry to the danger zone to avoid harming others
4 Activate warning alarms to alert staff and customers
5 Use fire extinguishers as per their operating procedures
6 Inform concerned officials — ambulance, fire brigade and police
7 Call emergency services and guide them properly to reach the premises
8 Use emergency lighting and exit doors to reach a place of safety
9 Alert competent people who are trained to meet emergencies

4. Reporting to the Right Persons

Report to the relevant enforcing authority — employers, or in certain circumstances others who control or manage the premises and keep records — about:

  • work-related deaths
  • work-related accidents that cause injuries to retail staff
  • injuries to customers in a retail store

Q02 Discuss the ways to encourage colleagues to follow health and safety norms. ›

A health and safety programme is a process for managing the prevention of work-related injuries and diseases at the workplace. Retailers have a responsibility towards employees to ensure their health and safety. Colleagues can be encouraged in the following ways:

  2. Workplace inspection — check equipment and tools and inspect the workplace regularly; colleagues then take safety practices seriously.
  3. Training — all employees should be trained properly and encouraged to follow proper health and safety practices.
  4. Supervision — proper supervision ensures that colleagues perform their jobs properly and safely; they should understand that not following instructions endangers their safety and makes them liable.
  5. Informal communication — meet staff and colleagues regularly and encourage them to share ideas for improving safety.

Q03 Discuss the various types of risk in a retail organisation. ›

A risk is an unforeseen situation that poses a potential threat to the workplace and its workers. If not handled properly, it can turn into an emergency. The main types are:

  1. Risk of musculoskeletal injuries (sprains and strains). Lifting, carrying, pushing or pulling heavy loads can cause serious injury, particularly when the load is too heavy, difficult to grasp or too large, when the physical effort is too strenuous, or when workers bend and twist while handling it. Injuries include muscle sprains and strains, injuries to muscles, ligaments, intervertebral discs and other back structures, and injuries to soft tissues such as nerves, ligaments and tendons in the wrists, arms, shoulders, neck or legs.

Other risks that can turn into emergencies include gas leaks, explosions, snake bite or poisoning, electrocution, equipment failure, emergencies requiring evacuation, hazardous substances and chemical spills, internal emergencies, serious injury events or medical emergencies, bomb threats, and hostage situations or terrorism.

Risk assessment is the keystone to effective management of health and safety.

The unit in one line: keeping the store, the staff and the customers safe — the rules, the equipment, the emergency plan and the reporting.

Who is responsible? In a retail store it is the retailer's responsibility to ensure, as far as is reasonably practicable, the health and safety of workers and anyone else who could be put at risk by the business — customers, visitors, children and young people, and the general public.

Session 1

Health and Safety Requirements

The chapter in 12 lines

  • Health and safety is the retailer's responsibility, as far as is reasonably practicable, towards workers and everyone else at risk.
  • Health (WHO) — "a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity."
  • Safety — the state of being "safe"; the condition of being protected from harm or other non-desirable outcomes.
  • Risk assessment — regular assessments to identify health and safety problems and eliminate or mitigate them as far as possible.
  • Hazards in retail are often thought to be low risk, but accidents remain common and add significant extra costs.
  • 9 requirements: abiding the law · risk assessments · safety readiness · ergonomics · air quality · visual inspection · crime · training · insurance.
  • Ergonomics — the science of matching the store's requirements to the retailer's capabilities; a weak person lifting heavy boxes for hours faces a higher risk of injury.
  • Air quality — without ventilation, air collects mould, fungus, bacteria or odours; the law recommends machines that cycle fresh outdoor air through the store.
  • Visual inspection — check for uneven flooring, spills and misplaced boxes.
  • Crime — install surveillance cameras in different parts of the store; a guard may also help monitor it.
  • Training — employees must be trained to tackle any situation that invites danger; insurance — the retailer must get the store insured.
  • A health and safety programme is a process for managing the prevention of work-related injuries and diseases at the workplace.

Session 2

Equipment and Materials

The chapter in 12 lines

  • Risk — an unforeseen situation that poses a potential threat to the workplace and its workers.
  • If not handled properly, a risk can turn into an emergency.
  • Emergencies can be natural or manmade — climbing accidents, electrocution, road and fire accidents, machine injuries.
  • An emergency can cause physical or environmental damage and disrupt retail business operations.
  • Two main risks: musculoskeletal injuries (sprains and strains) from handling heavy loads, and power tool/equipment injuries.
  • Musculoskeletal injuries affect muscles, ligaments, intervertebral discs, back structures, and soft tissues such as nerves, ligaments and tendons in wrists, arms, shoulders, neck or legs.
  • Power tool risks cause abrasions, cuts, lacerations, amputations, burns, electrocution and broken bones.
  • Other risks: gas leaks, explosions, snake bite or poisoning, electrocution, equipment failure, evacuation, hazardous substances and chemical spills, internal emergencies, serious injury/medical emergencies, bomb threats, hostage situations or terrorism.
  • Risk assessment is the keystone to effective management of health and safety.
  • 5 equipment/materials: first-aid kit, fire extinguishers, smoke detector, floor markers, PPE.
  • Fire extinguishers: Type A combustibles · Type B flammable/combustible liquids · Type C electrical equipment · Type ABC general fires.
  • OHS — protecting the safety, health and welfare of people engaged in work or employment; equipment must be used as per the manufacturer's instructions.

Use of equipment as per the manufacturer's instructions

It is mandatory so that its operations are understood. Two reasons:

  1. The instructions provide information about the controls and about wearing protective equipment against hazards associated with the machine.
  2. Reading the instructions and information can save the retailer money.

Session 3

Dealing with Accidents and Emergencies

The chapter in 12 lines

  • Reasons for accidents: leaky ceilings causing wet spots · narrow walkways · uneven floor conditions · unexpected fire or accidents due to power short-circuits.
  • Retail stores are prone to accidents, so retailers must take precautions.
  • Emergency — a dangerous or serious situation, such as an accident, that happens and needs immediate action.
  • An accident may lead to an emergency, and the retailer is expected to take remedial steps.
  • An emergency is also an unplanned critical event that could cause serious injury, illness, death and property damage.
  • Emergency conditions: explosion · insect bite · rat menace · electrocution · slip down · equipment failure · extreme environmental conditions · hazardous substances and chemical/acid spills · loss of power or water supply/structural collapse · bomb threats · robberies and shootings.
  • These arise from manmade mistakes or natural causes and need immediate attention and remedial action.
  • If an accident occurs, record it in the accident book if the store's rules require it; management may check records to grant sick or accident pay.
  • Accidents can be dealt with by an initial plan, calling for help, contacting the doctor, changing the layout, and updating emergency procedures.
  • During an emergency: remain calm, check for danger, protect yourself and the injured person, treat bleeding or shock first, check for broken bones, do not move a spinal-injury victim, call emergency services.
  • Every store needs an emergency action plan based on its OHS policies; every staff member must know it, and a hazard assessment must be done while developing it.
  • During evacuation, local officials order it; staff must listen to alarms, follow official orders, and a specially designated person decides to evacuate or shut down operations in other cases.

Following the retailer's evacuation procedure

  • Local officials order the evacuation of the premises; they may instruct employees to shut off water, gas and electricity.
  • If there is an alarm, listen attentively and follow the official orders received.
  • In other cases, a specially designated person in the store is responsible for deciding to evacuate or shut down operations.
  • Protect the health and safety of retail staff and customers.
  • In case of fire, evacuate immediately to a predetermined area.
  • Assess the situation to determine whether an emergency exists.
  • Supervise all efforts, including evacuating personnel.
  • Coordinate with emergency services such as medical and fire departments, and keep them informed.
  • Shut down plant operations directly.
