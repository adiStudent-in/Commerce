# Chapter 10 — International Business

## Meaning and the three trade types
- International business means business activities that take place across national frontiers
- It is much broader than international trade, which is only the export and import of goods and services
- It also includes foreign production, marketing, investment, technology and personnel
- Domestic business takes place within the geographical boundaries of one nation
- Three trade types: export, import and entrepot or re-export
- Export means selling goods or services to foreign countries
- Import means buying goods or services from foreign countries
- Entrepot means importing goods not for home consumption but for re-export to another country
- Test: goods enter the home country only to leave again

## Nature and scope
- Two features of the nature: involvement of two countries and payment in foreign currency
- Distance does not matter; the national border does
- Scope has four forms: merchandise trade, service trade, licensing and franchising, foreign investment
- Merchandise trade is trade in tangible goods and excludes services
- Service trade is trade in intangibles and is called invisible trade
- Licensing lets a foreign party use your trademark, patent or technology for a fee
- Franchising is licensing applied to service businesses
- Foreign investment is direct (FDI) or portfolio
- FDI gives a controlling interest and involves production or marketing abroad
- Portfolio investment is in shares, bonds, bills, notes or loans, without managing production

## Five problems of international business
- Legal procedures: lengthy and complicated; prior government permission is needed
- Restrictions: export or import licence is required for several items
- High risk: long distance, damage in transit and currency fluctuation
- Marine insurance is taken to cover the perils of the sea
- Different languages: price lists and terms must be read and understood in a foreign language
- Arrangement of foreign currency: the importer must arrange foreign exchange through a lengthy procedure
- Memory hook: L-R-R-L-F — Legal, Restrictions, Risk, Language, Foreign currency

## Reasons and benefits
- Five reasons: countries cannot produce everything equally well or cheaply; unequal natural resources; different factor availability; different labour productivity and cost; no country is best at everything
- Geographical specialisation explains trade: West Bengal in jute, Maharashtra in cotton textiles
- Labour-abundant developing countries export garments and import textile machinery
- Four benefits to nations: foreign exchange, efficient resources, growth and employment, higher standard of living
- Five benefits to firms: higher profit, capacity utilisation, growth, escape from domestic competition, improved vision
- Growth of the nation means output and jobs; growth of the firm means escaping a saturated market
- Textbook example: Singapore, South Korea and China followed “export and flourish”

## Objectives of export and import trade
- Seven export objectives: sell surplus, better use of resources, earn foreign exchange, raise national income, generate employment, increase government revenue, build international understanding
- Export revenue comes through customs duty, corporate tax and excise duty
- Five import objectives: speed up industrialisation, meet consumer demand, improve standard of living, overcome natural calamities, ensure national defence
- Memory hooks: S-U-F-N-E-R-U for exports; I-C-S-N-D for imports

## Internal vs external trade: ten bases
- Meaning: within the country vs beyond the country
- Countries involved: one vs a minimum of two
- Risk: lower vs higher
- Currency: home currency only vs foreign currency only
- Procedure: no long formalities vs long procedures and many formalities
- Mode of payment: cash or cheque vs bill of exchange or banks
- Legal rules: national laws vs international rules
- Transport: road and railway vs sea and air
- Cost: lower vs higher because of long distances
- Foreign reserves: no effect vs direct impact

## International vs domestic business: eight bases
- Nationality of buyers and sellers: same country vs different countries
- Nationality of other stakeholders: same country vs different nations and values
- Mobility of factors: labour and capital more mobile at home, less mobile across borders
- Customer heterogeneity: domestic markets more homogeneous; foreign markets differ in language, tastes and customs
- Business systems and practices: more homogeneous at home, varied abroad
- Political system and risks: one country vs different systems and degrees of risk
- Business regulations and policies: one country’s rules vs multiple countries’ laws, tariffs and quotas
- Currency: domestic currency vs more than one currency, with fluctuating exchange rates
- Examples: China bicycles vs Japan bikes; India right-hand drive vs US left-hand drive
- Americans change durables in two to three years; Indians use them until worn out

## Exporting, importing and contract manufacturing
- Exporting sends goods and services from home to a foreign country; importing brings foreign products home
- Two routes: direct (firm handles buyers and formalities) and indirect (middlemen such as export houses)
- Advantages: easiest mode, less time and money, nil or low foreign investment risk
- Limitations: packaging, transport, insurance and customs costs; import restrictions; little contact with the foreign market
- Contract manufacturing: local manufacturers abroad produce components or goods to the firm’s specifications
- It is also called outsourcing; three forms are components, assembly and complete manufacture
- Examples: Nike, Reebok, Levis and Wrangler; Godrej makes Dettol soap for Reckitt and Colman
- Advantages: no plant needed, low investment risk, lower cost, local capacity used, local firm enters international business
- Limitations: quality and design risk, local firm loses process control, restricted sale at predetermined prices

## Licensing, franchising and joint ventures
- Licensing: licensor grants patents, trade secrets or technology to a licensee for a royalty
- Franchising: licensing for services, with stricter operating rules; parties are franchiser and franchisee
- Licensing is for goods; franchising is for services and is more stringent
- Cross-licensing is mutual exchange of technology or patents
- Advantages: little or no investment abroad, no share in losses, lower political risk, local market knowledge, legal exclusivity
- Limitations: licensee may become a competitor, trade secrets may leak, conflicts and litigation
- Joint venture: a firm jointly owned by two or more otherwise independent firms
- Three ways: foreign investor buys into a local firm, local firm buys into a foreign firm, both form a new firm
- Advantages: shared equity, large projects possible, local partner’s knowledge, shared costs and risks
- Limitations: technology disclosure and conflict over dual ownership

## Wholly owned subsidiaries and mode choice
- Wholly owned subsidiary: parent invests 100 per cent in equity and gets full control
- Two ways: greenfield venture (new firm) or acquisition (buy an established firm)
- Advantages: full control and no need to disclose technology or trade secrets
- Limitations: heavy investment, parent bears all losses, higher political risk
- Not suitable for small and medium firms without enough funds
- Mode choice turns on control, investment, risk and import restrictions
- Exporting is easiest; contract manufacturing avoids a plant; licensing avoids investment but risks the brand
- A joint venture shares control and risk; a wholly owned subsidiary buys control with full investment

## Export procedure: seventeen steps
- 1 Enquiry and quotations: exporter replies with a proforma invoice
- 2 Order or indent: buyer places the order
- 3 Creditworthiness: exporter checks the importer and demands a letter of credit
- 4 Export licence: IEC, RCMC and ECGC registration
- 5 Pre-shipment finance: funds for raw material, processing, packing and transport to port
- 6 Production or procurement of goods to the buyer’s specifications
- 7 Pre-shipment inspection and inspection certificate
- 8 Excise or GST clearance; refund of excise duty is duty drawback
- 9 Certificate of origin from the trade consulate
- 10 Reservation of shipping space; shipping company issues a shipping order
- 11 Packing and forwarding; railway receipt is a title to the goods
- 12 Insurance of goods; marine insurance policy
- 13 Customs clearance; shipping bill in five copies, then carting order
- 14 Mate’s receipt from the ship’s commanding officer
- 15 Freight paid; bill of lading issued; airway bill for air cargo
- 16 Commercial invoice prepared and attested by customs
- 17 Securing payment through the bank against a bill of exchange
- Mnemonic: Eesha Ordered Idli, Old Papa Prepared Pizza; Evening Chintu Roasted Peanuts, Ishan Cooked Momos, Pizza, Ice-tea, Samosa

## Inspection and import procedure
- Three inspection methods: consignment-wise, in-process quality control, self-certification
- Consignment-wise checks each lot of finished goods after they are ready
- In-process checks raw material, technology and machinery during production
- Self-certification relies on the firm’s own quality assurance and testing; ISO 9000 series
- Inspection is compulsory only for notified products under the Export (Quality Control and Inspection) Act, 1963
- Exempt: star trading houses, trading houses, export houses, EPZ/SEZ units and 100 per cent EOUs
- Ten import steps: trade enquiry, import licence, foreign exchange, indent, letter of credit
- Then finance, shipment advice, retirement of documents, arrival of goods, customs clearance and release
- Bill of entry is prepared in triplicate; import general manifest is the basis for unloading
- Retirement means accepting the bill of exchange to get the import documents
- Sight draft is documents against payment; usance draft is documents against acceptance

## Documents
- Principal export documents (5): commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of inspection, certificate of origin
- Auxiliary export documents (4): shipping order, mate’s receipt, proforma invoice, shipping bill
- By function — goods: export invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, certificate of inspection
- By function — shipment: mate’s receipt, shipping bill, bill of lading, airway bill, marine insurance policy, cart ticket
- By function — payment: letter of credit, bill of exchange, bank certificate of payment
- Bill of lading: receipt, undertaking to carry and document of title, transferable by endorsement and delivery
- Bill of entry: import customs form, in triplicate, used to assess duty
- Shipping bill is for export clearance; bill of entry is for import clearance
- Proforma invoice is a quotation; commercial invoice is the final bill
- Letter of credit is the importer’s bank’s guarantee of payment to the exporter’s bank

## WTO and numbers to remember
- WTO is the only global international organisation dealing with the rules of trade between nations
- Established on 1 January 1995
- Five features: global trade rules, contracts signed by governments, trade liberalisation, neutral dispute settlement, smooth and free trade
- Five objectives: international peace, dispute settlement, smooth trade through common rules, growth of developing countries, quality products and higher standard of living
- WTO deals with trade rules; IMF with balance of payments and exchange stability; World Bank with development finance
- Counts: 3 trade types, 4 scope forms, 5 problems, 5 reasons, 4 national benefits, 5 firm benefits
- Counts: 7 export objectives, 5 import objectives, 10 + 8 comparison bases, 5 entry modes
- Counts: 17 export steps, 3 inspection methods, 10 import steps, 5 principal + 4 auxiliary documents
- Traps: international business is wider than trade; entrepot is not ordinary import
- Traps: pre-shipment inspection is not universal; ISO 9000, not ISO 2000
- Traps: sight = payment; usance = acceptance; joint venture shares, wholly owned subsidiary controls
