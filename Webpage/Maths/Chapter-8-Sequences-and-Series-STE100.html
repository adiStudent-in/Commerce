Sequences and Series

Class 11 Mathematics — Easy Revision Notes (STE100) · CBSE 2026-27

Big picture. A sequence is a list of numbers in a fixed order. A series is that list joined by + signs. Most of this chapter is about one special list — the Geometric Progression (GP). In a GP, you multiply by the same number at every step. You also meet two "middle values" — the Arithmetic Mean (A.M.) and the Geometric Mean (G.M.) — and one famous rule: A ≥ G.
How to use these notes. Read one section at a time. Each section follows the same order: Definition → Why it works → Worked example → CHECK. Do the CHECK before you move on. Section 10 is the formula wall. Section 11 lists the traps. Section 13 has the full practice bank with step-by-step solutions.

3. 🔢 Sequence — a list in a fixed order

Definition. A sequence is an arrangement of numbers in a definite order. The numbers in the sequence are its terms:

a1, a2, a3, …, an, …
  • a1 = first term, a2 = second term, and so on.
  • an = the nth term. It is also called the general term. Put any value of n into an and you get that term.
  • Order matters. The sequence 1, 3, 5, 7 is not the same as 7, 5, 3, 1.

Why it works this way. A sequence is really a function. The input is a natural number n = 1, 2, 3, … The output is the term an. So "sequence" and "function on N" are two views of the same thing.

3.1 Finite and infinite sequences

TypeMeaningExample
FiniteStops after a fixed number of terms2, 4, 6, 8 — 4 terms only
InfiniteNever stops2, 4, 6, 8, …

3.2 The three ways to give a sequence

Way 1 — A formula for an. This way is the most common in exams.

  • Even numbers: an = 2n ⇒ 2, 4, 6, 8, …
  • Odd numbers: an = 2n − 1 ⇒ 1, 3, 5, 7, …

Way 2 — A recurrence rule. Each term is built from the earlier terms. The rule gives the first term, then all later terms.

The Fibonacci sequence:

a1 = a2 = 1, an = an−1 + an−2 (n > 2)

This gives 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, …

Way 3 — Words only. Some sequences have no formula.

  • Prime numbers: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, … No single formula gives all prime numbers.

4. ➕ Series and sigma notation

Definition. A series is the expression you get when you join the terms of a sequence with + signs:

a1 + a2 + a3 + … + an

The sum of the series is the single number you get after adding the terms. Write it as Sn (the sum of the first n terms).

Exam point (NCERT remark). The series is the indicated sum. The "sum of the series" is the number. These are two different things. Write this difference clearly if the question asks about it.

4.1 Sigma notation

Sigma notation is a short way to write a long sum:

a1 + a2 + a3 + … + an = Σk=1n ak

Read Σk=1n ak like this: "start at k = 1. Put k = 1, 2, 3, …, n into ak. Add all the values."

  • k is only a counter. Any letter works.
  • Σ shows the process of adding. It is not the answer by itself.
  • Example: 2 + 4 + 6 + … + 2n = Σk=1n 2k
  • Example: 1 + (1)/(2) + (1)/(4) + … = Σk=1∞ (1)/(2k−1)

4.2 Worked examples

Example 4. Expand and evaluate Σk=14 (2k+1).

  • Put k = 1, 2, 3, 4: the terms are 3, 5, 7, 9.
  • Add: 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 24

✅ Σk=14(2k+1) = 24.

Example 5 (the pairing trick). Find 1 + 2 + 3 + … + 100.

  • Pair the numbers from the two ends: (1+100), (2+99), (3+98), …
  • Each pair adds to 101.
  • There are 50 pairs.
  • 50 × 101 = 5050

✅ Sum = 5050. This is the famous Gauss trick. Use it when no formula fits.

Example 6 (a pattern sum). Find 1 + 3 + 5 + … + 199.

  • The terms are odd numbers from 1 to 199. Count them: 1, 3, 5, …, 199 has 100 terms.
  • Pair the ends: 1 + 199 = 200. There are 50 pairs.
  • 50 × 200 = 10000

✅ Sum = 10000.

4.3 Find an from Sn

Sometimes the question gives you Sn and asks for the nth term. Use this rule:

an = Sn − Sn−1 (n ≥ 2), a1 = S1

Why it works. Sn is the total of the first n terms. Sn−1 is the total without the last one. Subtract and only the last term remains.

Example 7. Sn = 2n − 1. Find an.

  • an = Sn − Sn−1 = (2n − 1) − (2n−1 − 1) = 2n − 2n−1
  • Factor out 2n−1: an = 2n−1(2 − 1) = 2n−1
  • Check: a1 = S1 = 21 − 1 = 1 = 20 ✓

✅ an = 2n−1. The sequence is 1, 2, 4, 8, … — a GP with r = 2.

5. ✖️ Geometric Progression (GP) — the general term

Definition. A GP is a sequence in which each term comes from the previous term by multiplying a fixed number r. The number r is the common ratio:

r = (a2)/(a1) = (a3)/(a2) = …, r ≠ 0, a ≠ 0

Standard forms:

  • finite GP: a, ar, ar2, …, arn−1 (n terms)
  • infinite GP: a, ar, ar2, …

Ratio test — is it a GP? Divide each term by the term before it. If every answer is the same number, it is a GP.

5.1 The general term

an = a rn−1

Why the power is n−1 and not n. The first term must be a · r0 = a. So the power starts at 0. This off-by-one is the most common silly mistake in the chapter.

5.2 Worked examples

Example 8. Find the 6th term of 2, 6, 18, …

  • a = 2, r = (6)/(2) = 3 (check: (18)/(6) = 3 ✓)
  • a6 = 2 × 35 = 2 × 243 = 486

✅ a6 = 486.

Example 9 (two terms known). The 3rd term of a GP is 24 and the 6th term is 192. Find the 10th term.

  • Write the given facts as equations:
ar2 = 24 … (1)
ar5 = 192 … (2)
  • Divide (2) by (1). The a cancels: r3 = (192)/(24) = 8, so r = 2.
  • Put r = 2 in (1): 4a = 24, so a = 6.
  • a10 = 6 × 29 = 6 × 512 = 3072

✅ a10 = 3072.

Teacher's tip. In "two terms known" questions, always divide the bigger equation by the smaller one. The a cancels and r alone remains.

Example 10 (which term equals a given value?). Which term of 2, 8, 32, … is 131072?

  • a = 2, r = 4. Set up the equation: 2 · 4n−1 = 131072
  • 4n−1 = 65536 = 216 = 48
  • So n − 1 = 8, which gives n = 9

✅ 131072 is the 9th term.

Example 11 (negative r). Find the 7th term of 3, −6, 12, −24, …

  • r = (−6)/(3) = −2 (check: (12)/(−6) = −2 ✓)
  • a7 = 3 × (−2)6 = 3 × 64 = 192

✅ a7 = 192. With negative r, the signs alternate: +,−,+,−,…

5.3 GP facts worth remembering

  • Multiply or divide every term of a GP by the same non-zero number. The result is again a GP with the same r.
  • The reciprocals (1)/(a1), (1)/(a2), … of a GP form a GP.
  • The term-by-term product (or quotient) of two GPs is a GP.
  • Mirror pairs multiply to the same value: a1 an = a2 an−1 = … = a2 rn−1.

CHECK 5. For the GP 3, 6, 12, …: find r, then a8, then find which term equals 768.

Show answer
  • r = (6)/(3) = 2
  • a8 = 3 × 27 = 3 × 128 = 384
  • Solve 3 · 2n−1 = 768 ⇒ 2n−1 = 256 = 28 ⇒ n − 1 = 8 ⇒ n = 9

768 is the 9th term.

6. 🧮 GP — sum of the first n terms

6.1 The derivation — learn this once

Let Sn be the sum of the first n terms of a GP:

Sn = a + ar + ar2 + … + arn−1
  1. Multiply every term by r:
rSn = ar + ar2 + … + arn−1 + arn
  1. Subtract the new line from the first line. All the middle terms cancel. This is the magic of the derivation:
Sn − rSn = a − arn
  1. Take Sn common on the left:
(1 − r)Sn = a(1 − rn)
  1. Divide both sides by (1−r), which is allowed only when r ≠ 1:
Sn = (a(1−rn))/(1−r)

6.2 The formulas

CaseFormulaWhen to use it
r ≠ 1Sn = (a(1−rn))/(1−r)Default form
r ≠ 1Sn = (a(rn−1))/(r−1)The same formula. Use it when r > 1, so the top and bottom stay positive
r = 1Sn = naEvery term is a. The fraction forms would divide by zero here

Extra form (useful when the last term is known). If the last (nth) term is l, then l = arn−1 and

Sn = (a − lr)/(1−r) (r ≠ 1)

6.3 Worked examples

Example 12. Find 1 + 2 + 4 + … + 512.

  • a = 1, r = 2. The last term is 512 = 29, so n − 1 = 9 and n = 10.
  • Use the r > 1 form: S10 = (210 − 1)/(2 − 1) = 1024 − 1 = 1023

✅ S10 = 1023.

Example 13. Find the sum of the first 10 terms of 5 + 25 + 125 + …

  • a = 5, r = 5, n = 10
  • S10 = (5(510 − 1))/(5 − 1) = (5 × 9765624)/(4) = 12207030

✅ S10 = 12207030.

Example 14. How many terms of the GP 3, (3)/(2), (3)/(4), … add up to (3069)/(512)?

  • a = 3, r = (1)/(2). Use Sn = (a(1−rn))/(1−r):
(3(1 − ((1)/(2))n))/(1 − (1)/(2)) = (3069)/(512) ⇒ 6(1 − (1)/(2n)) = (3069)/(512)
  • Divide by 6: 1 − (1)/(2n) = (3069)/(3072) = (1023)/(1024)
  • So (1)/(2n) = 1 − (1023)/(1024) = (1)/(1024) = (1)/(210)
  • Therefore n = 10.

✅ 10 terms are needed.

Example 15 (r = 1 case). Find 7 + 7 + 7 + … up to 12 terms.

  • Every term is 7, so a = 7, r = 1, n = 12.
  • S12 = na = 12 × 7 = 84

✅ S12 = 84. Never use the fraction formula when r = 1. It divides by zero.

Example 16 (word problem — depreciation). A machine costs ₹1250. Its value drops by 20% every year. Find its value after 5 years.

  • After each year, the value becomes 80\% of the value one year before. So the values form a GP with a = 1250 and r = 0.8.
  • Value after 5 years: a6 = 1250 × (0.8)5 = 1250 × 0.32768
  • = 409.6

✅ The machine is worth ₹409.60 after 5 years. Teacher's tip: pick a as the value at the start (day 0, year 0). Then the value after k years is a rk.

Example 17 (word problem — ancestors). A person has 2 parents, 4 grandparents, 8 great-grandparents, and so on. Find the number of ancestors in the ten generations above the person.

  • The numbers form a GP: 2, 4, 8, … with a = 2, r = 2, n = 10.
  • S10 = (2(210 − 1))/(2 − 1) = 2(1024 − 1) = 2046

✅ The person has 2046 ancestors in those ten generations.

Example 18 (sum with known last term). Find 2 + 6 + 18 + … + 4374.

  • a = 2, r = 3, last term l = 4374. Check l: 2 · 3n−1 = 4374 ⇒ 3n−1 = 2187 = 37 ⇒ n = 8 ✓
  • Use the last-term form:
Sn = (a − lr)/(1−r) = (2 − 4374 × 3)/(1 − 3) = (2 − 13122)/(−2) = (−13120)/(−2) = 6560
  • Quick check with the standard form: S8 = (2(38 − 1))/(2) = 38 − 1 = 6560 ✓

✅ S = 6560.

CHECK 6. (a) Find the sum of the first 8 terms of 1 + 3 + 9 + … (b) Find n if 2 + 4 + 8 + … up to n terms = 2046.

Show answer

(a) a = 1, r = 3, n = 8:

S8 = (38 − 1)/(3 − 1) = (6561 − 1)/(2) = 3280

(b) a = 2, r = 2: (2(2n − 1))/(2 − 1) = 2046 ⇒ 2n − 1 = 1023 ⇒ 2n = 1024 = 210 ⇒ n = 10.

7. ♾️ Infinite GP — the sum to infinity

7.1 The idea

Look at the finite-sum formula: Sn = (a(1−rn))/(1−r).

  • If | r | < 1, then the powers rn become tiny as n grows: (0.5)10 ≈ 0.001, (0.5)20 ≈ 0.000001, and so on.
  • So for very large n, rn ≈ 0. The formula settles down to one fixed number.
  • If | r | ≥ 1, the terms do not die out. The sum grows without bound or keeps flipping. There is no sum to infinity in this chapter.

7.2 The formula

S∞ = (a)/(1−r), valid only when | r | < 1
Teacher's tip. Always check | r | < 1 before you use the formula. Write the check in your answer. It is often a separate marking point.

7.3 Worked examples

Example 19. Find 1 + (1)/(2) + (1)/(4) + …

  • a = 1, r = (1)/(2). Check: | r | = 0.5 < 1 ✓
  • S∞ = (1)/(1 − (1)/(2)) = (1)/((1)/(2)) = 2

✅ S∞ = 2.

Example 20. Find 3 + 1 + (1)/(3) + …

  • a = 3, r = (1)/(3). Check: | r | < 1 ✓
  • S∞ = (3)/(1 − (1)/(3)) = (3)/((2)/(3)) = (9)/(2)

✅ S∞ = (9)/(2).

Example 21 (negative r, still valid). Find 1 − (1)/(3) + (1)/(9) − …

  • a = 1, r = −(1)/(3). Check: | −(1)/(3) | = (1)/(3) < 1 ✓
  • S∞ = (1)/(1 − (−(1)/(3))) = (1)/(1 + (1)/(3)) = (3)/(4)

✅ S∞ = (3)/(4).

Example 22 (repeating decimals). Write 0.6̄ = 0.666… as a rational number.

  • Split the decimal: 0.6̄ = 0.6 + 0.06 + 0.006 + …
  • This is an infinite GP with a = 0.6 and r = 0.1. Check: | r | < 1 ✓
  • S∞ = (0.6)/(1 − 0.1) = (0.6)/(0.9) = (2)/(3)

✅ 0.6̄ = (2)/(3).

Example 23 (reverse problem). The sum to infinity of a GP is 8 and its second term is 2. Find the series.

  • Let the first term be a and the ratio be r. Second term: ar = 2, so a = (2)/(r).
  • Sum to infinity: (a)/(1−r) = 8. Substitute a:
(2)/(r(1−r)) = 8 ⇒ 2 = 8r(1−r) ⇒ 4r2 − 4r + 1 = 0
  • Solve: (2r − 1)2 = 0, so r = (1)/(2) (a repeated root).
  • Then a = (2)/((1)/(2)) = 4. Check: | r | < 1 ✓, and (4)/(1 − (1)/(2)) = 8 ✓

✅ Series: 4 + 2 + 1 + (1)/(2) + …

Example 24 (trap). Find 5 − 5 + 5 − 5 + …

  • a = 5 and r = −1. The condition | r | < 1 fails.
  • The partial sums jump between 5 and 0 forever. No fixed total exists.

✅ No sum to infinity. This is exactly why you must check the condition first.

CHECK 7. (a) Find the sum to infinity of 6 + 3 + (3)/(2) + … (b) Find S∞ of 2 − (2)/(3) + (2)/(9) − …

Show answer

(a) a = 6, r = (1)/(2), | r | < 1 ✓. S∞ = (6)/(1 − (1)/(2)) = 12.

(b) a = 2, r = −(1)/(3), | r | < 1 ✓. S∞ = (2)/(1 + (1)/(3)) = (2)/((4)/(3)) = (3)/(2).

8. 📐 Geometric Mean (G.M.)

8.1 Single G.M.

If three numbers a, G, b are in GP, then G is the geometric mean of a and b:

(G)/(a) = (b)/(G) ⇒ G2 = ab ⇒ G = √(ab)
  • For positive a and b, NCERT takes the positive root: G = √(ab).
  • Worked: G.M. of 4 and 16 = √(4 × 16) = √(64) = 8. Check: 4, 8, 16 has ratio 2 ✓
  • Worked: G.M. of 0.04 and 0.09 = √(0.0036) = 0.06. Check: (0.06)/(0.04) = (0.09)/(0.06) = 1.5 ✓

8.2 Inserting n geometric means between a and b

You want a, G1, G2, …, Gn, b to form a GP. Count the terms: b is term number n+2. So

b = a rn+1 ⇒ r = ((b)/(a))(1)/(n+1)
Gk = a rk (k = 1, 2, …, n)

Worked (a). Insert 3 numbers between 1 and 256 so that the result is a GP.

  • Here n = 3 and b is the 5th term: 1 · r4 = 256 ⇒ r4 = 256 = 44
  • Real roots: r = 4 or r = −4.
  • With r = 4: the numbers are 4, 16, 64. With r = −4: the numbers are −4, 16, −64.

✅ Both answers are valid: 1, 4, 16, 64, 256 or 1, −4, 16, −64, 256.

Worked (b). Insert two numbers between 3 and 81 so that the result is a GP.

  • n = 2 and b is the 4th term: 3 r3 = 81 ⇒ r3 = 27 ⇒ r = 3
  • Numbers: 3 × 3 = 9, then 9 × 3 = 27. Check: 3, 9, 27, 81 ✓

✅ The numbers are 9 and 27. (With r = −3: −9 and 27.)

8.3 A handy product rule

The product of the n G.M.s inserted between a and b is

G1 G2 ·s Gn = (ab)n/2

Quick check: insert 2 numbers between 2 and 16: r3 = 8 ⇒ r = 2, so the numbers are 4 and 8. Product = 32. Compare: (ab)n/2 = (2 × 16)1 = 32 ✓

CHECK 8. (a) Find the G.M. of 3 and 27. (b) Insert 2 numbers between 2 and 16 so that the result is a GP.

Show answer

(a) G = √(3 × 27) = √(81) = 9.

(b) 2 r3 = 16 ⇒ r3 = 8 ⇒ r = 2. The numbers are 4 and 8: check 2, 4, 8, 16 ✓

9. ⚖️ A.M., G.M. and the relation between them

9.1 The two means

  • Arithmetic Mean of a and b (the "A.M." named in your syllabus):
A = (a+b)/(2)
  • Geometric Mean of positive a and b:
G = √(ab)

Think of A as the "middle" that makes the steps equal (add-style thinking). Think of G as the "middle" that makes the ratios equal (multiply-style thinking).

9.2 The theorem

For positive real numbers a and b:
A ≥ G

Equality holds (that is, A = G) if and only if a = b.

9.3 The proof — three lines, easy marks

A − G = (a+b)/(2) − √(ab) = (a + b − 2√(ab))/(2) = ((√(a) − √(b))2)/(2) ≥ 0

A square is never negative. So A − G ≥ 0, which means A ≥ G.

Equality: A − G = 0 happens exactly when √(a) = √(b), that is, a = b. ∎

9.4 Applications (the standard question family)

Application 1: x + (1)/(x) ≥ 2 for x > 0.

  • Apply A ≥ G to the positive numbers x and (1)/(x):
(x + (1)/(x))/(2) ≥ √(x · (1)/(x)) = √(1) = 1
  • Multiply by 2: x + (1)/(x) ≥ 2.
  • Equality when x = (1)/(x), that is x2 = 1, so x = 1 (since x > 0). ∎

Application 2 (minimum value). Find the minimum value of 4x + (16)/(x) for x > 0.

  • Apply A ≥ G to 4x and (16)/(x):
(4x + (16)/(x))/(2) ≥ √(4x · (16)/(x)) = √(64) = 8
  • So 4x + (16)/(x) ≥ 16.
  • Equality when 4x = (16)/(x) ⇒ x2 = 4 ⇒ x = 2.

✅ Minimum value = 16, and it happens at x = 2.

Application 3 (product inequality). For positive x, y, z, prove (x+y)(y+z)(z+x) ≥ 8xyz.

  • Apply A ≥ G to each pair:
x + y ≥ 2√(xy), y + z ≥ 2√(yz), z + x ≥ 2√(zx)
  • Multiply the three lines. All quantities are positive, so the inequality signs keep their direction:
(x+y)(y+z)(z+x) ≥ 8√(xy · yz · zx) = 8√(x2 y2 z2) = 8xyz ∎

Application 4 (find the numbers). The A.M. of two positive numbers is 10 and the G.M. is 8. Find the numbers.

  • Translate: (a+b)/(2) = 10 ⇒ a + b = 20, and √(ab) = 8 ⇒ ab = 64.
  • a and b are the roots of t2 − 20t + 64 = 0 (sum 20, product 64).
  • Solve: t = (20 ± √(400 − 256))/(2) = (20 ± 12)/(2), so t = 16 or t = 4.

✅ The numbers are 4 and 16. Check: (4+16)/(2) = 10 ✓, √(4 × 16) = 8 ✓

Teacher's tip. A ≥ G works only for positive numbers. Before you apply it, write one line to show the numbers are positive (or explain why).

CHECK 9. (a) Find the A.M. and G.M. of 5 and 20. Verify A ≥ G. (b) Find the minimum value of x + (4)/(x) for x > 0.

Show answer

(a) A = (5+20)/(2) = 12.5, G = √(100) = 10. And 12.5 ≥ 10 ✓

(b) (x + (4)/(x))/(2) ≥ √(4) = 2, so x + (4)/(x) ≥ 4. Equality when x = (4)/(x) ⇒ x = 2. Minimum value = 4 at x = 2.

11. 🪤 Common traps

  1. Series ≠ sum. The series is the indicated sum a1 + a2 + … The "sum of the series" is the number. Keep the words separate.
  2. Not every sequence has a formula. The prime numbers have no formula. Some sequences need a recursion or words.
  3. GP needs non-zero values. The first term a ≠ 0 and the ratio r ≠ 0. r = 0 is not allowed.
  4. The power in an is n − 1, not n. The first term must be a · r0 = a.
  5. Never use the fraction sum formula when r = 1. It divides by zero. Use Sn = na instead.
  6. Infinite GP: check | r | < 1 first. r = 1, r = −1, r = 2, … none of them have an infinite sum.
  7. G.M. sign. For positive a, b, the G.M. in your book is +√(ab). The value −√(ab) also squares to ab, but it is not the NCERT G.M.
  8. A ≥ G needs positive numbers. Also, equality (A = G) happens when a = b. The statement is "A ≥ G", not "always strictly greater".
  9. Σ shows the adding process. It is notation, not the answer.
  10. Do not mix GP with AP. AP is deleted from your syllabus. In a GP you multiply; in an AP you add. Read the question carefully.

Q23. (3 marks · ★★☆) The sum to infinity of a GP is 4 and the sum of its first two terms is 3. Find the GP.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — write the two facts. Sum to infinity: (a)/(1−r) = 4, so a = 4(1−r). First two terms: a + ar = 3, so a(1+r) = 3.
  • Step 2 — substitute a = 4(1−r) into the second fact:
4(1−r)(1+r) = 3 ⇒ 4(1 − r2) = 3 ⇒ r2 = (1)/(4)
  • Step 3 — so r = (1)/(2) or r = −(1)/(2). Both satisfy | r | < 1 ✓
  • Step 4 — find a for each case:
    • r = (1)/(2): a = 4(1 − (1)/(2)) = 2 ⇒ series 2 + 1 + (1)/(2) + …
    • r = −(1)/(2): a = 4(1 + (1)/(2)) = 6 ⇒ series 6 − 3 + (3)/(2) − …
  • Step 5 — check both: (2)/(1−(1)/(2)) = 4 ✓ with first two terms 3 ✓; (6)/(1+(1)/(2)) = 4 ✓ with first two terms 3 ✓

✅ Answer: 2 + 1 + (1)/(2) + … or 6 − 3 + (3)/(2) − … (two valid answers)

  • Step 2 — write the repeating part as an infinite GP:

with a = 0.6 and r = 0.1. Check: | r | < 1 ✓

Q25. (3 marks · ★★★) Can the sum to infinity of a GP (with r ≠ 0) equal exactly half of its first term? Explain.

  • Step 2 — since a ≠ 0, cancel a from both sides: (1)/(1−r) = (1)/(2)
  • Step 3 — solve: 1 − r = 2, so r = −1
  • Step 4 — check the condition: | r | = 1, which is not < 1. So the infinite sum does not exist for r = −1.

Q30. (3 marks · ★★☆) The A.M. of two positive numbers is 12.5 and the G.M. is 10. Find the numbers.

Solution.

  • Step 2 — a and b are the roots of t2 − 25t + 100 = 0

Q31. (3 marks · ★★☆) For positive a and b, prove that a/b + b/a ≥ 2. State the equality case.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — both (a)/(b) and (b)/(a) are positive (a, b > 0), so A ≥ G applies.
  • Step 2 — apply it:
((a)/(b) + (b)/(a))/(2) ≥ √((a)/(b) · (b)/(a)) = √(1) = 1
  • Step 3 — multiply by 2: (a)/(b) + (b)/(a) ≥ 2
  • Step 4 — equality when the two numbers are equal: (a)/(b) = (b)/(a) ⇒ a2 = b2 ⇒ a = b (both positive)

✅ Proved. Equality if and only if a = b.

Q32. (3 marks · ★★☆) For positive a and b, prove that a² + b² ≥ 2ab.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — a2 and b2 are positive, so A ≥ G applies to them.
  • Step 2 — apply it:
(a2 + b2)/(2) ≥ √(a2 b2) = ab
  • Step 3 — multiply by 2: a2 + b2 ≥ 2ab
  • Step 4 — equality when a2 = b2, that is a = b (both positive)

✅ Proved. Equality if and only if a = b. (In fact the result is true for all real a, b: the difference a2 + b2 − 2ab = (a−b)2 ≥ 0.)

Q33. (5 marks · ★★★) For positive a and b, prove that (a + 1)(b + 1)(a + b) ≥ 8ab.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — apply A ≥ G to three pairs separately:
a + 1 ≥ 2√(a), b + 1 ≥ 2√(b), a + b ≥ 2√(ab)
  • Step 2 — all quantities are positive, so multiply the three inequalities (the sign keeps its direction):
(a+1)(b+1)(a+b) ≥ 2√(a) · 2√(b) · 2√(ab)
  • Step 3 — simplify the right side:
8√(a · b · ab) = 8√(a2 b2) = 8ab
  • Step 4 — write the final line:
(a+1)(b+1)(a+b) ≥ 8ab ∎
  • Equality holds when a = 1, b = 1 and a = b all at once, that is a = b = 1.

✅ Proved.

(2x + (18)/(x))/(2) ≥ √(2x · (18)/(x)) = √(36) = 6
  • Step 2 — multiply by 2:

  • Step 3 — equality when the two numbers are equal:

  • Step 4 — check: 2(3) + (18)/(3) = 6 + 6 = 12 ✓

(a) 3, 6, 12, 24    (b) 2, −4, 8, −16    (c) 1, 1, 1, 1    (d) 2, 4, 6, 8

Solution.

  • Step 1 — test the ratios in each option:
    • (a): (6)/(3) = 2, (12)/(6) = 2 ✓ (GP, r = 2)
    • (b): (−4)/(2) = −2, (8)/(−4) = −2 ✓ (GP, r = −2)
    • (c): all ratios = 1 ✓ (GP, r = 1)
    • (d): (4)/(2) = 2 but (6)/(4) = 1.5 ✗ (ratios differ — not a GP; it adds 2 each time)

✅ Answer: (d).

Q40. (1 mark · ★☆☆ Assertion–Reason) Assertion (A): The 6th term of the GP 1, 2, 4, 8, … is 32. Reason (R): The nth term of a GP is aₙ = arⁿ⁻¹.

Choose: (a) both true, R explains A (b) both true, R does not explain A (c) A true, R false (d) A false, R true

Solution.

  • Step 1 — check A: a = 1, r = 2; a6 = 1 × 25 = 32 ✓ true
  • Step 2 — check R: an = arn−1 is the correct GP general-term formula ✓ true
  • Step 3 — does R explain A? Yes — using R with n = 6 gives exactly 32.

✅ Answer: (a).

Q41. (4 marks · ★★☆ Case study) A fitness app gives 20 points on day 1. Every next day, the app doubles the points of the previous day.

(i) How many points does the user get on day 5? (ii) Write the expression for the points on day n. (iii) Find the total points for the first 7 days.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — the daily points form a GP: 20, 40, 80, … with a = 20, r = 2.
  • Step 2 (i) — day 5 is the 5th term: a5 = 20 × 24 = 20 × 16 = 320
  • Step 3 (ii) — day n: an = 20 × 2n−1
  • Step 4 (iii) — use the sum formula with n = 7:
S7 = (20(27 − 1))/(2 − 1) = 20 × (128 − 1) = 20 × 127 = 2540

✅ Answers: (i) 320 points; (ii) an = 20 · 2n−1; (iii) 2540 points.

Q42. (4 marks · ★★☆ Case study) A ball is dropped from a height of 8 m. After each bounce, it rises to exactly half of the previous height.

(i) Write the heights reached after the 1st, 2nd and 3rd bounces. (ii) Write the height after the nth bounce. (iii) Find the total distance the ball travels before it stops.

Solution.

  • Step 1 (i) — after each bounce the height halves: 4 m, 2 m, 1 m.
  • Step 2 (ii) — height after n bounces: 8 × ((1)/(2))n
  • Step 3 (iii) — the ball falls 8 m first. Then each up-and-down pair follows the GP 4, 2, 1, … (up) and 4, 2, 1, … (down).
  • Step 4 — sum to infinity of the bounce heights: S∞ = (4)/(1 − (1)/(2)) = 8 m (for the upward trips), and the same 8 m comes down.
  • Step 5 — total:
8 + 2 × 8 = 24 m

✅ Answers: (i) 4 m, 2 m, 1 m; (ii) 8((1)/(2))n; (iii) 24 m.

14. ✍️ Exam writing tips from a maths teacher

  1. Write the formula first. Then substitute the values. Examiners give steps the marks, not just the final number.
  2. Infinite GP: write the condition check. One line — "Here | r | < 1, so the sum to infinity exists" — protects your whole answer.
  3. Keep exact values. Work with fractions and powers. Convert to decimals only at the end.
  4. "Which term" questions: make both sides powers of the same base. Then compare the powers.
  5. "Two terms known" questions: divide the bigger equation by the smaller one. The first term cancels.
  6. Word problems: define a and r in words first ("start value = …, ratio = …"). Then use the GP tools.
  7. Watch the exponent n − 1. The first term must carry the power 0.
  8. Insertion questions: if the power is even, both +r and −r give real answers. Give both unless the question asks for positive numbers only.
  9. Check quickly. Add the first three or four terms with your calculator. If they do not match, something is wrong.
  10. Do not mix AP and GP. AP is deleted. In this chapter you mostly multiply, not add.

A.1 Arithmetic Progression (AP) — deleted in 2023

  • Definition: each term = previous term + a fixed difference d. Form: a, a+d, a+2d, …
  • General term: an = a + (n−1)d
  • Sum: Sn = (n)/(2)[2a + (n−1)d] = (n)/(2)(a + l), with last term l
  • The word "A.M." in your syllabus uses only A = (a+b)/(2). The rest of AP theory is gone.
  • Quick example: 3, 7, 11, … has a = 3, d = 4, so a10 = 3 + 9 × 4 = 39.
