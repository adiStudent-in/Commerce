Binomial Theorem

Class 11 Mathematics — Easy Revision Notes (STE100) · CBSE 2026-27

Big picture. A binomial is an expression with exactly two terms, like a + b or 2x − 3. The binomial theorem turns a bracket like (a+b)n into a long sum of simple terms — without multiplying the brackets again and again. The coefficient numbers come from nCr ("n choose r") and from Pascal's triangle. The chapter ends with simple applications: big powers like 995, "which is larger" comparisons, divisibility proofs, and useful sums of binomial coefficients.
How to use these notes. Read one section at a time. Each section follows the same order: Definition → Why it works → Worked example → CHECK. Do the CHECK before you move on. Section 10 is the formula wall. Section 11 lists the traps. Section 13 has the full practice bank with step-by-step solutions.

3. 🔑 The binomial theorem — statement

A little history. The numbers of Pascal's triangle were known in India long before Blaise Pascal. Indian mathematicians (Pingala, and Halayudha around the 10th century) used them to count poetic metres. Omar Khayyam (Persia) and Yang Hui (China) also studied them. In 1654 Pascal wrote a full study of the triangle — in Europe the numbers now carry his name. The theorem for positive integral indices is proved by induction; the full proof is in Section 4.

Statement. For every positive integer n:

(a+b)n = nC0 an + nC1 an−1b + nC2 an−2b2 + … + nCn bn

In sigma notation:

(a+b)n = Σr=0n nCr an−r br

The pattern of every expansion. Look at (a+b)3 = a3 + 3a2b + 3ab2 + b3:

  • The number of terms is n + 1. Here n = 3, so there are 4 terms.
  • The powers of a go down: 3, 2, 1, 0.
  • The powers of b go up: 0, 1, 2, 3.
  • In every term, (power of a) + (power of b) = n. Here every pair adds to 3.
  • The coefficients 1, 3, 3, 1 are 3C0, 3C1, 3C2, 3C3.

Worked example 1. Write the expansion of (x+2)4.

  • Here n = 4, so 5 terms. The coefficients are 4C0 = 1, 4C1 = 4, 4C2 = 6, 4C3 = 4, 4C4 = 1.
  • Write the powers of x (going down) and the powers of 2 (going up):
(x+2)4 = 4C0 x4 + 4C1 x3(2) + 4C2 x2(2)2 + 4C3 x(2)3 + 4C4 (2)4
  • Simplify each term: 1 · x4 + 4 · 2x3 + 6 · 4x2 + 4 · 8x + 1 · 16
  • Final answer: x4 + 8x3 + 24x2 + 32x + 16.

✅ (x+2)4 = x4 + 8x3 + 24x2 + 32x + 16.

Worked example 2 (the special case (1+x)n). Put a = 1 in the theorem. The powers of 1 disappear, and the coefficients stand alone:

(1+x)n = nC0 + nC1 x + nC2 x2 + … + nCn xn

Example: (1+x)5 = 1 + 5x + 10x2 + 10x3 + 5x4 + x5.

CHECK 1. Write the expansion of (x+3)4. Then count its terms.

Show answer
  • Coefficients: 1, 4, 6, 4, 1. Powers of x go down, powers of 3 go up.
  • (x+3)4 = x4 + 4x3(3) + 6x2(9) + 4x(27) + 81
  • = x4 + 12x3 + 54x2 + 108x + 81
  • 5 terms.

4. 🏗️ Why it works — the proof

The syllabus says "statement and proof". The proof uses induction (the Principle of Mathematical Induction). It has two steps. This is all you need to write in the exam.

The claim. For every positive integer n:

(a+b)n = Σr=0n nCr an−r br

Step 1 — check the claim for n = 1.

  • Left side: (a+b)1 = a + b.
  • Right side: 1C0 a1 + 1C1 b1 = a + b.
  • Both sides match. ✓

Step 2 — assume the claim for n, then prove it for n + 1.

Assume (a+b)n = Σr=0n nCr an−rbr. Multiply both sides by (a+b):

(a+b)n+1 = (a+b)Σr=0n nCr an−rbr = Σr=0n nCr an+1−rbr + Σr=0n nCr an−rbr+1

In the second sum, change the counter to k = r + 1 (so r = k − 1):

Σr=0n nCr an−rbr+1 = Σk=1n+1 nCk−1 an+1−kbk

Now put the two sums together. The first sum runs from r = 0 to n; the second runs from k = 1 to n + 1. Every middle term gets a pair of coefficients. Use Pascal's rule:

nCk + nCk−1 = n+1Ck

Then:

(a+b)n+1 = Σk=0n+1 n+1Ck an+1−kbk

That is the claim for n + 1. So the claim is true for every positive integer n. ∎

The one-line intuition (write this too — it shows understanding). In the expansion of (a+b)n, the term an−rbr comes from choosing b from r of the n brackets, and a from the other n − r brackets. The number of ways to choose those r brackets is nCr. That is where the coefficients come from.

CHECK 2. Write the two steps of the induction proof in your own words. No full algebra needed.

Show answer
  • Base step: for n = 1, the formula gives 1C0 a + 1C1 b = a + b, which matches (a+b)1.
  • Induction step: assume the formula for n. Multiply by (a+b). Combine the two sums term by term with Pascal's rule nCk + nCk−1 = n+1Ck. The result is the formula for n + 1.

5. 🔺 Pascal's triangle

Pascal's triangle is a quick way to write the binomial coefficients without factorials.

How to build it. Start with a 1 at the top. Every new number is the sum of the two numbers above it. Where nothing is above, take 0.

Row nNumbersThese are
010C0
11, 11C0, 1C1
21, 2, 12C0, …, 2C2
31, 3, 3, 13C0, …, 3C3
41, 4, 6, 4, 14C0, …, 4C4
51, 5, 10, 10, 5, 15C0, …, 5C5
61, 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 16C0, …, 6C6

How to read it. Row n gives the coefficients of (a+b)n, in order. For example, row 4 is 1, 4, 6, 4, 1, so:

(a+b)4 = a4 + 4a3b + 6a2b2 + 4ab3 + b4

Three facts to remember.

  • Every row starts and ends with 1.
  • Every row reads the same from both sides (symmetric).
  • Row n has n + 1 numbers.

Why the triangle builds the coefficients. Each number is the sum of the two numbers above it. In coefficient language that is exactly Pascal's rule:

nCr + nCr−1 = n+1Cr

This is the same identity you used in the proof. The triangle and the theorem are the same idea in two shapes.

Worked example 3. Write row 7 of Pascal's triangle from row 6.

  • Row 6: 1, 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1.
  • Now add neighbours: 1; 1 + 6 = 7; 6 + 15 = 21; 15 + 20 = 35; 20 + 15 = 35; 15 + 6 = 21; 6 + 1 = 7; 1.
  • Row 7: 1, 7, 21, 35, 35, 21, 7, 1.

✅ These are the coefficients of (a+b)7.

CHECK 3. Use row 5 of Pascal's triangle to write the expansion of (x−y)5.

Show answer
  • Row 5: 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1.
  • With alternating signs (odd powers of y get minus):
  • (x−y)5 = x5 − 5x4y + 10x3y2 − 10x2y3 + 5xy4 − y5
  • It has 6 terms.

6. 🎲 Binomial coefficients — the toolbox

The numbers nCr follow four handy rules. They turn long sums into one-line answers.

RuleWhat it says
nC0 = 1 and nCn = 1Every expansion starts and ends with coefficient 1
nC1 = nThe second coefficient equals n
nCr = nCn−rSymmetry: choosing r items is the same as leaving out n − r items
nCr + nCr−1 = n+1CrPascal's rule — the engine behind the triangle and the proof

Worked example 4. Show that 7C3 = 7C4, and find the value.

  • Symmetry: 7C3 = 7C7−3 = 7C4. ✓
  • Value: 7C3 = (7 × 6 × 5)/(3 × 2 × 1) = 35.

✅ Both coefficients equal 35.

Worked example 5 (find n from a symmetry). If nC8 = nC6, find n.

  • The rule: nCr = nCs gives r = s or r + s = n.
  • Here 8 = 6 is false. So use the sum: 8 + 6 = n.
  • Check: 14C8 = 14C6. ✓

✅ n = 14.

CHECK 4. Evaluate 6C2 + 6C3 in two ways: directly, and with Pascal's rule.

Show answer
  • Directly: 6C2 = 15 and 6C3 = 20, so the sum is 35.
  • Pascal's rule with n = 6, r = 3: 6C3 + 6C2 = 7C3 = 35. ✓
  • Both give 35.

7. 🧩 Writing expansions — methods and special cases

Method for (a+b)n. Four steps:

  1. Write n + 1 terms. Powers of a go down (n to 0). Powers of b go up (0 to n).
  2. Put the coefficients nC0, nC1, …, nCn in front — or read them from Pascal's triangle.
  3. Substitute the actual values of a and b.
  4. Simplify the numbers.

Method for (a−b)n. Replace b by −b in the theorem. The signs alternate:

(a−b)n = nC0 an − nC1 an−1b + nC2 an−2b2 − … + (−1)n nCn bn

The sign rule. A term with an odd power of b gets a minus sign. A term with an even power of b gets a plus sign.

Worked example 6. Expand (2x−3)4.

  • Here n = 4: coefficients 1, 4, 6, 4, 1. Now a = 2x and b = 3, with alternating signs.
  • (2x−3)4 = (2x)4 − 4(2x)3(3) + 6(2x)2(32) − 4(2x)(33) + 34
  • = 16x4 − 4 · 8x3 · 3 + 6 · 4x2 · 9 − 4 · 2x · 27 + 81
  • = 16x4 − 96x3 + 216x2 − 216x + 81

✅ (2x−3)4 = 16x4 − 96x3 + 216x2 − 216x + 81.

Worked example 7 (minus sign inside the bracket). Expand (1−2x)5.

  • Here a = 1 and b = 2x. Coefficients: 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1.
  • (1−2x)5 = 1 − 5(2x) + 10(2x)2 − 10(2x)3 + 5(2x)4 − (2x)5
  • = 1 − 10x + 40x2 − 80x3 + 80x4 − 32x5

✅ (1−2x)5 = 1 − 10x + 40x2 − 80x3 + 80x4 − 32x5.

Counting terms. The expansion of (a+b)n has n + 1 terms. Quick facts: (a+b)10 has 11 terms; (2x−3y)9 also has 10 terms — the two letters do not change the count.

Finding one coefficient without writing everything. For a bracket like (2x+3)6, write the running term pattern 6Cr (2x)6−r 3r. Then solve for the power you need. This is the "general term" idea — NCERT keeps it as a formative topic (Section 9).

CHECK 5. Expand (x−2)5.

Show answer
  • Coefficients: 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1. Signs alternate (odd power of 2 gets minus).
  • (x−2)5 = x5 − 5x4(2) + 10x3(4) − 10x2(8) + 5x(16) − 32
  • = x5 − 10x4 + 40x3 − 80x2 + 80x − 32

8. ⚙️ Simple applications — powers, divisibility and identities

8.1 Evaluating a large power

Write the base near a round number, then expand.

Example. Evaluate 995.

  • 99 = 100 − 1. So 995 = (100−1)5.
  • = 1005 − 5 · 1004 + 10 · 1003 − 10 · 1002 + 5 · 100 − 1
  • = 10000000000 − 500000000 + 10000000 − 100000 + 500 − 1
  • = 9509900499

✅ 995 = 9509900499.

Method. Powers of the round number are easy (powers of 10 here). Write them in a column, keep the signs from the sign rule, and add carefully.

8.2 Which number is larger?

Example. Which is larger: (1.1)10000 or 1000?

  • (1.1)10000 = (1 + 0.1)10000 = 1 + 10000(0.1) + 10000C2(0.1)2 + …
  • = 1 + 1000 + (positive terms)
  • = 1001 + (positive terms)

✅ (1.1)10000 is larger — its first two terms already beat 1000.

Method. Expand two or three terms. If they already beat the other number, the whole value beats it too. Every coefficient and power here is positive, so nothing can cancel down.

8.3 Divisibility proofs

Example. Show that 9n+1 − 8n − 9 is divisible by 64, for every positive integer n.

  • 9n+1 = 9 · 9n = 9(1+8)n
  • Expand the bracket:
9(1+8)n = 9[1 + 8n + (n(n−1))/(2) · 82 + …] = 9 + 72n + 64k
  • Here k is an integer: every term from the third onward carries 82 = 64.
  • So 9n+1 − 8n − 9 = (9 + 72n + 64k) − 8n − 9 = 64n + 64k = 64(n + k).

✅ Divisible by 64. ∎

Method. Write the power as (1 + round number)n, expand, and collect the factor you need (64 here). Then arrange the expression so that factor stands alone.

8.4 Sums of binomial coefficients

Example. Evaluate nC0 + nC1 + … + nCn.

  • Put a = b = 1 in the theorem: (1+1)n = Σr=0n nCr.
  • So the sum is 2n. For n = 8: 256.

✅ Sum = 2n.

Two more results in the same style.

  • Put a = 1, b = −1: (1−1)n = Σr=0n nCr (−1)r, so nC0 − nC1 + nC2 − … = 0 (for n ≥ 1).
  • Add the two lines: nC0 + nC2 + nC4 + … = 2n−1 and nC1 + nC3 + nC5 + … = 2n−1.

Example. Prove that Σr=0n 3r nCr = 4n.

  • Put a = 1, b = 3 in the theorem: (1+3)n = Σr=0n nCr · 1n−r · 3r.
  • The powers of 1 disappear, so the right side is exactly Σr=0n 3r nCr.

✅ The sum equals 4n. ∎

CHECK 6. Evaluate 8C0 + 8C1 + … + 8C8. Then evaluate the even-index sum 8C0 + 8C2 + 8C4 + 8C6 + 8C8.

Show answer
  • All coefficients: 28 = 256.
  • Even-index sum: 28−1 = 27 = 128.
  • Quick check: odd-index sum is also 128, and 128 + 128 = 256. ✓

9. 📝 General term and middle term (formative only)

Read this box for school tests, not for the board paper. CBSE 2026-27 marks this topic as formative only. It is in the NCERT book and it is useful, but it carries no summative (board) weightage. The board paper will not require the general-term formula. School tests often ask it — so learn it, and practise Topic F of the bank.

9.1 General term

Number the terms of (a+b)n from T1 (first) to Tn+1 (last). The term at position r + 1 is:

Tr+1 = nCr an−r br

Why. The power of b tells you the position: the term Tr+1 carries br. Check on (a+b)3: the third term of a3 + 3a2b + 3ab2 + b3 is 3ab2, and the formula with r = 2 gives 3C2 a1b2 = 3ab2. ✓

Worked example 8. Find the 5th term of (x−2)7.

  • "5th term" means r + 1 = 5, so r = 4.
  • T5 = 7C4 x7−4(−2)4 = 35 · x3 · 16

✅ T5 = 560x3.

9.2 Middle term

  • If n is even, there is one middle term. Its position is (n)/(2) + 1.
  • If n is odd, there are two middle terms. Their positions are (n+1)/(2) and (n+3)/(2).

Worked example 9. Find the middle term of (x + 2y)8.

  • n = 8 — even. The middle term is at position (8)/(2) + 1 = 5, so r = 4.
  • T5 = 8C4 x4 (2y)4 = 70 · x4 · 16y4

✅ Middle term = 1120x4y4.

CHECK 7. Find the middle terms of (x+y)9.

Show answer
  • n = 9 — odd, so two middle terms: positions (9+1)/(2) = 5 and (9+3)/(2) = 6.
  • T5 = 9C4 x5y4 = 126x5y4
  • T6 = 9C5 x4y5 = 126x4y5
  • Nice pattern: the two answers are mirror images. ✓

11. 🪤 Common traps

  1. Count the terms as n + 1. (a+b)5 has 6 terms, not 5. Check this first in every expansion question.
  2. In every term, powers add to n. (Power of a) + (power of b) = n. Use this one-second check on every term you write.
  3. Signs in (a−b)n. A term with an odd power of b gets a minus sign. A lost sign is the most common lost mark.
  4. nC0 = 1. It is never 0. Also nC1 = n, and every row starts and ends with 1.
  5. (a+b)n is not an + bn. Never. Try n = 2: (a+b)2 = a2 + 2ab + b2.
  6. The 2n trick needs a = b = 1 exactly. With a = 1, b = −1 you get 0, not 2n.
  7. The alternating sum is 0 only for n ≥ 1. For n = 0 the sum is 1.
  8. Term vs coefficient. "Find the term" needs the whole piece, like 1512x5. "Find the coefficient" needs only the number, 1512.
  9. General term position is r + 1. The term Tr+1 carries br. Off by one, and the answer changes. (📝 formative topic)
  10. Middle term: check the parity of n first. One middle term if n is even; two middle terms if n is odd. (📝 formative topic)
  11. Divisibility proofs: keep the block factor visible. Write 64k as one piece and pull it out at the end. Do not mix it into the other numbers.
  12. Big powers: pick the right split. 99 = 100 − 1, 101 = 100 + 1, 102 = 100 + 2. Expanding powers of 10 is easy; expanding powers of 99 is not.

Topic A — Expansions with the theorem

Q1. (2 marks · ★☆☆) Expand (x+2)5.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — n = 5, so 6 terms. Coefficients: 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1.
  • Step 2 — powers of x go down, powers of 2 go up:
(x+2)5 = x5 + 5x4(2) + 10x3(4) + 10x2(8) + 5x(16) + 32
  • Step 3 — simplify each term: x5 + 10x4 + 40x3 + 80x2 + 80x + 32

✅ Answer: x5 + 10x4 + 40x3 + 80x2 + 80x + 32.

Q2. (3 marks · ★☆☆) Expand (2x+3)4.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — n = 4: coefficients 1, 4, 6, 4, 1. Here a = 2x, b = 3.
  • Step 2 — no alternating signs (both terms are positive):
(2x+3)4 = (2x)4 + 4(2x)3(3) + 6(2x)2(32) + 4(2x)(33) + 34
  • Step 3 — simplify: 16x4 + 4 · 8x3 · 3 + 6 · 4x2 · 9 + 4 · 2x · 27 + 81
  • Step 4 — finish the numbers: 16x4 + 96x3 + 216x2 + 216x + 81

✅ Answer: 16x4 + 96x3 + 216x2 + 216x + 81.

Q3. (3 marks · ★★☆) Expand ((x)/(3) + (1)/(x))5.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — n = 5: coefficients 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1. Here a = (x)/(3), b = (1)/(x).
  • Step 2 — write each term with 5Cr:
Tr+1 = 5Cr ((x)/(3))5−r ((1)/(x))r = 5Cr · (x5−2r)/(35−r)
  • Step 3 — list the six terms:
r = 0: (x5)/(243), r = 1: (5x3)/(81), r = 2: (10x)/(27), r = 3: (10)/(9x), r = 4: (5)/(3x3), r = 5: (1)/(x5)
  • Step 4 — join with + signs.

✅ Answer: (x5)/(243) + (5x3)/(81) + (10x)/(27) + (10)/(9x) + (5)/(3x3) + (1)/(x5).

Q4. (3 marks · ★★☆) Expand ((2)/(x) − (x)/(2))4.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — n = 4: coefficients 1, 4, 6, 4, 1. Here a = (2)/(x), b = (x)/(2), with alternating signs.
  • Step 2 — expand directly:
((2)/(x))4 − 4((2)/(x))3((x)/(2)) + 6((2)/(x))2((x)/(2))2 − 4((2)/(x))((x)/(2))3 + ((x)/(2))4
  • Step 3 — simplify each term: (16)/(x4) − 4 · (8)/(x3) · (x)/(2) + 6 · (4)/(x2) · (x2)/(4) − 4 · (2)/(x) · (x3)/(8) + (x4)/(16)
  • Step 4 — reduce the fractions: (16)/(x4) − (16)/(x2) + 6 − x2 + (x4)/(16)

✅ Answer: (16)/(x4) − (16)/(x2) + 6 − x2 + (x4)/(16).

Q5. (3 marks · ★★☆) Evaluate (√(3) + √(2))6 using the binomial theorem.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — n = 6: coefficients 1, 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1.
  • Step 2 — a term (√(3))k(√(2))j is an integer when k, j are both even; it is a multiple of √(6) when k, j are both odd.
  • Step 3 — add the integer terms:
(√(3))6 + 15(√(3))4(√(2))2 + 15(√(3))2(√(2))4 + (√(2))6 = 27 + 270 + 180 + 8 = 485
  • Step 4 — add the √(6) terms:
6(√(3))5(√(2)) + 20(√(3))3(√(2))3 + 6(√(3))(√(2))5 = 54√(6) + 120√(6) + 24√(6) = 198√(6)
  • Step 5 — combine: 485 + 198√(6).

✅ Answer: (√(3) + √(2))6 = 485 + 198√(6).

Q6. (3 marks · ★★☆) Expand (x + (1)/(x))6.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — n = 6: coefficients 1, 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1.
  • Step 2 — term pattern: 6Cr x6−r((1)/(x))r = 6Cr x6−2r.
  • Step 3 — list the terms: x6 + 6x4 + 15x2 + 20 + (15)/(x2) + (6)/(x4) + (1)/(x6)
  • Step 4 — note the middle term 20 is a constant (the term independent of x).

✅ Answer: x6 + 6x4 + 15x2 + 20 + (15)/(x2) + (6)/(x4) + (1)/(x6).

Q7. (3 marks · ★★☆) Expand (3−2x)5.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — n = 5: coefficients 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1. Here a = 3, b = 2x, with alternating signs.
  • Step 2 — expand: 35 − 5(34)(2x) + 10(33)(2x)2 − 10(32)(2x)3 + 5(3)(2x)4 − (2x)5
  • Step 3 — simplify: 243 − 5 · 81 · 2x + 10 · 27 · 4x2 − 10 · 9 · 8x3 + 5 · 3 · 16x4 − 32x5
  • Step 4 — finish: 243 − 810x + 1080x2 − 720x3 + 240x4 − 32x5

✅ Answer: 243 − 810x + 1080x2 − 720x3 + 240x4 − 32x5.

  • Step 3 — simplify: 1000000 − 30000 + 300 − 1

  • Step 3 — each power of 100 is a 1 followed by zeros: 10000000000 + 500000000 + 10000000 + 100000 + 500 + 1
  • Step 4 — add carefully.

Q10. (3 marks · ★★☆) Evaluate 963 using the binomial theorem.

Solution.

  • Step 2 — expand: 1003 − 3 · 1002 · 4 + 3 · 100 · 42 − 43
  • Step 3 — simplify: 1000000 − 120000 + 4800 − 64
  • Step 4 — add: 1000000 − 120000 = 880000; then 880000 + 4800 = 884800; then 884800 − 64 = 884736.

Q11. (3 marks · ★★☆) Evaluate 1024 using the binomial theorem.

  • Step 2 — expand: 1004 + 4 · 1003 · 2 + 6 · 1002 · 22 + 4 · 100 · 23 + 24
  • Step 3 — simplify: 100000000 + 8000000 + 240000 + 3200 + 16
  • Step 4 — add.

✅ Answer: 1024 = 108243216.

Q12. (3 marks · ★★☆) Which is larger: (1.01)1000000 or 10000?

Solution.

  • Step 2 — expand the first terms:
1 + 1000000(0.01) + (positive terms) = 1 + 10000 + (positive terms)
  • Step 3 — compare: the value is more than 10001, and every further term is positive.

Topic C — The theorem, divisibility and coefficient identities

Q13. (5 marks · ★★☆) State and prove the binomial theorem for positive integral indices.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — statement: for every positive integer n, (a+b)n = Σr=0n nCr an−rbr.
  • Step 2 — base step: for n = 1, left side is a + b, right side is 1C0 a + 1C1 b = a + b. ✓
  • Step 3 — assume the statement for n, then multiply by (a+b):
(a+b)n+1 = Σr=0n nCr an+1−rbr + Σr=0n nCr an−rbr+1
  • Step 4 — in the second sum, put k = r+1. Now match the two sums term by term and use Pascal's rule nCk + nCk−1 = n+1Ck:
(a+b)n+1 = Σk=0n+1 n+1Ck an+1−kbk
  • Step 5 — this is the statement for n+1. By induction, it is true for every positive integer n. ∎

✅ Answer: proved — base step plus the induction step.

Q14. (3 marks · ★★☆) Show that 32n+2 − 8n − 9 is divisible by 8, for every positive integer n.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — rewrite the power: 32n+2 = 9n+1 = 9 · 9n = 9(1+8)n.
  • Step 2 — expand the bracket. Every term from the third onward carries 82 = 64:
9(1+8)n = 9[1 + 8n + (n(n−1))/(2) · 64 + …] = 9 + 72n + 64k
  • Step 3 — subtract:
32n+2 − 8n − 9 = (9 + 72n + 64k) − 8n − 9 = 64n + 64k = 8(8n + 8k)
  • Step 4 — the bracket is an integer, so the expression is a multiple of 8 (and of 64).

✅ Answer: proved — the expression equals 8 × (integer).

Q15. (3 marks · ★★☆) Show that 8n − 7n − 1 is divisible by 49, for every positive integer n.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — write the power with a small bracket: 8n = (1+7)n.
  • Step 2 — expand. Every term from the third onward carries 72 = 49:
(1+7)n = 1 + 7n + (n(n−1))/(2) · 49 + (n(n−1)(n−2))/(6) · 343 + … = 1 + 7n + 49m
  • Step 3 — subtract: 8n − 7n − 1 = (1 + 7n + 49m) − 7n − 1 = 49m.
  • Step 4 — m is an integer, so the expression is a multiple of 49.

✅ Answer: proved. (This is the same method as the 64 example in Section 8 — find the block factor, then pull it out.)

Q16. (3 marks · ★★☆) (a) Prove that Σr=0n 3r nCr = 4n. (b) Hence evaluate Σr=0n 2r nCr.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — (a) put a = 1, b = 3 in (a+b)n:
(1+3)n = Σr=0n nCr 1n−r · 3r = Σr=0n 3r nCr
  • Step 2 — the left side is 4n, so the sum is 4n. ∎
  • Step 3 — (b) the same trick with b = 2: the sum is (1+2)n = 3n.

✅ Answer: (a) 4n; (b) 3n.

Q17. (3 marks · ★★☆) Show that nC0 − nC1 + nC2 − … + (−1)n nCn = 0 for n ≥ 1, and state the value for n = 6.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — put a = 1, b = −1 in the theorem:
(1−1)n = Σr=0n nCr 1n−r(−1)r = nC0 − nC1 + nC2 − …
  • Step 2 — the left side is 0n = 0 (true for every n ≥ 1).
  • Step 3 — for n = 6 the value is still 0.

✅ Answer: proved for n ≥ 1; value at n = 6 is 0.

Q18. (3 marks · ★★★) Prove that nC0 + nC2 + nC4 + … = 2n−1.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — put a = b = 1:
nC0 + nC1 + nC2 + … = 2n …(1)
  • Step 2 — put a = 1, b = −1:
nC0 − nC1 + nC2 − … = 0 …(2)
  • Step 3 — add (1) and (2). The odd-index terms cancel:
2(nC0 + nC2 + nC4 + …) = 2n
  • Step 4 — divide both sides by 2.

✅ Answer: the even-index sum is 2n−1 (and so is the odd-index sum).

Topic D — Binomial coefficients and sums

Q19. (2 marks · ★☆☆) Evaluate 10C0 + 10C1 + … + 10C10.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — the sum of all binomial coefficients of index n is 2n (put a = b = 1).
  • Step 2 — here n = 10: 210 = 1024.

✅ Answer: 1024.

Q20. (2 marks · ★☆☆) If nC4 = nC5, find n.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — the rule: nCr = nCs gives r = s or r + s = n.
  • Step 2 — 4 = 5 is false, so use the sum: n = 4 + 5 = 9.
  • Step 3 — check: 9C4 = 126 and 9C5 = 126. ✓

✅ Answer: n = 9.

Q21. (3 marks · ★★☆) Find the positive integer n for which nC3 = 20.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — write the formula: nC3 = (n(n−1)(n−2))/(3 × 2 × 1) = 20.
  • Step 2 — multiply out: n(n−1)(n−2) = 120.
  • Step 3 — try small values: n = 6 gives 6 × 5 × 4 = 120. ✓
  • (The values n = 5 and below give less than 120; large values give more.)

✅ Answer: n = 6.

Q22. (3 marks · ★★☆) Evaluate 9C3 + 9C4 using Pascal's rule. Check the answer directly.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — Pascal's rule with n = 9, r = 4: 9C4 + 9C3 = 10C4.
  • Step 2 — 10C4 = (10 × 9 × 8 × 7)/(4 × 3 × 2 × 1) = 210.
  • Step 3 — direct check: 9C3 = 84, 9C4 = 126, and 84 + 126 = 210. ✓

✅ Answer: 210.

Q23. (2 marks · ★☆☆) Write row 8 of Pascal's triangle, using row 7: 1, 7, 21, 35, 35, 21, 7, 1.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — start with 1. Then add neighbours: 1+7 = 8; 7+21 = 28; 21+35 = 56; 35+35 = 70; 35+21 = 56; 21+7 = 28; 7+1 = 8; then 1.
  • Step 2 — write in order.

✅ Answer: 1, 8, 28, 56, 70, 56, 28, 8, 1.

Q24. (2 marks · ★☆☆) (a) How many numbers are in row 9 of Pascal's triangle? (b) What is their sum?

Solution.

  • Step 1 — row n has n + 1 numbers. For row 9: 9 + 1 = 10 numbers.
  • Step 2 — the sum of row 9 is 29.

✅ Answer: (a) 10 numbers; (b) 29 = 512.

Q25. (3 marks · ★★☆) In the expansion of (a+b)10: (i) how many terms are there? (ii) what is the coefficient of the 4th term?

Solution.

  • Step 2 — (ii) the 4th term is 10C3 a7b3. Its coefficient is 10C3.
  • Step 3 — 10C3 = (10 × 9 × 8)/(3 × 2 × 1) = 120.

Q26. (3 marks · ★★☆) Row 10 of Pascal's triangle is 1, 10, 45, 120, 210, 252, 210, 120, 45, 10, 1. (i) Write the expansion of (1+x)10 up to the term in x2. (ii) State the coefficient of x5.

Topic F — General term and middle term (📝 formative practice)

These questions go beyond the board scope. Practise them for school tests.
Q27. (3 marks · ★★☆) Find the coefficient of x5 in (x+3)8.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — general term: Tr+1 = 8Cr x8−r · 3r.
  • Step 2 — we need the power of x to be 5: 8 − r = 5, so r = 3.
  • Step 3 — T4 = 8C3 · 33 = 56 × 27 = 1512.

✅ Answer: the term is 1512x5, so the coefficient is 1512.

Q28. (3 marks · ★★☆) Find the term independent of x in the expansion of ((3)/(2)x2 − (1)/(3x))9.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — general term:
Tr+1 = 9Cr ((3)/(2)x2)9−r (−(1)/(3x))r = 9Cr ((3)/(2))9−r (−(1)/(3))r x18−3r
  • Step 2 — "independent of x" means the power of x is 0: 18 − 3r = 0, so r = 6.
  • Step 3 — the sign is positive (r = 6 is even). Now compute:
T7 = 9C6 ((3)/(2))3 ((1)/(3))6 = 84 · (27)/(8) · (1)/(729) = (84)/(216) = (7)/(18)

✅ Answer: (7)/(18).

Q29. (3 marks · ★★☆) Find the middle terms in the expansion of (3 − (x3)/(6))7.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — n = 7 is odd, so there are two middle terms: positions (7+1)/(2) = 4 and (7+3)/(2) = 5.
  • Step 2 — 4th term (r = 3):
T4 = 7C3 · 34 (−(x3)/(6))3 = 35 · 81 · (−(x9)/(216)) = −(105x9)/(8)
  • Step 3 — 5th term (r = 4):
T5 = 7C4 · 33 ((x3)/(6))4 = 35 · 27 · (x12)/(1296) = (35x12)/(48)

✅ Answer: −(105x9)/(8) and (35x12)/(48).

Q30. (3 marks · ★★☆) Find the middle term in the expansion of ((x)/(3) + 9y)10.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — n = 10 is even. The middle term is at position (10)/(2) + 1 = 6, so r = 5.
  • Step 2:
T6 = 10C5 ((x)/(3))5 (9y)5 = 252 · (x5)/(35) · 95 y5
  • Step 3 — combine the powers of 3: (95)/(35) = 35 = 243.
  • Step 4 — multiply: 252 × 243 = 61236.

✅ Answer: 61236x5y5.

Q31. (3 marks · ★★☆) Find the coefficient of x6y3 in the expansion of (x+2y)9.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — general term: Tr+1 = 9Cr x9−r(2y)r.
  • Step 2 — we need y3, so r = 3. Check x: 9 − 3 = 6. ✓
  • Step 3 — T4 = 9C3 · 23 = 84 × 8 = 672.

✅ Answer: 672.

Q32. (2 marks · ★☆☆) Find the positive value of m for which the coefficient of x2 in (1+x)m is 6.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — the coefficient of x2 in (1+x)m is mC2.
  • Step 2 — set mC2 = (m(m−1))/(2) = 6, so m(m−1) = 12.
  • Step 3 — try small values: m = 4 gives 4 × 3 = 12. ✓

✅ Answer: m = 4.

Q33. (3 marks · ★★★) If the coefficients of the (r−5)th and (2r−1)th terms in the expansion of (1+x)34 are equal, find r.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — the coefficient of the (r−5)th term is 34Cr−6. The coefficient of the (2r−1)th term is 34C2r−2.
  • Step 2 — equal coefficients: either r − 6 = 2r − 2, giving r = −4 (rejected — r must be a positive integer), or the sum condition:
(r−6) + (2r−2) = 34
  • Step 3 — solve: 3r − 8 = 34, so 3r = 42, giving r = 14.
  • Step 4 — check: the coefficients are 34C8 and 34C26, and 34C26 = 34C8. ✓

✅ Answer: r = 14.

Q34. (3 marks · ★★☆) Find the coefficient of x5 in (1+x)3(1+x)4.

Solution.

  • Step 1 — multiply the brackets first: (1+x)3(1+x)4 = (1+x)7.
  • Step 2 — the coefficient of x5 in (1+x)7 is 7C5.
  • Step 3 — 7C5 = 7C2 = (7 × 6)/(2) = 21.

✅ Answer: 21.

Q38. (1 mark · ★☆☆ Assertion–Reason) Assertion (A): The expansion of (x−y)6 has 7 terms. Reason (R): The expansion of (a+b)n has n+1 terms.

Choose: (a) both true, R explains A (b) both true, R does not explain A (c) A true, R false (d) A false, R true

  • Step 2 — check R: the standard term-count rule ✓ true.
  • Step 3 — R is exactly the rule used in A, so R explains A.

Topic H — Case-study practice (Section E style)

Q41. (4 marks · ★★☆ Case study) A student expands (1+x)7.

(i) How many terms does the expansion have? (ii) What is the sum of all its coefficients? (iii) What is the coefficient of x3?

Solution.

  • Step 1 (i) — here n = 7, so the number of terms is 7 + 1 = 8.
  • Step 2 (ii) — the sum of all coefficients of (1+x)n is 2n (this is the case a = b = 1 of the theorem).
27 = 128
  • Step 3 (iii) — the coefficient of x3 is 7C3.
7C3 = (7 × 6 × 5)/(3 × 2 × 1) = 35

✅ Answers: (i) 8 terms; (ii) 128; (iii) 35.

Q42. (4 marks · ★★☆ Case study) Row 6 of Pascal's triangle is 1, 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1.

(i) Write row 7. (ii) What is the sum of the numbers in row 6? (iii) Show one pair of equal numbers in row 6, and write the pair with nCr notation.

Solution.

  • Step 1 (i) — add neighbours: 1; 1+6 = 7; 6+15 = 21; 15+20 = 35; 20+15 = 35; 15+6 = 21; 6+1 = 7; 1.
  • Step 2 (ii) — sum of row n is 2n: 26 = 64. Check: 1+6+15+20+15+6+1 = 64. ✓
  • Step 3 (iii) — the row reads the same from both sides. One pair: 6C2 = 6C4 = 15.

✅ Answers: (i) 1, 7, 21, 35, 35, 21, 7, 1; (ii) 64; (iii) 6C2 = 6C4 = 15 (also 6C1 = 6C5 = 6).

14. ✍️ Exam writing tips from a maths teacher

  1. Write the formula first. Then substitute. Examiners give marks for visible steps, not just the final number.
  2. First line of every expansion question: the term count. Write "n + 1 terms" before you start. It is often worth a mark by itself.
  3. In (a−b)n, fix the signs before the numbers. Place all minus signs on the odd powers of b first; simplify only after.
  4. Big powers: show the split line. Write 99 = 100 − 1 (or 101 = 100 + 1) as its own line. Then expand.
  5. Divisibility proofs: end with a conclusion. Finish with "… = 64(n+k), where n+k is an integer. So the expression is divisible by 64." The conclusion line earns the last mark.
  6. Coefficient-sum proofs: name the substitution. Write "Put a = b = 1 in the binomial theorem" — the examiner sees the method instantly.
  7. Induction proof: label the steps. Write "Base step" and "Induction step" clearly. Use Pascal's rule by name when you combine the sums.
  8. Keep exact values. Add big numbers in a neat column, aligned by place value. No rounding in this chapter.
  9. Formative term questions: start from Tr+1. First line: Tr+1 = nCr an−rbr. Then solve for r. Show the position logic ("5th term → r = 4").
  10. One-second check on every term: powers must add to n. If a term shows a4b3 in a power-6 expansion, you have made an error.

16. 📦 Appendix A — reference only

Read this box only if a school test follows an old book. These topics are not in the CBSE 2026-27 summative syllabus for this chapter.

A.1 Approximation — deleted from the old book

The old book taught a shortcut for small x: (1+x)n ≈ 1 + nx (keep only the first two terms). Example: (1.01)5 ≈ 1 + 5(0.01) = 1.05 (true value ≈ 1.0510). This approximation is not in the current syllabus — do not use it in board answers.

A.2 Negative and fractional indices — never in Class 11

Newton extended the binomial theorem to indices like −1 or (1)/(2), giving infinite series. Example: (1+x)−1 = 1 − x + x2 − x3 + … This is outside the Class 11 course. The theorem in this chapter is only for positive integral indices.

A.3 Things the internet adds that are not in this chapter

  • Multinomial theorem — expansions of (a+b+c)n.
  • General index theorem — any real n.
  • Approximation tricks inside main answers.
  • Very large "C chains" and double-sigma notation.

None of these are part of NCERT Binomial Theorem for Class 11. Skip them for tests that follow NCERT.
