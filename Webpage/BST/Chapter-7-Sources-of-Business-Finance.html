Section 1

Business Finance: Meaning, Nature and Significance

The idea of this section. Every business needs money to run. This part defines business finance, lists its seven features in the nature, shows the seven benefits of having enough finance, and separates money into fixed capital and working capital.

1.1 What Is Business Finance?

The definition — learn this line word for word:

Business finance may be defined as planning, raising, managing and controlling all the money used or capital funds of any kind used in connection with business.

  • In short, business finance refers to the money required for carrying out business activities.
  • The four verbs in the definition carry four marks:
    • Planning — working out how much money the business will need.
    • Raising — arranging that money from the right source.
    • Managing — using the money wisely day after day.
    • Controlling — keeping a check on how the money is spent.

The starting point. Every business requires some money to start, which is called initial capital. The amount of capital required depends upon the nature and size of the business.

What this money pays for. Business finance covers every kind of fund used in the business — both owners' funds and borrowed funds.

Exam tip. For a 1-mark answer, write "Business finance refers to the money required for carrying out business activities." For a 3-mark answer, write the full four-verb definition.

1.3 Significance of Business Finance

Every business requires some money to start, which is called initial capital. The amount of capital required depends upon the nature and size of the business.

Adequate finance provides these seven benefits to a business concern:

  1. The firm can meet its liabilities on time.
  2. The firm can carry on its business smoothly.
  3. The firm can adopt latest technology and new innovative methods of production.
  4. The firm can make use of its business opportunities.
  5. The firm can face competition more strongly.
  6. The firm can replace its assets and machinery whenever necessary.
  7. The firm can face recession and depression period of trade cycle.

Exam tip. Write the seven benefits as seven short bullet lines. Each one earns one mark, and the line "the firm can..." can be kept as it is.

Section 2

Owners' Fund and Borrowed Fund

The idea of this section. Every business gets its money from two sides — the owners themselves, or the outside world. This part explains both sides, their features, and the seven-point difference between them.

2.2 Owners' Fund: Meaning and Features

The meaning. Owners' fund refers to the funds provided by the owner of the business enterprises. This fund remains with the company and it has no liability to return this fund. For example, funds or capital provided by a sole trader or shareholders of a company.

Main features of owners' fund

1 · Source of Permanent Capital

  • The owners' fund remains permanently invested in the business.
  • It is not refundable like a loan or borrowed fund.
  • This type of finance is available for all purposes throughout the life of a business.

2 · Provision of Risk Capital

  • The owners' fund is also known as the risk capital of the business, because the return on this capital depends upon the rate of earning of the company.
  • When the company is incurring loss, it is not compulsory to pay any return on owners' fund.
  • If loss continues, owners may be unable to recover even their original investment.
  • In times of prosperity a high level of return is given on owners' fund.
  • The return varies with the profit earning of the company — that is why it is called risky capital.

3 · No Security Required

  • No security has to be offered against ownership capital.

Sources of owners' fund

SourceWhat it is
Share capitalEquity shares and preference shares issued by the company
Retained earningsAccumulated profit kept inside the business

2.3 Borrowed Fund: Meaning and Features

The meaning. Borrowed fund refers to the borrowing of the firm. It includes all funds available by way of loans or credit.

Features of borrowed fund

1 · Fixed Time

  • The borrowed fund is raised by business firms for specified periods — short-term, medium-term or long-term.
TypePeriod
Short-termUp to 1 year
Medium-term1 to 5 years
Long-term5 years or more

2 · Security

  • Generally firms can get borrowed funds against the securities of assets.
  • Banks and financial institutions offer loans on different terms against the security of assets.

3 · Regular Payment of Interest

  • It is a legal compulsion on business firms to pay a regular amount of interest on borrowed fund.
  • The payment of interest as well as the principal amount is a legal liability of a company, irrespective of its earnings.

4 · Control

  • The borrowed fund security holders do not get the right to control and manage the activities of the business firm.
  • They have the right to sue the firm in case there is default in payment of interest or repayment of loan amount.

Sources of borrowed fund

Debentures · Loan from commercial banks · Loan from financial institutions · Public deposits · Inter-corporate deposits · Trade credit · etc.

Section 3

Owners' Fund in Long Term: Shares and Retained Earnings

The idea of this section. Owners' fund is raised in long term in two ways — by issuing shares and by keeping profits inside the business. This part explains equity shares, preference shares, and retained earnings.

3.1 Issue of Shares: Equity Shares

The meaning. A share is the smallest unit in which the owner's capital of the company is divided. A share may also be defined as a unit of measure of a shareholder's interest in the company.

According to the Companies Act, a public company can issue two types of shares:

  1. Equity Shares
  2. Preference Shares

Equity shares — the definition. Equity shares are the most important source of raising long-term capital. They are those shares which do not carry any special or preferential rights in the payment of annual dividend or repayment of capital. At the time of winding up also, capital of equity shareholders is returned only after every claim has been settled.

Features of equity shares

  1. Primary risk bearers — they are primary risk bearers because they enjoy the reward as well as bear the risk of ownership.
  2. Claim over residual income — they get dividend only after satisfying the claims of all creditors, outsiders and preference shareholders.
  3. Maturity — equity shares provide permanent capital to the company and cannot be redeemed during the lifetime of the company.
  4. Return — the return earned by equity shareholders is known as "dividend", which varies with the earnings of the company.
  5. Limited liability — the liability of equity shareholders is limited to the extent of capital contributed by them in the company.
  6. Voting rights — equity shareholders have voting rights in proportion to the shares held by them.

Memory hook. Six features, one line: Risk → Residual → Maturity → Return → Liability → Vote. Equity shareholders are the real owners of the company.

3.2 Preference Shares

The meaning. Preference shares are those shares which get preference over equity shares in respect of:

  1. Right to receive fixed rate of dividend.
  2. Right to receive repayment of capital.

Features of preference shares

  1. Fixed rate of dividend — preference shareholders get a fixed rate of dividend before paying dividend to equity shareholders.
  2. No security — companies do not offer any security against preference shares. The preference share capital is a part of the owner's fund capital.
  3. Voting rights — the preference shareholders do not get voting rights under general conditions. They get voting right when dividend is not paid for two years.
  4. Hybrid security — preference shares are called hybrid securities, as these shares have the features of equity shares as well as features of debentures. - Like equity shares, preference shares get dividend only when the company is earning profit. - Like debentures, preference shares get a fixed rate of return (dividend).
  5. No fixed liability — dividend to preference shareholders is paid only when the company is earning profit. During a loss period, there is no fixed liability to pay dividend as in the case of loans and borrowings.

Exam tip. The hybrid-security point is the most repeated MCQ of this chapter: Preference share = Equity share + Debenture.

3.4 Retained Earnings

The meaning. Retained earning refers to undistributed profits after payment of dividend and taxes. It refers to that part of profits which is kept as reserve for use in the future.

  • It provides the basis of expansion and growth of companies.
  • It is considered the most important source of finance; since it is internally generated, this method of financing is known as self-financing.
  • It is the ploughing back of profit inside the business.

Features of retained earnings

  1. Cushion of security — retained earnings are considered as a cushion of security because they provide support in times of adversity, when a company finds it difficult to arrange finance from any other source.
  2. Funds for new and innovative projects — a common source of funds for financing risky and innovative projects. This fund is generally used for research work, expansion projects, etc.
  3. No dilution of control — with retained earning there is no increase in the number of shareholders, so there is no risk of dilution of control.
  4. Medium and long-term finance — retained profit is considered as an ownership fund. It serves the purpose of medium- and long-term finance.
  5. Most dependable source — as an internal source, this is more dependable than the external source of finance, which depends upon the preference of investors and market conditions.
  6. Stability of dividend — a company with adequate surplus can pay a stable rate of dividend to the equity shareholders during insufficient profits.

Answer (b). The merits: cushion of security; funds for new and innovative projects; no dilution of control; medium- and long-term finance; most dependable source; stability of dividend.

Borrowed Funds: Short, Medium and Long Term

The idea of this section. Borrowed fund has to be paid back, so the period, the security, the interest and the control all change with the source. This part covers the six sources the lectures teach, from short-term trade credit to long-term debentures and financial institution loans.

4.1 Trade Credit — Short-Term Finance

The meaning. Trade credit refers to an arrangement whereby a manufacturer is granted credit from the supplier of raw materials, inputs, spare parts, etc.

  • Generally the duration of trade credit is three to six months.
  • The availability of trade credit depends upon:
    • Nature of the firm
    • Size of the firm
    • Status or creditworthiness of the firm

Features of trade credit

  1. Readily available — trade credit is available without any special efforts on the part of a manufacturer or trader.
  2. Flexible — there are no rigid rules and regulations involved in trade credit.
  3. No floatation cost — no floatation cost is involved in arranging trade credit.
  4. No interest commitment — no interest is payable on trade credit.
  5. Legal action — the buyer generally issues a promissory note, and in case he fails to meet his commitment, the supplier can take legal action.
  6. Promotes sales — trade credit helps to promote the sales of an organisation.

Merits: readily available, flexible, no floatation cost, no interest commitment, legal action through a promissory note, promotes sales.

4.2 Public Deposits — Medium-Term Finance

The meaning. Public deposits refer to unsecured deposits invited from the public. A company wishing to invite public deposits places an advertisement in newspapers. Any member of the public can fill up the prescribed form and deposit money with the company.

Features / characteristics of public deposits

  1. Unsecured — companies mortgage no assets against the public deposits raised from the general public.
  2. No loss of control — the control of the company is not diluted, as the depositors do not have voting rights.
  3. Time period — public deposits can be invited by companies for a period of six months to three years.
  4. Simple procedure to raise — a company simply has to place an advertisement in the newspaper. It carries no restrictive conditions and legal formalities like in case of other loan agreements.
  5. Economical — it is an economical source of finance, as the cost of obtaining deposits is generally lower than the cost of borrowings from banks and financial institutions.
  6. Limited amount — the amount of funds that can be raised by way of public deposits is limited. Public deposits cannot exceed 25 per cent of share capital and free reserves.

The lecture's illustration. With share capital and free reserves of ₹50 crore, the ceiling of public deposits works out to ₹12.5 crore (25 per cent).

What the lecture's case study adds

  • Public deposits are mainly used to finance working capital needs.
  • They are primarily a source of short-term finance, and can be renewed from time to time. The renewal facility enables companies to use them as medium-term finance.
  • Companies prefer this source because it is cheaper than bank loans; investors prefer it because the rate of interest is higher than on bank deposits.

4.3 Debentures / Bonds — Long-Term Finance

The definition. A debenture can be defined as "a document or a certificate" issued by a company under its seal as an acknowledgement of its debt.

  • Debentures are instruments for raising long-term debt capital.
  • Debentures are called creditorship securities, because debenture holders are called creditors of a company.

Features / characteristics of debentures

  1. Borrowed fund — debentures are part of borrowed fund capital, as debenture holders are considered as the creditors of the company.
  2. Fixed rate of interest — the interest on debentures is paid at a fixed rate. The rate of interest is decided by the Board of Directors at the time of issue of the debentures.
  3. Compulsory payment of interest — payment of regular and fixed rate of interest is a legal obligation of the company. The company has to pay interest to its debenture holders irrespective of its profit-earning capacity.
  4. Appointment of trustee — when a large number of debentures are sold to the general public, a trustee is usually appointed. The trustee ensures that the borrowing firm fulfils its contractual obligations.
  5. Security — most of the time debentures are issued against the charge of some fixed assets of the company.
  6. Redeemable — debentures are always redeemed or paid back on expiry of a fixed period of time.
  7. No voting rights — debenture holders have no say in the management, as they are never granted voting rights in the company.

4.4 A — Loan from Commercial Banks (Short & Medium-Term Finance)

The meaning. A commercial bank is an institution which performs the functions of accepting deposits, granting loans and making investments, with the aim of earning profits.

  • Banks mentioned in the lecture: SBI, Punjab National Bank, Canara Bank.

Features of commercial banks

  1. Banks provide funds to firms as and when required.
  2. The banks keep the information of the borrower confidential, so the business can maintain its secrecy.
  3. No formalities of issue of prospectus, etc. are required — it is a very easy and economical source of funds.
  4. It is a very flexible source, as the loan amount can be increased as well as decreased.
  5. Funds are generally available for a short period.
  6. Banks make a detailed investigation of the company's affairs and financial structure before issue of loan.

The meaning. Public financial institutions are referred to as lending institutions, development banks or financial institutions. After Independence, the Government of India realised that for economic development of a country only commercial banks are not sufficient.

Features of financial institutions

  2. Managerial advice — they provide managerial as well as financial advice on various matters.
  3. Source of goodwill — obtaining a loan from financial institutions increases the goodwill of the borrowing company, and it can raise funds from other sources as well.
  4. Finance even during depression — funds can be raised from financial institutions even during periods of recession or depression.
  5. Loan in foreign currency — financial institutions provide loan in foreign currency to import machinery. They also provide guarantee for purchase of capital goods from foreign countries.

6.3 Common Traps to Avoid

  1. Owners' fund vs borrowed fund on return — the return on owners' fund is risky capital that varies with profit; borrowed fund carries a legal right to a regular return and a legal liability of interest plus principal, irrespective of earnings.
  2. Hybrid security = Preference share — it carries the features of equity shares (dividend only in profit) and of debentures (fixed rate of return). Equity shares carry no fixed dividend.
  3. Order of payment — equity shareholders get dividend only after creditors, outsiders and preference shareholders; at winding up equity capital is returned last, after every claim is settled.
  4. Preference shareholders' voting right — they get it when dividend is not paid for two years. This one line is easy to miss.
  5. Public deposits figure — the ceiling is 25 per cent of share capital and free reserves, and the period is six months to three years. Renewable deposits work as medium-term finance.
  6. Trade credit — the supplier grants credit, the duration is three to six months, no interest is payable, and the buyer issues a promissory note giving the supplier the right to take legal action.
  7. Debentures — they are loan capital, the interest rate is fixed and decided by the Board at the time of issue, interest payment is compulsory, and they are redeemed on expiry of a fixed period.
  8. ICD interest rates — three months' 12%, six months' 15%, call 10% with one day's notice. ICDs are unsecured, so they carry high risk.
  9. Who regulates what — bank loans follow the rules and regulations of the RBI; public deposits follow the provisions of the Companies Act.
  10. Fixed capital vs working capital — premises, furniture and fixtures and refrigeration equipment = fixed capital; buying goods, wages, salaries and electricity expenses = working capital.

Meaning. Borrowed fund refers to the borrowing of the firm; it includes all funds available by way of loans or credit.

TypePeriod

Shares and Retained Earnings

3.1 Issue of shares — equity shares

A share is the smallest unit in which the owner's capital of a company is divided. It is also a unit of measure of a shareholder's interest in the company.

Under the Companies Act, a public company can issue two types of shares: equity shares and preference shares.

Equity shares are the most important source of raising long-term capital. They carry no special or preferential rights in the payment of annual dividend or repayment of capital. At winding up, equity capital is returned only after every claim has been settled.

    Both cases involve trade credit; VTM gets two months' credit on raw cotton, while Rajiv should not get credit because his past payment record is poor and his reputation is not good.

  4. Define public deposits, explain how they are invited, and give their period and ceiling.

    Show answer

    Public deposits are unsecured deposits invited from the public through a newspaper advertisement, which any member of the public can respond to by filling the prescribed form; they are invited for six months to three years and cannot exceed 25 per cent of share capital and free reserves.

  5. Write the six features of public deposits, and apply the 25 per cent ceiling to ₹50 crore.

    Show answer

    They are unsecured, cause no loss of control, run for six months to three years, require a simple procedure, are economical and are limited to 25 per cent of share capital and free reserves; with ₹50 crore of share capital and free reserves, the ceiling is ₹12.5 crore.

  6. What does the public deposits case study add, and how does ABC Ltd use this source?

    Show answer

    Public deposits mainly finance working capital, are primarily short-term but renewable so they can serve as medium-term finance, are cheaper than bank loans for companies and pay more than bank deposits to investors; ABC Ltd uses them for its three-year modernisation because it avoids floatation cost and the stock exchange, approaching the public by advertisement.

  7. Define a debenture, explain why it is a creditorship security, and write its seven features.

    Show answer

    A debenture is a document or certificate issued by a company under its seal as an acknowledgement of its debt; it is a creditorship security because debenture holders are creditors, and its features are borrowed fund, fixed interest fixed at the time of issue, compulsory interest irrespective of profit, appointment of a trustee, security on fixed assets, redemption after a fixed period and no voting rights.

  8. Give five differences between shares and debentures.

    Show answer

    Shares are owners' fund while debentures are borrowed fund, shareholders are owners while debenture holders are creditors, shareholders vote while debenture holders do not, shareholders have no regular return in a loss while debenture holders have a legal right to interest, and shares are paid at winding up while debentures are redeemed after a fixed period.

  9. Define a commercial bank, name three examples and write its seven features.

    Show answer

    A commercial bank accepts deposits, grants loans and makes investments to earn profit, with SBI, Punjab National Bank and Canara Bank as examples; it provides funds as and when required, keeps borrower information confidential, needs no prospectus formalities, is flexible, generally lends for a short period, investigates the company's affairs and financial structure, and may impose restrictions and difficult terms.

  10. Give five differences between public deposits and a commercial bank loan.

    Show answer

    Public deposits need no security while bank loans generally do, public deposits are governed by the Companies Act while bank loans follow RBI rules, public deposits carry a lower interest rate, public deposits run up to three years while bank loans are short and medium term, and public deposits suit companies with good reputation while bank loans suit all enterprises.

  11. Write the five features of financial institutions, and name the two sources suggested for Hina.

    Show answer

    Financial institutions provide medium and long-term finance, managerial and financial advice, a source of goodwill, finance even during depression, and foreign currency loans to import machinery; for Hina's working capital need, the suggested sources are trade credit and a loan from a commercial bank.

  12. Define ICD, name the three situations and three types, and give the six features with the Tizen case.

    Show answer

    ICDs are deposits made by one company with another and are available to all public companies whether having share capital or not; they cover acquiring securities of another company, giving a loan to another company and giving a guarantee to a person or institution lending to another company; their types are three months' deposits at 12 per cent, six months' deposits at 15 per cent and call deposits at 10 per cent on one day's notice; their features are secrecy, immediate short-term solution, freedom from bureaucratic and legal problems, unsuitability for long-term finance, high risk as unsecured deposits and a higher rate than banks, and Tizen Ltd borrowed ₹5 crores from Sky Ltd at a higher rate than a bank because no security had to be given.
