Section 1

Entrepreneurship: The Foundation

The idea of this section. Entrepreneurship is the starting point of every business. This part explains what it is, why a country needs it, what makes it different from a job, and the step-by-step journey an entrepreneur follows.

1.1 What Is Entrepreneurship?

The definition — learn this line word for word:

Entrepreneurship is a systematic, purposeful and creative activity of identifying a need, mobilising resources and organising production, with a view to delivering value to customers, returns to investors and profits to the self, in accordance with the risks and uncertainties associated with business.

Break the definition into parts — every keyword carries a mark:

Part of the definitionWhat it tells you
Systematic, purposeful, creativeIt is planned work with a clear goal, not a lucky accident
Identifying a needIt starts from a real problem in the market
Mobilising resourcesLand, labour, capital and technology are gathered
Organising productionThose resources are turned into a running firm
Value to customersThe customer gains something useful
Returns to investorsThe money-lenders get their share
Profits for the selfThe entrepreneur earns the profit
Risks and uncertaintiesProfit is never guaranteed

The bigger picture. Entrepreneurship is important for national growth and social development. The problem developing countries face is the outflow of limited national wealth and the rising demand for consumer goods and services. Producing them locally solves both problems at once.

Exam tip. For a 1-mark answer, write the first half of the definition (up to "organising production"). For a 3-mark answer, write the full line.

1.2 Why Does Entrepreneurship Matter?

What developing countries want entrepreneurs to do

  1. Produce import substitute goods to stop the outflow of national wealth.
  2. Produce more consumer goods and services locally to meet the growing demand.
  3. Produce goods for export to earn more and more foreign exchange.

All three requirements are met by more and more entrepreneurs.

Six reasons entrepreneurship is important

1 · Life line of a nation

  • Entrepreneurship is a yardstick to measure the development of any country.
  • No country can grow without the development of entrepreneurship (business).
  • U.S., U.K. and other developed countries have well-developed and well-established enterprises.
  • Every nation tries to increase its trade by having more enterprises.

2 · Provides innovation

  • The entrepreneur is an innovator.
  • He tries new methods of production, new technology, new ways of marketing.
  • He develops new business ideas and puts them into action.
  • This enhances the process of economic development.

3 · Growth of the economy

  • Entrepreneurs adapt to changes in the environment.
  • Changes inside enterprises also bring changes in society and the economy.
  • Latest technology brings growth to the enterprise and to the economy at large.

4 · Increased profits

  • The entrepreneur tries to minimise cost and get optimum utilisation of resources.
  • Lower cost plus higher efficiency always results in higher profit.

5 · Employment opportunities

  • Entrepreneurship gives scope for self-employment.
  • It also offers employment to a large number of people through successful enterprises.
  • Growth in entrepreneurial activity leads to more and more employment.

6 · Social benefits

  • Entrepreneurs adopt the latest technology and raise the standard of living.
  • They provide good quality products and services at the lowest cost.
  • By setting up enterprises in backward areas, they bring balanced regional growth.
  • By optimum utilisation of resources, they save scarce resources of society.

Exam tip. "Life line of a nation" is the most quoted line in the chapter. Learn the keyword yardstick along with it.

1.3 Characteristics of Entrepreneurship

Systematic Activity

  • Entrepreneurship is a systematic, step-by-step and purposeful activity.
  • It has temperamental, skill, knowledge and competency requirements.
  • These can be acquired, learnt and developed through formal education and vocational training, and through observation and work experience.

Lawful and Purposeful Activity

  • Entrepreneurship aims to conduct a lawful business.
  • The purpose of entrepreneurship is to create value, and it does so within the law.

Related to Innovation

  • Entrepreneurship involves a continuous search for new ideas.
  • It covers:
    • introduction of new products,
    • discovery of new markets,
    • discovery of new sources of supply of inputs,
    • technological breakthroughs,
    • introduction of newer organisational forms.
  • The aim is to do things better, cheaper, faster, and in a manner that causes the least harm to the environment.

Organisation of Production

  • Production needs the combined use of land, labour, capital and technology.
  • The entrepreneur mobilises these resources into a productive enterprise or firm.
  • The entrepreneur may own none of these resources — he may just have the 'idea', which he promotes among the resource providers.

Risk-taking

  • Entrepreneurship involves bigger risk than a career in employment or a profession, because there is no assured payoff.
  • The essence of entrepreneurship is the 'willingness to assume risk' that arises from creating and implementing new ideas.
  • Without this willingness, entrepreneurship would never succeed.

Memory hook. Five words, one line, correct order: Systematic → Lawful → Innovation → Organisation → Risk. Say "SLI-OR" out loud and the order never slips.

1.4 The Process of Entrepreneurship

Eight steps, always in this order:

#StepWhat the entrepreneur does
1Self-discoveryAssesses his strengths and weaknesses, what he enjoys doing, and his work experience
2Identifying opportunitiesFinds the need, want and problems, so he can get an opportunity to overcome the problems and fulfil the need
3Generating and evaluating ideasUses discussion, brainstorming, creativity and his own experience to develop ideas that overcome problems and meet customer expectations
4PlanningConducts research, makes the business plan and strategies
5Raising start-up capitalUses the business plan to attract venture capitalists and partners
6Start-upLaunches the enterprise, develops more customers, makes changes as per changes in the environment
7GrowthExpands and grows the enterprise
8HarvestSells the business and harvests the reward; many entrepreneurs then start a new venture

Exam tip. Step 5 has two halves — capital and partners. Step 8 is about selling the business, and it usually leads into a new venture.

Section 2

Small Business in India

The idea of this section. Small business is the practical half of entrepreneurship — how it is defined, how it is measured, and why the Indian economy depends on it.

2.1 What Is a Small Business?

Immediately after independence, small-scale industry was defined by the Industries Development and Regulation Act, 1951 as:

"Any enterprise which employs not more than 50 persons when using power and 100 when not using power and with capital assets not exceeding ₹5 lakh."

Parameter1951 definition
Persons (using power)Not more than 50
Persons (not using power)Not more than 100
Capital assetsNot exceeding ₹5 lakh

Exam tip. This is the historical definition. The working definition today is investment + turnover (section 2.2). Write both when the question says "define small-scale industry".

2.3 Role of Small Business in India's Development

Output generation

  • Small industries in India contribute almost 40% of gross industrial value added.
  • They contribute 45% of total exports from India.

Employment generation

  • After agriculture, small industries are the second largest employers of human resources in India.
  • They use more labour-intensive and less capital-intensive techniques.
  • This is a blessing for a labour-surplus country like India.

Balanced regional development

  • They produce simple products using simple technologies.
  • They depend on locally available resources.
  • So they can be set up anywhere in the country.
  • Because they can be widely spread, they contribute to balanced development.

Enormous variety of products

  • Consumer goods and readymade garments.
  • Hosiery goods, stationery items, soaps, detergents, domestic utensils.
  • Rubber, plastic, leather goods; wood and steel furniture.
  • Electronic items like television and refrigerator.

Opportunity for entrepreneurship

  • A very good platform to convert the skill and talent of people into a business idea.
  • It requires little capital.
  • Lot of support is given by the government.

Advantage of low cost production

  • They use locally available resources.
  • They have low overhead expenses and low running cost.
  • This is their competitive strength.

Two numbers to remember: 40% (gross industrial value added) and 45% (total exports).

Startups: Policy, Schemes and Funding

The idea of this section. A startup is a young, innovative firm. This part covers the government push behind startups, what actually counts as a startup, the benefits a startup gets, and the seven ways a startup can raise money.

3.1 The Startup India Scheme

  • Startup India is an initiative of the Government of India.
  • The campaign was first announced by Prime Minister Mr. Narendra Modi in his speech on 15 August, 2015.
  • It is an action plan to develop an eco-system to promote and nurture entrepreneurship across the country.
  • The Government offers various bank financing schemes to promote startups.

3.2 What Is a Startup?

A Startup is a business enterprise in the form of a private limited company, partnership, limited liability partnership or sole proprietor, registered in India, which was started less than five years ago and has an annual turnover of less than ₹25 crore.

3.4 Funding Options for Startups

Seven ways to raise money.

Self Funding

  • The first option to start a business.
  • The entrepreneur uses his own money.
  • At a later stage, investors may get attracted.

Crowd Funding

  • The entrepreneur puts a detailed description of his business on a crowdfunding platform.
  • He mentions the goals of the business, plans for making a profit, how much funding he needs and for what reasons.
  • Investors read it and give money if they like the idea.
  • Those giving money make online pledges with the promise of pre-buying the product or giving a donation.
  • Anyone can contribute money toward a business they really believe in.

Angel Investment

  • Angel investors are individuals with surplus cash and a keen interest to invest in upcoming startups.
  • They have helped start many prominent companies, including Google, Yahoo and Alibaba.
  • This form of investing generally occurs in a company's early stages of growth.
  • They prefer to take more risk for higher returns.

Venture Capital

  • Venture capitals are professionally managed funds who invest in companies that have huge potential.

Bank Loans

  • Banks are the first place entrepreneurs go when thinking about funding.
  • Almost every bank in India offers finance through various programmes.
  • Leading banks — SBI, HDFC, ICICI and Axis — have 7-8 different options to offer collateral-free business loans.

Loans from NBFCs

  • NBFC = Non Banking Financial Company.
  • NBFCs provide services to those who do not have access to conventional banking services.
  • Increasingly popular for those whose requirements are limited and whose credit ratings are not favoured by a bank.
  • Benefits: flexible terms, faster approval, less paperwork.
  • Interest rate is generally higher than banks.

Government Programmes that Offer Startup Capital

The Government of India has launched a ₹10,000 crore Startup Fund in the Union Budget to improve the startup ecosystem in India.

Central Government Schemes

  • Bank of Ideas and Innovations programme — to boost innovative product companies.
  • Pradhan Mantri Micro Units Development and Refinance Agency Limited (MUDRA) — to extend benefits to 10 lakh small and medium enterprises.
  • SIDBI (Small Industries Development Bank of India) — also offers business loans to the MSME sector.

State Government Schemes

  • Kerala State Self Entrepreneur Development Mission (KSSEDM).
  • Maharashtra Centre for Entrepreneurship Development.
  • Rajasthan Startup Fest.

These schemes are set up to boost up small business in India.

Exam tip. When the question says "contribution, investment or loan from more than one person", the answer is Crowdfunding. Angel = a person. Venture capital = a professionally managed fund.

Section 4

Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)

The idea of this section. IPR protects what you create. It is the shield an innovative entrepreneur puts around an invention, a brand or a creative work.

4.1 Meaning and the Two Categories

What IPR does for an entrepreneur

  • IPR allows innovative entrepreneurs to protect their inventions.
  • It also gives an edge over your competitor.

The general definition

  • Intellectual Property (IP) is divided into two categories:
  1. Industrial property — includes inventions (patents), trademarks, industrial designs and geographic indications.
  2. Copyright — includes literary and artistic works. - IPR is the general term for the assignment of property rights on these assets.

│   ├── Trademarks
│   ├── Industrial Designs
│   └── Geographic Indications
└── Copyright
    └── Literary and artistic works

Show answers
  1. Intellectual Property Rights. It allows innovative entrepreneurs to protect their inventions and gives an edge over competitors.
  2. Industrial property — inventions (patents), trademarks, industrial designs, geographic indications. Copyright — literary and artistic works.

4.2 The Seven Types of IPR

Copyright

  • Protection gives the copyright holder the exclusive right to copy the work.
  • Right includes modifying it (creating "derivative works").
  • Right includes distributing, performing and displaying the work publicly.
  • Examples: films, music, songs, books, novels, paintings, drawings.

Patent

  • Patents protect processes, methods and inventions that are "novel", "non-obvious" and "useful".
  • If granted, a patent gives you a monopoly on selling, using, making or importing the invention.
  • The requirements for a patent are complex.
  • Examples: machines, devices, solutions.

Trademark

  • A trademark is like a brand name.
  • It is any word(s) or symbol(s) that represent a product to identify and distinguish it from other products in the marketplace.
  • Example: 'Nike' is the trademark of the Nike company.
  • Trademark gives legal protection that no one else can use this trademark.
  • Examples: logo, symbol, brand name.

Geographical Indication (GI)

  • A GI is primarily an indication that identifies agricultural, natural or manufactured products (handicrafts, industrial goods, food stuffs) originating from a definite territory.
  • A given quality, reputation or other feature must be essentially attributable to its geographical origin.

Plant Variety

  • Plant variety is essentially grouping plants into categories based on their botanical characteristics.

Design

  • A design includes shape, pattern and arrangement of lines, or colour combination, that is applied to any article.

Semiconductor Integrated Circuits Layout Design

  • A semiconductor layout design means the layout of transistors and other circuitry elements.
  • It includes lead wires connecting such elements.
  • It is expressed in any manner in semiconductor integrated circuits.

Memory hook. Seven types in order: Copyright → Patent → Trademark → GI → Plant variety → Design → Semiconductor layout. Read them as "C-P-T-G-P-D-S".

4.3 IPR as an Asset for the Entrepreneur

IPR is very important and beneficial for the innovative entrepreneur because:

  • It gives an edge over your competitor.

What is entrepreneurship? — definition decoded

Entrepreneurship is a systematic, purposeful and creative activity of identifying a need, mobilising resources and organising production, with a view to delivering value to customers, returns to investors and profits to the self, in accordance with the risks and uncertainties associated with business.

Part of the definitionWhat it tells you
Systematic, purposeful, creativePlanned work with a clear goal, not a lucky accident
Identifying a needIt starts from a real problem in the market
Mobilising resourcesLand, labour, capital and technology are gathered
Organising productionThose resources are turned into a running firm
Value to customersThe customer gains something useful
Returns to investorsThe people who put in money get their share
Profits for the selfThe entrepreneur earns the profit
Risks and uncertaintiesProfit is never guaranteed

The bigger picture. Entrepreneurship drives national growth and social development. Developing countries face the outflow of limited national wealth and a rising demand for consumer goods and services. Producing those goods locally solves both problems at once. The entrepreneur is the person behind this — he identifies the need, mobilises resources and organises production to earn profit.

Exam tip. For 1 mark, write the first half up to "organising production". For 3 marks, write the full line.

Why entrepreneurship matters — the three national needs

  1. Produce import substitute goods to stop the outflow of national wealth.
  2. Produce more consumer goods and services locally to meet growing demand.
  3. Produce goods for export to earn more and more foreign exchange.

All three needs are met by more and more entrepreneurs.

ReasonWhat it means

2 Provides innovationThe entrepreneur is an innovator — new methods of production, new technology, new ways of marketing. He develops new business ideas and puts them into action, enhancing economic development.
3 Growth of the economyEntrepreneurs adapt to changes in the environment. Changes inside enterprises bring changes in society and the economy, and the latest technology brings growth to the enterprise and the economy at large.

Five characteristics of entrepreneurship — SLI-OR

CharacteristicDetail

The 1951 definition — the historical test

Immediately after independence, small-scale industry was defined by the Industries Development and Regulation Act, 1951 as:

"Any enterprise which employs not more than 50 persons when using power and 100 when not using power and with capital assets not exceeding ₹5 lakh."

Capital assetsNot exceeding ₹5 lakh

Exam tip. This is the historical definition. The working definition today is investment + turnover. Write both when the question says "define small-scale industry".

MSME classification — investment + turnover

A business is classified as Micro, Small or Medium using two criteria together — investment and turnover. Both conditions must be satisfied.

CategoryInvestmentTurnover
Micro EnterpriseUp to ₹1 croreDoes not exceed ₹5 crore
Small EnterpriseMore than ₹1 crore but does not exceed ₹10 croreDoes not exceed ₹50 crore

Seven funding options for startups

  1. Self funding — the first option; the entrepreneur uses his own money. Later, investors may get attracted.
  2. Crowd funding — the entrepreneur puts a detailed description of the business on a platform: goals, plans for profit, how much funding is needed and why. Investors read it and give money if they like the idea, making online pledges with the promise of pre-buying the product or giving a donation. Anyone can contribute to a business they believe in.
  3. Angel investment — individuals with surplus cash and a keen interest in upcoming startups. They have helped start Google, Yahoo and Alibaba. This generally occurs in a company's early stages of growth, and angels prefer more risk for higher returns.
  4. Venture capital — professionally managed funds that invest in companies with huge potential.
  5. Bank loans — banks are the first place entrepreneurs go. Almost every bank offers finance; SBI, HDFC, ICICI and Axis have 7–8 different options for collateral-free business loans.
  6. Loans from NBFCs — NBFC = Non Banking Financial Company. They serve those without access to conventional banking and are increasingly popular among people whose requirements are limited and whose credit ratings are not favoured by a bank. Benefits are flexible terms, faster approval and less paperwork; the interest rate is generally higher than banks.
  7. Government programmes — the Government launched a ₹10,000 crore Startup Fund in the Union Budget to improve the startup ecosystem. These schemes are set up to boost small business in India.

  1. Who announced Startup India, when, and what is its aim?

    Show answer

    Prime Minister Mr. Narendra Modi announced it in his speech on 15 August 2015; it is a Government of India action plan to develop an eco-system to promote and nurture entrepreneurship across the country, and the Government offers various bank financing schemes.

  2. Define a startup using the four-part test.

    Show answer

    A startup is a private limited company, partnership, limited liability partnership or sole proprietor, registered in India, started less than five years ago, with an annual turnover of less than ₹25 crore.

  3. Which form of business is easy to forget in the startup definition, and what are the age and turnover limits?

    Show answer

    Sole proprietor is the form most often forgotten; the age limit is less than five years and the turnover limit is less than ₹25 crore.

  4. Explain the no-inspection benefit.

    Show answer

    A startup is not inspected for three years regarding labour laws and similar matters; it only needs self-certification.

  5. How does startup registration work?

    Show answer

    A mobile app launched in April 2016 and a portal allow a startup to register in one day, with a single window for clearance, approvals and registration.

  6. Explain the income tax exemption for startups.

    Show answer

    A startup gets income tax exemption for the first three years, but only after receiving a certificate from the inter-ministerial board set up for the purpose.

  7. What is the capital gains exemption, and what is the exam caution?

    Show answer

    A startup can claim capital gains tax exemption for the first three years; in NCERT the benefit under Section 54GB comes with conditions and a five-year lock-in, so the lecture line alone earns only partial marks.

  8. Give the patent-fee rebate and the two funding amounts.

    Show answer

    An 80% rebate in patent fees, a ₹10,000 crore fund for new startups and ₹500 crore for the credit guarantee scheme.

  9. Name the seven funding options in order.

    Show answer

    Self funding, crowd funding, angel investment, venture capital, bank loans, loans from NBFCs and government programmes.

  10. Explain self funding and crowdfunding.

    Show answer

    Self funding is the first option, where the entrepreneur uses his own money and later may attract investors; crowdfunding puts a detailed description of the business, its goals, profit plans and funding needs on a platform, and anyone can give money through online pledges promising to pre-buy the product or make a donation.

  11. Angel investor vs venture capitalist — and name three companies angels helped start.

    Show answer

    An angel investor is an individual with surplus cash who invests in a startup's early stages and takes more risk for higher returns, while a venture capitalist is a professionally managed fund investing in companies with huge potential; angels helped start Google, Yahoo and Alibaba.

  12. What do banks and NBFCs offer startups?

    Show answer

    Banks are the first place entrepreneurs go, and SBI, HDFC, ICICI and Axis offer seven to eight collateral-free business loan options; NBFCs serve people without conventional banking access, especially those with limited requirements or weak credit ratings, giving flexible terms, faster approval and less paperwork at a higher interest rate than banks.

  13. Name the three central government schemes for startup capital.

    Show answer

    The Bank of Ideas and Innovations programme for innovative product companies, MUDRA for extending benefits to 10 lakh small and medium enterprises, and SIDBI for business loans to the MSME sector.

  14. Name the three state government schemes and the one-line clue for crowdfunding.

    Show answer

    Kerala State Self Entrepreneur Development Mission, Maharashtra Centre for Entrepreneurship Development and Rajasthan Startup Fest; the clue for crowdfunding is contribution, investment or loan from more than one person.

Intellectual Property Rights

What IPR does — and the two categories

  • IPR allows innovative entrepreneurs to protect their inventions.
  • It also gives an edge over the competitor.

Intellectual Property (IP) is divided into two categories:

CategoryWhat it includes
Industrial propertyInventions (patents), trademarks, industrial designs and geographic indications
CopyrightLiterary and artistic works

IPR is the general term for the assignment of property rights on these assets.

Seven types of IPR — C-P-T-G-P-D-S

TypeWhat it protectsExample
CopyrightThe exclusive right to copy a work, including modifying it (creating derivative works) and distributing, performing and displaying it publiclyFilms, music, songs, books, novels, paintings, drawings
PatentProcesses, methods and inventions that are novel, non-obvious and useful; a granted patent gives a monopoly on selling, using, making or importing the invention. The requirements are complex.Machines, devices, solutions
TrademarkA brand name — any word(s) or symbol(s) that identify and distinguish a product in the marketplace, with legal protection so no one else can use itNike; logo, symbol, brand name

Recall drill — Session 4

  1. What does IPR stand for, and what two benefits does it give an entrepreneur?

    Show answer

    Intellectual Property Rights; it allows innovative entrepreneurs to protect their inventions and gives them an edge over the competitor.

  2. Name the two categories of Intellectual Property and what each includes.

    Show answer

    Industrial property, which includes inventions or patents, trademarks, industrial designs and geographic indications, and copyright, which includes literary and artistic works.

  3. What does the term IPR itself mean?

    Show answer

    IPR is the general term for the assignment of property rights on intellectual assets.

  4. What rights does copyright give, and what are its examples?

    Show answer

    The copyright holder gets the exclusive right to copy the work, including modifying it to create derivative works and distributing, performing and displaying it publicly; examples are films, music, songs, books, novels, paintings and drawings.

  5. What are the three conditions for a patent, and what monopoly does it give?

    Show answer

    The invention must be novel, non-obvious and useful; a granted patent gives a monopoly on selling, using, making or importing the invention, and its requirements are complex.

  6. What is a trademark, and what is the Nike example?

    Show answer

    A trademark is like a brand name — any word or symbol that represents a product to identify and distinguish it in the marketplace, with legal protection so no one else can use it; Nike is the trademark of the Nike company.

  7. What is a Geographical Indication?

    Show answer

    A GI identifies agricultural, natural or manufactured products such as handicrafts, industrial goods and food stuffs that originate from a definite territory, where a given quality, reputation or other feature is essentially attributable to that geographical origin, as with Darjeeling tea or Kashmir Pashmina.

  8. What is a plant variety?

    Show answer

    Plant variety is essentially the grouping of plants into categories based on their botanical characteristics.

  9. What does a design protect?

    Show answer

    A design includes the shape, pattern and arrangement of lines, or a colour combination, applied to any article.

  10. What is a semiconductor integrated circuits layout design?

    Show answer

    It is the layout of transistors and other circuitry elements, including the lead wires connecting those elements, expressed in any manner in semiconductor integrated circuits.

  11. Recite the seven types of IPR in order.

    Show answer

    Copyright, patent, trademark, geographical indication, plant variety, design and semiconductor integrated circuits layout design.

  12. How is IPR an asset for an innovative entrepreneur?

    Show answer

    It gives an edge over the competitor and helps the entrepreneur become a leader in his field; intellectual property includes copyrights, patents and trademarks.
