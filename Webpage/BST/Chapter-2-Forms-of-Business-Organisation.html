Key Terms — Read These First

The words that carry marks. Read these before the sections, then test yourself after.

Form of Business Organisation
The legal shape a business takes — sole proprietorship, HUF, partnership, cooperative society or company
Sole Proprietorship
Business owned, managed and controlled by one individual who takes all profits and bears all risks
HUF Business
Business found only in India, owned and carried on by members of the Hindu Undivided Family
Karta
Eldest male member of an HUF who manages and controls the HUF business; his decisions bind all members
Co-parceners
Members of an HUF holding equal ownership right over ancestral property
Dayabhaga
System of inheritance prevailing in West Bengal & Assam; both male and female members can be co-parceners
Mitakshara
System of inheritance prevailing all over India except West Bengal & Assam; only male members can be co-parceners
Partnership
Relation between persons who have agreed to share the profit of the business carried on by all or any one of them acting for all
Mutual Agency
Every partner acts as agent for the others and as principal bound by the others' acts
Active Partner
Partner who takes an active part in management, contributes capital and shares profits and losses
Sleeping Partner
Partner who contributes capital and shares profits but stays out of day-to-day management
Secret Partner
Partner who takes an active part in management while his identity stays unknown to the public
Nominal Partner
Partner who lends his name and goodwill only, without capital, management or profit share
Partner by Estoppel
Person who himself creates the impression of being a partner and is liable in the eyes of third parties
Partner by Holding Out
Person who knowingly allows himself to be represented as a partner and is liable to outsiders
Partnership at Will
Partnership whose life depends on the will of the partners; any partner can dissolve it by notice
Particular Partnership
Partnership formed for a particular project or a specified period; it dissolves on completion or expiry
General Partnership
Partnership in which every partner has unlimited liability and may take an active part in management
Limited Partnership
Partnership with at least one partner of unlimited liability; registration is compulsory
Partnership Deed
Written agreement stating the terms and conditions of the partnership
Registrar of Firms
Officer to whom a partnership firm applies for registration and issues the certificate
Cooperative Society
Voluntary association of persons joining for the welfare of members; an association of persons, and capital carries no extra voting weight
One-man-one-vote
Democratic control in a cooperative society — one vote per member, whatever the capital contributed
Joint Stock Company
Association of persons formed to carry on business, with a legal status independent of its members
Artificial Person
Company created by law — it holds rights and duties without any physical existence
Perpetual Succession
Company continues to exist despite the death, insolvency or exit of its members
Common Seal
The company's signature, used by directors to authorise its acts
One Person Company (OPC)
Company with a single member, incorporated as a private company — Sec 2(62)
Private Company
Company that restricts transfer of shares and stays within a fixed membership — Sec 2(68)
Public Company
Any company that is not a private company; it may invite subscription from the public — Sec 2(71)
Prospectus
Document inviting the general public to subscribe for shares; used by public companies
Memorandum of Association
The "Charter of the Company" — contains the powers and objects of the company
Articles of Association
Rules and regulations for the internal management of the company
Promoter
Person (individual, group or institution) who conceives the business idea and takes the initiatives to implement it
Section 1

Choosing the Form of Organisation

The idea of this section. Every business has to pick a legal form. This section names the five forms, lines them up side by side, gives the four difference tables examiners love, and lists the factors that settle the choice.

1.1 The Five Forms of Business Organisation

Business activities cannot be performed in isolation. Choosing the right form of organisation is one of the most important decisions while starting or expanding a business.

The best form is chosen by weighing the merits and limitations of each type against one's own requirements — capital, risk, control, continuity, secrecy.

The five forms at a glance

2Joint Hindu Family (HUF) BusinessFound only in India; owned and carried on by the members of the Hindu Undivided FamilyHindu Law — Hindu Succession Act, 1956
3PartnershipRelation between persons who have agreed to share the profit of the businessIndian Partnership Act, 1932
4Cooperative SocietyVoluntary association of persons joining for the welfare of membersCooperative Societies Act, 1912
5Joint Stock CompanyAssociation of persons with a legal status independent of its membersCompanies Act, 2013

Why each new form emerged

Sole proprietorship → too little capital & skills
        ↓
HUF → limited resources & Karta's dominance
        ↓
Partnership → limited resources & unlimited liability
        ↓
Cooperative (service motive)  &  Company (large-scale needs)

Each form grew out of the limits of the one before it. The chain ends with the cooperative society, which serves its members, and the company, which meets large-scale needs.

1.4 Factors That Affect the Choice of Form

The best form is chosen by weighing the merits and limitations of each type against one's own requirements. Nine factors decide it:

Seven key points to remember

  1. Cost & ease of setup – sole proprietorship cheapest; company most expensive.
  2. Liability – company & cooperative = limited; sole proprietorship & partnership = unlimited.
  3. Continuity – company & cooperative most stable; sole proprietorship most unstable.
  4. Management ability – company (professionals) vs sole proprietorship (one person does all).
  5. Capital – company can raise the most; sole proprietorship the least.
  6. Degree of control – want full control → sole proprietorship; okay sharing → partnership/company.
  7. Nature of business – personal contact needed → sole proprietorship; large-scale manufacturing → company; professional services → partnership.

Section 2

Sole Proprietorship and Joint Hindu Family Business

The idea of this section. The two oldest forms. One owner carries everything alone; the other spreads it across a family and places the eldest male member in charge of the family business.

2.1 Sole Proprietorship: Meaning and Features

Definition — learn this line word for word:

A form of business organisation which is owned, managed and controlled by an individual who is the recipient of all profits and bearer of all risks.

It is the oldest and simplest form of business organisation.

Features

FeatureDetail
Formation & ClosureVery easy to start; no legal formalities, no separate law governs it; can be dissolved at the will of the owner
LiabilityUnlimited – personal property may be sold to pay business debts if business assets are insufficient
Sole Risk Bearer & Profit RecipientOwns all business affairs; bears all risk alone; receives all profits as reward
ControlOne-man show; all decisions lie with the proprietor; no need to consult anyone
No Separate EntityNo legal distinction between firm and owner; owner is responsible for all business activities
Lack of Business ContinuityDeath, physical ailment or insolvency of owner directly affects business – may even close it

Merits

  • Quick decision making – complete freedom, no consultation needed.
  • Confidentiality of information – sole authority keeps business info secret.
  • Direct incentive – keeps all profits; no sharing.
  • Sense of accomplishment – personal satisfaction & confidence for self-employed.
  • Ease of formation and closure – minimum legal formalities.

Limitations

  • Limited resources – only personal savings + borrowings; small size, low growth rate.
  • Unlimited liability – creditors can recover dues from personal assets too.
  • Limited life of business – illness, death, insolvency of owner affects the business.
  • Limited managerial ability – one person cannot be expert in all areas.

Best suited for: small-scale businesses and personalised services — retail shops, beauty parlours, medical shops — where limited capital is needed and personal contact with customers matters.

2.2 Joint Hindu Family (HUF) Business: Members and Features

Definition:

A unique form of business organisation found only in India, owned and carried on by the members of the Hindu Undivided Family.

It is governed by the Hindu Law (Hindu Succession Act, 1956) and is one of the oldest forms in the country.

Key members

  • Karta (Manager) – the eldest male member; manages & controls the business; his decisions are binding on all members.
  • Co-parceners – all members having equal ownership right over ancestral property.
  • Membership basis = birth in the family; up to 3 successive generations can be members.

Two systems of inheritance (Hindu Law)

SystemWhere PrevailsWho Can Be Co-parcener
DayabhagaWest Bengal & Assam onlyBoth male and female members
MitaksharaAll over India except West Bengal & AssamOnly male members; right acquired by birth

Features

FeatureDetail
FormationAt least 2 members + ancestral property inherited; no agreement needed
LiabilityKarta = unlimited (self-acquired property can be attached) · Co-parceners = limited (to their share in coparcenary property)
ControlLies with Karta; decisions binding on all members
ContinuityNot affected by death of members; next eldest male becomes Karta
Minor MembersMinors can be members (inclusion by birth)

Merits

  • Effective control – Karta's absolute decision-making avoids conflicts.
  • Continued business existence – death of Karta doesn't stop business.
  • Limited liability of members – risk of co-parceners is well-defined and precise.
  • Increased loyalty & cooperation – family members work together.

Limitations

  • Limited resources – depends mainly on ancestral property; limited scope for expansion.
  • Unlimited liability of Karta – burdened with decisions + full liability.
  • Dominance of Karta – may cause conflict, even breakdown of the family unit.
  • Limited managerial skills – Karta can't be expert in all areas; unwise decisions → losses.

On the decline in modern India due to the shrinking joint-family system.

  2. Q2. Who manages and controls the HUF business, and on what basis is he appointed?

  2. The Karta, who is the eldest male member of the family.
  3. Limited — to the co-parcener's share in the coparcenary property. The Karta alone carries unlimited liability.
  4. The business is not affected by the death of members; the next eldest male becomes Karta.
Section 3

Partnership

The idea of this section. Partnership is the answer to the limits of sole proprietorship and HUF. This section covers its features, its six types of partners, its two kinds of duration and liability, and the deed and registration that hold it together.

3.1 Partnership: Meaning and Features

Definition (Indian Partnership Act, 1932):

"Partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profit of the business carried on by all or any one of them acting for all."

Partnership emerged from the limitations of Sole Proprietorship and HUF — it answers the needs of greater capital, varied skills and sharing of risks.

Features

FeatureDetail
FormationGoverned by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932; comes into existence through a legal agreement (terms, profit-sharing, manner of business)
LiabilityUnlimited – personal assets usable for debts; partners are liable jointly AND individually (a paying partner can recover proportionate share from others)
Risk BearingRisks borne as a team; profits & losses shared in agreed ratio
Decision-Making & ControlShared among partners; decisions by mutual consent
MembershipMin: 2 (Sec 464, Companies Act 2013) · Max: 50 (Rule 10, Companies (Miscellaneous) Rules, 2014)
Mutual AgencyEvery partner acts as agent (binds others) and principal (bound by others' acts)

Merits

  • Ease of formation & closure – agreement-based; can start even without registration.
  • Balanced decision-making – varied knowledge, skills & experience; division of work.
  • More funds – capital from many partners; larger resources than sole proprietorship.
  • Sharing of risks – reduces anxiety and burden on individual partners.
  • Secrecy – accounts are not required to be published.

Limitations

  • Unlimited liability – joint & individual; a wealthy partner may have to repay entire debts if others fail.
  • Limited resources – legal ceiling on partners restricts large-scale growth.
  • Possibility of conflicts – differences of opinion; one wrong decision can ruin all.
  • Lack of continuity – ends on death/insolvency/insanity of a partner (fresh agreement needed to continue).
  • Lack of public confidence – accounts not published; true financial status unknown.

  2. Every partner acts as agent for the other partners (binding them) and as principal (bound by the others' acts).
  3. Joint and several liability — partners are liable jointly AND individually.
  4. Any three of — ease of formation & closure; balanced decision-making; more funds; sharing of risks; secrecy.

Minor as partner

  • His liability is limited to his capital contribution, and he stays out of management.
  • On attaining majority he must decide within 6 months whether to remain a partner. If he gives no notice, he becomes a full partner with unlimited liability.

3.4 Partnership Deed and Registration of the Firm

Partnership Deed

The partnership deed is the written agreement specifying the terms & conditions of partnership. It usually covers:

  • Name of the firm.
  • Nature & location of business.
  • Duration of business.
  • Investment by each partner.
  • Distribution of profits & losses.
  • Duties & obligations of partners.
  • Salaries & withdrawals; interest on capital & drawings.
  • Procedure for dissolution.
  • Preparation & auditing of accounts.
  • Method of solving disputes.
  • Terms of admission, retirement & expulsion.

The agreement may be verbal or written; written is advisable as it is evidence of agreed conditions.

Registration of the firm

AspectDetail
NatureOptional but highly advisable; registration gives conclusive proof of existence
Procedure① Submit application to the Registrar of Firms (name, location, other business places, date of joining, names & addresses of partners, duration) → ② deposit fees → ③ entry in register → ④ certificate of registration
Consequences of non-registrationPartner cannot file suit against firm/other partners; firm cannot file suit against third parties; firm cannot file case against partners. Outsiders can sue the unregistered firm.

Section 4

Cooperative Society and Joint Stock Company

The idea of this section. Two forms that share the idea of a separate legal entity. The cooperative society works for its members; the company works for capital and scale. This section covers their features, their types and how a company is formed.

4.1 Cooperative Society: Meaning and Features

Definition:

A voluntary association of persons who join together with the motive of welfare of the members — an association of persons; voting power stays the same whatever capital a member contributes.

Registration under the Cooperative Societies Act, 1912 is compulsory. Minimum 10 adult persons are required.

Features

FeatureDetail
Voluntary MembershipOpen to anyone 18+ with common interest; no force, no bar on religion/caste/gender
Legal StatusSeparate legal entity after registration; can own property, make contracts, sue & be sued in own name; unaffected by entry/exit of members
Limited LiabilityLimited to capital contributed; max risk = subscribed share capital
ControlDemocratic pattern; members elect a managing committee; one-man-one-vote
Service MotiveFormed for service; surplus distributed as dividend

Merits

  • Equality in voting status – one vote per member irrespective of capital.
  • Limited liability – risk limited and known.
  • Stable existence – corporate body; not affected by death/insolvency/insanity of members.
  • Economy in operations – managed on honorary basis; eliminates middlemen.
  • Support from government – low taxes, subsidies, low interest loans.
  • Ease of formation – just 10 adult members; governed by Cooperative Societies Act, 1912.

Limitations

  • Limited resources – members have limited means; low dividend deters capital.
  • Inefficiency in management – can't pay experts; committee not professionally equipped.
  • Lack of secrecy – affairs discussed openly in meetings.
  • Government control – bound by rules on auditing, submission of accounts, etc.
  • Differences of opinion – internal quarrels; difficulty in decision-making.

4.3 Joint Stock Company: Meaning and Features

Definition:

An association of persons formed for carrying out business activities, having a legal status independent of its members.

It is the most suitable form for large-scale business and is governed by the Companies Act, 2013.

Features

FeatureDetail
Artificial PersonCreated by law – no body, soul, conscience; can't breathe/eat/run/talk, and it can own property, borrow, contract, sue & be sued
Separate Legal EntityOn incorporation, assets & liabilities are separate from owners
FormationTime-consuming, expensive, complicated; several documents & legal requirements; registration compulsory
Perpetual SuccessionNot affected by death/insolvency/entry/exit of members; "Members may come, members may go, but the company remains forever"; ends only through legal winding up

Merits

  • Limited liability – personal assets safe; debts settled only from company assets.
  • Transfer of interest – free transfer of shares avoids blockage of investment.
  • Perpetual existence – not affected by death/retirement/insolvency/insanity of members; liquidated only per Companies Act.
  • Scope for expansion – large financial resources; capital from public + banks.
  • Professional management – specialists head each department; balanced, rational decisions.

Limitations

  • Complexity in formation – time, effort, legal knowledge required.
  • Lack of secrecy – information filed with Registrar available to public.
  • Impersonal work environment – no direct link between effort and reward; no personal contact.
  • Numerous regulations – audit, voting, filing of reports, certificates; time-consuming & expensive.
  • Delay in decision-making – important decisions by Board/general meeting; opportunities lost.
  • Oligarchic management – democracy in theory, rule by a few in practice; scattered shareholders take little interest; directors may act against their interests.
  • Conflict of interests – employees want higher salaries, consumers want low prices, shareholders want high dividends.

4.4 Types of Companies and Privileges of a Private Company

A. One Person Company (OPC) — Sec 2(62)

  • Only one person as member; incorporated as a private company.
  • Avails benefits of private company: separate legal entity, protection of personal assets, perpetual succession.
  • Only a natural person, Indian citizen & resident (stay ≥ 182 days in the preceding calendar year) can incorporate or be the nominee.
  • One person cannot have more than one OPC or be the nominee in more than one.
  • No minor can be member or nominee.
  • It cannot be incorporated under Section 8, and it cannot make financial investments in other corporations.
  • It cannot convert voluntarily into any other kind of company until 2 years from incorporation, unless paid-up capital crosses ₹50 lakh or average annual turnover exceeds ₹2 crore.
  • The words "One Person Company" must appear in brackets below its name.

B. Private Company — Sec 2(68)

By its Articles of Association:

  • Restricts transfer of shares.
  • Min 2, Max 200 members (excluding past/present employees who are members).
  • Prohibits invitation to the public to subscribe for shares.
  • Must use "Private Limited" in its name.
  • Contravening any provision → it ceases to be a private company and loses all its privileges.

C. Public Company — Sec 2(71)

  • Any company which is not a private company.
  • Min 7 members, no maximum limit.
  • No restriction on transfer of shares.
  • Can invite subscription from the general public.
  • A private company which is a subsidiary of a public company is also treated as a public company.

Privileges of a private company over a public company

  • Can be started with just 2 members (public needs 7).
  • No prospectus needed.
  • Can allot shares without minimum subscription.
  • Can start work right after certificate of incorporation.
  • Only 2 directors needed (public needs 3).
  • No need to keep an index of members.
  • Can lend money or give guarantee to directors without government approval.

4.5 Formation of a Company

Formation of a company is complex & lengthy — unlike sole proprietorship or partnership. The 4 stages:

1. PROMOTION  →  2. INCORPORATION  →  3. CAPITAL SUBSCRIPTION  →  4. COMMENCEMENT OF BUSINESS
  • Stage 3 applies to public companies only.
  • A private company can start work right after incorporation.
  • Preliminary contracts are signed before incorporation.

Stage 1: Promotion

Identification of business opportunity, analysis of its prospects, steps to implement it.

The person performing these tasks = Promoter (individual, group or institution); conceives the idea and takes all initiatives.

Functions of a Promoter (6 Steps):

  1. Identification of Business Opportunity – new business / expansion / merger; preliminary analysis of profitability, risk, resources.
  2. Feasibility Studies (before investing money): - Technical – can it be implemented? (technology, raw materials availability) - Financial – can funds be arranged within available means? - Economic – will it be profitable?
  3. Name Approval – select name, get it approved from the Registrar of Companies; it must not be identical to or too close to an existing name (3 names are given in order of preference).
  4. Fixing up Signatories to Memorandum of Association – signatories usually become first directors; written consent to act as director & buy qualification shares; MOA signed by min 7 persons (public) / 2 persons (private).
  5. Appointment of Professionals – merchant bankers, auditors, etc. to prepare & submit documents.
  6. Preparation of Necessary Documents – MOA, AOA, consent of directors, etc.

Stage 2: Incorporation — documents to the Registrar

Contents of MOA (6 Clauses):

  1. Name Clause – name of company.
  2. Registered Office Clause – state where registered office is situated.
  3. Objects Clause – purpose; company can't do business not authorised by it (divided into Main Objects and Other Objects).
  4. Liability Clause – liability limited to unpaid amount on shares.

5.2 Case Study with Model Answer

Suresh has run a fruit business alone for several years from a single stall. He knows every customer by name and carries the day's stock on his own cart. Business has grown, and his single cart can no longer hold the stock. Two of his cousins, who belong to the same joint Hindu family and hold a share in the ancestral property, offer to join him and bring in capital. Suresh also wants someone who will only put in money and stay out of day-to-day work, and a respected trader who is willing to lend his name and goodwill to the firm. One supplier has agreed to give the firm regular credit terms. A large retailer now wants a much bigger quantity every month than the stall can supply, and Suresh's accountant tells him that a company could be registered so that more people could invest and the business could continue even if a member leaves.

Questions

  • (a) Which form of organisation is Suresh running his fruit business as today, and why is this the oldest and simplest form?
  • (b) Which form of organisation should Suresh consider for the capital and shared risk from his cousins? Name the Act that governs it and state the maximum number of partners allowed.
  • (c) Which types of partners would suit the person who only contributes money and stays out of day-to-day work, and the trader who lends his name and goodwill?
  • (d) Suresh's accountant mentions a company. State one feature of a company that protects his personal assets and one feature that keeps the business running even if a member leaves.

Model answer

  • (a) Sole Proprietorship. It is the oldest and simplest form of business organisation because it is owned, managed and controlled by a single individual who receives all profits and bears all risks. It needs no legal formalities, no separate law governs it, and the owner can dissolve it at will.
  • (b) Partnership. It is governed by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 and comes into existence through a legal agreement covering terms, profit-sharing and the manner of business. It brings together greater capital, varied skills and sharing of risks. The maximum number of partners allowed is 50 (minimum 2). Its liability is unlimited, with partners liable jointly and individually, and the firm can start even before registration.
  • (c) The person who contributes money and stays out of day-to-day work is a Sleeping (Dormant) Partner — he contributes capital and shares profits but has no day-to-day role. The trader who lends his name and goodwill is a Nominal Partner — he gives his name and goodwill only, without capital, management or a share of profits. Both carry unlimited liability.
  • (d) Protection of personal assets: the liability of a company member is limited to the unpaid amount on the shares held, so the company's debts are settled from company assets and the members' personal property stays safe. Continuity: the company enjoys perpetual succession — it is not affected by the death, insolvency or exit of its members and ends only through legal winding up.

Recall drill — Session 3

  1. Define partnership and explain why it emerged.

    Show answer

    Partnership is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profit of the business carried on by all or any one of them acting for all, under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932.   2. State the six features of partnership.

    Show answer

    It is governed by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 and comes into existence through a legal agreement covering terms, profit-sharing and the manner of business. Liability is unlimited, with partners liable jointly and individually, and a paying partner can recover a proportionate share from the others. Risks are borne as a team and profits and losses are shared in an agreed ratio. Decision-making is shared by mutual consent. Membership is minimum 2 under Sec 464 of the Companies Act 2013 and maximum 50 under Rule 10 of the Companies (Miscellaneous) Rules 2014. Mutual agency makes every partner an agent binding the others and a principal bound by their acts.

  3. What is mutual agency, and what does joint and several liability mean?

    Show answer

    Mutual agency means every partner acts as an agent for the other partners, binding them by his acts, and as a principal who is bound by the acts of the others. Joint and several liability means the partners are liable together and also individually, so a partner who has paid the whole firm's debt may recover a proportionate share from the other partners.

  4. Name the five merits of partnership.

    Show answer

    Ease of formation and closure, because it is agreement-based and can start even without registration; balanced decision-making, because partners bring varied knowledge, skills and experience and divide the work; more funds, because capital comes from many partners; sharing of risks, which reduces anxiety and the burden on each partner; and secrecy, because accounts are not required to be published.

  5. Name the five limitations of partnership.

    Show answer

    Unlimited liability, which is joint and individual, so a wealthy partner may have to repay entire debts if others fail; limited resources, because the legal ceiling on partners restricts large-scale growth; possibility of conflicts, because differences of opinion mean one wrong decision can ruin all; lack of continuity, because the firm ends on death, insolvency or insanity of a partner and a fresh agreement is needed; and lack of public confidence, because accounts are not published and the true financial status is unknown.

    Show answer

    All three contribute capital and share profits with unlimited liability. The active partner takes an active part in management and is publicly known. The sleeping partner stays out of day-to-day management but is known. The secret partner takes an active part in management while his identity stays unknown to the public.

  8. Who is a nominal partner, and how is estoppel different from holding out?

    Show answer

    A nominal partner lends his name and goodwill only, without capital, management or a share of profits, yet carries unlimited liability. In estoppel the person himself creates the impression of being a partner, while in holding out the person knowingly allows himself to be represented as a partner; both are liable to outsiders.

  9. Can a minor be a partner? State the rules and the 6-month window.

    Show answer

Three types of companies

A. One Person Company (OPC) — Sec 2(62). Only one person as member, incorporated as a private company with benefits of separate legal entity, protection of personal assets and perpetual succession. Only a natural person, Indian citizen and resident staying at least 182 days in the preceding calendar year can incorporate it or be the nominee; one person cannot have more than one OPC or be nominee in more than one, and no minor can be a member or nominee. It cannot be incorporated under Section 8 or make financial investments in other corporations. It cannot convert voluntarily for 2 years from incorporation unless paid-up capital crosses ₹50 lakh or average annual turnover exceeds ₹2 crore, and the words "One Person Company" must appear in brackets below its name.

B. Private Company — Sec 2(68). By its Articles of Association it restricts transfer of shares, has minimum 2 and maximum 200 members excluding past and present employees who are members, prohibits invitation to the public to subscribe for shares, and must use "Private Limited" in its name. On contravening any provision, it ceases to be a private company and loses all privileges.

C. Public Company — Sec 2(71). Any company which is not a private company, with minimum 7 members and no maximum, no restriction on transfer of shares, and the right to invite subscription from the public. A private company which is a subsidiary of a public company is also treated as a public company.

Privileges of a private company. It can start with just 2 members against 7; needs no prospectus; can allot shares without minimum subscription; can start work right after incorporation; needs only 2 directors against 3; need not keep an index of members; and can lend money or give a guarantee to directors without government approval.

Recall drill — Session 4

  1. Define a cooperative society and state its Act and minimum membership.

    Show answer

    A cooperative society is a voluntary association of persons who join together with the motive of welfare of the members; it is an association of persons, and voting power stays the same whatever capital a member contributes. Registration under the Cooperative Societies Act, 1912 is compulsory, and a minimum of 10 adult persons is required.

  2. State the five features of a cooperative society.

    Show answer

    Membership is voluntary and open to anyone 18 and above with a common interest, with no bar on religion, caste or gender. It has a separate legal entity after registration, so it can own property, make contracts and sue or be sued, and is unaffected by the entry or exit of members. Liability is limited to the capital contributed, with the maximum risk being the subscribed share capital. Control is democratic through an elected managing committee on one-man-one-vote. Its motive is service, and surplus is distributed as dividend.

  3. Name the six merits and five limitations of a cooperative society.

    Show answer

    The merits are equality in voting status, limited liability, stable existence, economy in operations because it is managed on an honorary basis and eliminates middlemen, support from government through low taxes, subsidies and low-interest loans, and ease of formation with just 10 adult members under the 1912 Act. The limitations are limited resources because members have limited means and low dividend deters capital, inefficiency in management because experts cannot be paid, lack of secrecy because affairs are discussed openly, government control through rules on auditing and accounts, and differences of opinion that make decision-making difficult.

  4. Name the six types of cooperative societies with one purpose each.

    Show answer

    Consumers' societies buy in bulk for good quality products at reasonable prices. Producers' or industrial societies help small producers procure inputs, with one type supplying raw materials and equipment and the other selling members' produced goods. Marketing societies sell small producers' output through one centralised agency and perform transport, warehousing and packaging. Farmers' or agricultural societies jointly take up farming and provide seeds, fertilisers and machinery while consolidating fragmented holdings. Credit societies give short-term loans at low interest and protect members from exploitative lenders. Housing societies help people with limited income construct houses or obtain plots at reasonable cost, with payment in instalments.

  5. Define a joint stock company and state its nine features.

    Show answer

    A joint stock company is an association of persons formed for carrying out business activities, having a legal status independent of its members, under the Companies Act, 2013. It is an artificial person created by law with no body, soul or conscience, and a separate legal entity whose assets and liabilities are separate from its owners. Formation is time-consuming, expensive and complicated with compulsory registration. It has perpetual succession, unaffected by the death, insolvency, entry or exit of members, ending only through legal winding up. Control rests with the Board of Directors while ownership rests with shareholders. Liability is limited to the unpaid amount on shares. It acts through directors using the common seal as its signature. Risk is spread over many shareholders, and shares are transferable in the open market or stock exchange.

  6. Explain artificial person, perpetual succession and the ₹1,500 liability illustration.

    Show answer

    An artificial person is created by law, has no body, soul or conscience, cannot breathe, eat, run or talk, yet can own property, borrow, contract, sue and be sued. Perpetual succession means the company continues despite the death, insolvency, entry or exit of members — "Members may come, members may go, but the company remains forever" — and ends only through legal winding up. Liability is limited to the unpaid amount on shares, so a holder of 500 shares of ₹10 each with ₹7 paid carries ₹3 × 500 = ₹1,500.

  7. Name the five merits and seven limitations of a company.

    Show answer

    The merits are limited liability, which keeps personal assets safe; transfer of interest, which avoids blockage of investment; perpetual existence; scope for expansion through large resources from the public and banks; and professional management by specialists. The limitations are complexity in formation; lack of secrecy because information filed with the Registrar is public; an impersonal work environment with no link between effort and reward; numerous regulations on audit, voting and filing; delay in decision-making through the Board or general meeting; oligarchic management where scattered shareholders lose interest and directors may act against them; and conflict of interests among employees, consumers and shareholders.

  8. State the OPC rules from Sec 2(62), including 182 days and the 2-year lock-in.

    Show answer

    An OPC has only one person as member and is incorporated as a private company, getting the benefits of separate legal entity, protection of personal assets and perpetual succession. Only a natural person who is an Indian citizen and resident staying at least 182 days in the preceding calendar year can incorporate it or be the nominee; one person cannot have more than one OPC or be nominee in more than one, and no minor can be a member or nominee. It cannot be incorporated under Section 8 or make financial investments in other corporations. It cannot convert voluntarily for 2 years from incorporation unless paid-up capital crosses ₹50 lakh or average annual turnover exceeds ₹2 crore, and the words "One Person Company" must appear in brackets below its name.

  9. Define private and public companies and compare them on members, directors, deposits, transfer, invitation and name.

    Show answer

    A private company, under Sec 2(68), restricts transfer of shares, has 2 to 200 members excluding past and present employees who are members, prohibits public invitation and uses "Private Limited" in its name. A public company, under Sec 2(71), is any company that is not private, has minimum 7 members with no maximum, does not restrict transfer, can invite public subscription and uses "Limited"; a private company that is a subsidiary of a public company is treated as public. On directors, an OPC needs 1 to 15, a private company 2 and a public company 3. OPC and private cannot invite public deposits or public subscription, while public can invite both shares and debentures.

  10. Name the seven privileges of a private company.

    Show answer

    It can start with just 2 members against 7 for a public company; it needs no prospectus; it can allot shares without minimum subscription; it can start work right after the certificate of incorporation; it needs only 2 directors against 3; it need not keep an index of members; and it can lend money or give a guarantee to directors without government approval.

  11. Name the four stages of company formation and the promoter's six functions.

    Show answer

    The stages are promotion, incorporation, capital subscription and commencement of business; capital subscription applies to public companies only, because it involves inviting the public through a prospectus. The promoter identifies a business opportunity through a new business, expansion or merger, studies technical, financial and economic feasibility, gets name approval from the Registrar of Companies with 3 names in order of preference, fixes MOA signatories who usually become first directors and consent to buy qualification shares with 7 in a public company and 2 in a private company, appoints professionals such as merchant bankers and auditors, and prepares the MOA, AOA and consent of directors.

  12. Name the incorporation documents, the six MOA clauses and the MOA vs AOA differences.

    Show answer

    The incorporation documents are the MOA, the AOA, the consent of proposed directors, any agreement for a Managing Director, Whole-time Director or Manager, a statutory declaration signed by an Advocate of the High Court or Supreme Court, a practising CA or CS, a director named in the articles or the manager or secretary, and the filing and registration fees based on authorised share capital. The six MOA clauses are name, registered office, objects divided into main and other objects, liability limited to the unpaid amount on shares, capital showing authorised capital such as ₹2 crore divided into 20 lakh shares of ₹10, and association with signatories consenting to buy qualification shares. The MOA defines objectives and is the main document with compulsory filing, while the AOA contains internal management rules, is subsidiary, and a public company may adopt Table F; altering the MOA is difficult and needs government approval, while the AOA can be altered by special resolution, and acts beyond the MOA are invalid even if unanimously ratified while acts beyond the AOA can be ratified if they do not violate the MOA.
