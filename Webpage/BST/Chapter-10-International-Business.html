Section 1

International Business: Meaning, Nature, Scope and Problems

The idea of this section. International business is business that crosses a national border. This part defines it, separates it from international trade, shows the three types of trade, explains its nature and scope, and lists the five problems that make it harder than doing business at home.

Source note. The five lecture transcripts provide the exam framework: definitions, lists, procedures, documents and MCQs. The current NCERT chapter is used to complete the scope, the modes of entry and the precise textbook definitions. Where the lecture simplifies a rule, the textbook position is flagged in a Source note so you write the accurate line in the exam.

1.1 What Is International Business?

The definition — learn this line word for word:

International business refers to the buying and selling of goods and services beyond the geographical limits of a country. It is also called trade between two countries.

The textbook definition — write this when the question says "international business", not "international trade":

International business refers to business activities that take place across national frontiers. It involves not only the international movement of goods and services, but also of capital, personnel, technology and intellectual property such as patents, trademarks, know-how and copyrights.

The one idea that decides most answers:

International business is a much broader term than international trade. It includes trade in goods and services and production, marketing and investment in foreign countries.

Domestic business, for contrast:

Business transaction taking place within the geographical boundaries of a nation is known as domestic or national business. It is also called internal business or home trade.

1.2 The Three Types of International Trade

International trade is of three types. The first two are direct trade; the third is re-export trade.

TypeSimple meaningWho does what
ExportSelling goods or services to foreign countriesThe seller is the exporter; the foreign buyer is the importer
ImportBuying goods or services from foreign countriesThe buyer at home is the importer; the foreign seller is the exporter
Entrepot (Re-export)Importing goods not for consumption in the home country but for exporting them to another countryThe home country acts as a trading base between two other countries

Entrepot in one example. A trader in India imports toys from China and re-exports them to Sri Lanka without selling them in India. The goods entered India only to be sent onward — that is entrepot trade.

The three types in one line each:

  • Export — goods leave the home country.
  • Import — goods enter the home country.
  • Entrepot — goods enter the home country only to leave again.

Exam tip. Do not confuse entrepot trade with ordinary import. In an ordinary import the goods are consumed or used in the home country; in entrepot trade they are imported for re-export.

1.3 Nature of International Business

The lecture gives two main features of the nature of external trade.

#FeatureWhat it means
1Involvement of two countriesIn international business, a minimum of two countries is involved. Buying and selling across the borders of a country is external trade. Trading between two far-off cities of the same country is not international business
2Payment in foreign currencyEach country has its own currency. Payment for imports is made in foreign currency, and payment for exports is also received in foreign currency

The border test. The test is not distance; it is the border. Delhi to Mumbai is internal trade even though the distance is long. Delhi to Kathmandu is international business even though the distance is short.

The currency point. The lecture treats payment in foreign currency as a feature of external trade. The decisive test is still the national border: a transaction across a border is international business even if the parties choose to settle it in a currency that is not the importer's or exporter's own.

A third point the textbook adds. Cross-border business also involves the movement of capital, personnel, technology and intellectual property — not just goods and services. This is why international business is wider than a simple export sale.

1.4 Scope of International Business

The scope of international business is wider than trade. The textbook lists four major forms of business operations that fall within it.

(i) Merchandise Exports and Imports

  • Merchandise means goods that are tangible — they can be seen and touched.
  • Merchandise exports means sending tangible goods abroad.
  • Merchandise imports means bringing tangible goods from a foreign country into one's own country.
  • Merchandise trade is also called trade in goods. It includes only tangible goods and excludes trade in services.

(ii) Service Exports and Imports

  • Service exports and imports involve trade in intangibles.
  • Because services are intangible, trade in services is also called invisible trade.
  • The services traded internationally include tourism and travel, boarding and lodging, entertainment and recreation, transportation, professional services, communication, construction and engineering, marketing, educational services and financial services.
  • Tourism, transportation and business services are major constituents of world trade in services.

(iii) Licensing and Franchising

  • Licensing permits a party in a foreign country to produce and sell goods under your trademarks, patents or copyrights in return for a fee.
  • Franchising is similar to licensing but applies to services.
  • Example: Pepsi and Coca-Cola are produced and sold all over the world by local bottlers under licensing; McDonald's operates through franchising.

(iv) Foreign Investments

  • Foreign investment means investment of funds abroad in exchange for a financial return.
  • Direct investment (FDI) takes place when a company directly invests in plant, machinery or a foreign company with a view to undertaking production and marketing there. It gives the investor a controlling interest.
  • Portfolio investment is investment in shares, bonds, bills or notes, or a loan to a foreign firm. The investor earns dividends or interest but does not get directly involved in production and marketing.

The scope in one line:

International business = merchandise trade + invisible trade + licensing and franchising + foreign investment.

Service exports and importsTrade in intangibles; invisible tradeAn Indian software firm serves a US client; a tourist travels to India

1.5 Problems of International Business

The lecture lists five problems. Each one is a reason why international business is more difficult than internal trade.

1. Legal Procedures

  • Import or export of goods involves a lengthy and complicated procedure.
  • Prior permission of the government has to be obtained before exporting or importing goods or services.
  • The exporter or importer has to deal with customs, licensing authorities, banks and other agencies.

2. Restrictions

  • International business is not as free as internal trade.
  • For several items, an export or import licence has to be obtained.
  • Many restrictions are imposed by the government on the export or import of certain items.

3. High Risk

  • The risk in international business is much higher than in internal trade.
  • Because of long distances, there is a risk of damage to goods in transit.
  • There is also the risk of fluctuation in the currency conversion rate.
  • The exporter must take a marine insurance policy before sending goods by ship.

4. Different Languages

  • Different languages are spoken and written in different countries.
  • Traders must appoint someone who can read and understand the foreign language to read the price list, terms and conditions of trade, and correspondence.

5. Arrangement of Foreign Currency

  • For making payment for imports, the importer has to arrange foreign currency.
  • This involves a lengthy procedure because foreign exchange transactions are regulated.

The five problems in one line each:

  1. Legal procedures — long paperwork and government permission.
  2. Restrictions — licences and controls on many items.
  3. High risk — distance, damage, currency fluctuation.
  4. Different languages — translation and interpretation.
  5. Foreign currency — arranging it takes time and procedure.

Section 2

Why Nations Trade: Reasons, Benefits and Objectives

The idea of this section. No country can produce everything it needs equally well or cheaply. This part explains why nations trade, what nations and firms gain from it, and the specific objectives that guide export trade and import trade.

2.1 Reasons for International Business

The lecture gives five reasons. They all point to one conclusion: countries specialise in what they produce best and trade for the rest.

#ReasonWhat it means
1Countries cannot produce equally well or cheaply all that they needEvery country has limits on what it can produce efficiently
2Unequal distribution of natural resourcesSome countries have oil, some have minerals, some have fertile land; no country has everything
3Different availability of factors of productionLand, labour, capital and raw materials differ from nation to nation
4Difference in labour productivity and production costSocio-economic, geographical and political reasons make costs differ across countries
5No single country can produce better quality at lower cost in everythingEach country is best at some things and weaker at others

The conclusion the lecture draws:

Each country finds it advantageous to produce those selected goods or services that it can produce more effectively and efficiently, and to procure the rest through trade with other countries that can produce them at lower cost.

Geographical specialisation — the textbook explanation

  • International business today is largely the result of geographical specialisation.
  • The same principle explains domestic trade between states or regions within a country.
  • West Bengal specialises in jute products; Mumbai and neighbouring areas of Maharashtra are more involved in cotton textiles.
  • At the international level, labour-abundant developing countries specialise in producing and exporting garments. They lack capital and technology, so they import textile machinery from developed nations that can produce it more efficiently.

The firm-level reason. Firms engage in international business to import what is available at lower prices in other countries and to export goods to countries where they can fetch better prices. Price is the first consideration; the benefits in 2.2 and 2.3 are the additional pull.

Exam tip. A "reasons for international business" answer is not a list of benefits. The reasons explain why trade exists — unequal resources, different factor availability, different productivity and cost. The benefits explain what the country or firm gains — foreign exchange, growth, profits. Keep the two separate.

2.2 Benefits to Nations

The lecture gives four benefits to nations or countries.

1. Earning of Foreign Exchange

  • International business helps a country earn foreign exchange.
  • This foreign exchange can be used to import capital goods, technology, petroleum products, fertilisers and other products that may not be available domestically.

2. More Efficient Use of Resources

  • Every country has some natural resources — minerals, labour, technology, water, and so on.
  • Every country tries to use these resources in the best possible manner.
  • Over time, countries become more efficient and specialised in using those resources than other countries.
  • The textbook principle: produce what your country can produce more efficiently, and trade the surplus for what others can produce more efficiently.
  • When countries trade on this principle, they end up producing much more than if each tried to produce everything on its own.

3. Improving Growth Prospects and Employment Potential

  • External trade boosts economic growth because firms in developing countries increase their production capacity to supply goods to foreign countries.
  • It creates employment directly and indirectly.
  • Increased production raises the demand for labour.
  • Export-oriented industries also create jobs in forwarding agencies, clearing agencies and related services.
  • The textbook cites Singapore, South Korea and China, which adopted the strategy "export and flourish" and became star performers.

4. Increases Standard of Living

  • Without international trade, the world community could not use goods and services produced in other countries.
  • People in developing and underdeveloped countries can use these products and raise their standard of living.

The four benefits in one line each:

  1. Foreign exchange — earn it and use it for imports.
  2. Efficient use of resources — specialise and produce more.
  3. Growth and employment — produce for foreign markets, create jobs.
  4. Standard of living — consume a wider range of goods.

2.3 Benefits to Firms

The lecture gives five benefits to firms.

1. Prospects for Higher Profit

  • International business is generally more profitable than domestic business.
  • When domestic prices are low, a firm can sell at a higher price in countries where prices are high.

2. Increased Capacity Utilisation

  • Firms may have production capacity that is in excess of domestic demand.
  • Overseas orders let them use this surplus capacity.
  • Producing on a larger scale gives economies of scale, which lowers cost per unit and improves profit margin.

3. Prospects for Growth

  • When demand for a product starts getting saturated in the domestic market, firms can grow by entering overseas markets.
  • This is the main motivation for many MNCs from developed countries to enter developing markets.

4. Way Out from Intense Competition in the Domestic Market

  • A highly competitive domestic market drives many companies to go international in search of markets.
  • International business acts as a catalyst of growth for firms facing tough conditions at home.

5. Improved Business Vision

  • The vision to become international comes from the urge to grow, the need to be more competitive, the need to diversify and the desire to gain strategic and technical advantages.
  • Many companies include internationalisation as a main objective and strategy.

The five benefits in one line each:

  1. Higher profit — sell where prices are better.
  2. Capacity utilisation — use idle capacity and gain economies of scale.
  3. Growth — escape a saturated home market.
  4. Escape intense competition — find new markets.
  5. Improved vision — become more competitive and strategic.

Exam tip. The two "growth" ideas are different. Benefit to nation is growth of the economy and employment. Benefit to firm is growth of the business when the domestic market is saturated. Do not mix the two columns.

2.4 Objectives of Export Trade

The lecture gives seven objectives of export trade.

#ObjectiveWhat the lecture says
1To sell surplus goodsA country may produce more than it needs at home; the surplus can be exported
2To make better utilisation of resourcesProducing goods on a large scale for export allows better use of resources
3To earn foreign exchangeSelling goods in foreign markets earns foreign exchange, which can buy capital goods and technology
4To increase national incomeExport earnings raise national income, which improves the standard of living and quality of life
5To generate employmentGrowth of export trade creates employment opportunities for a large number of people

The seven objectives in one line each:

  1. Sell the surplus.
  2. Use resources better.
  3. Earn foreign exchange.
  4. Raise national income.
  5. Generate employment.
  6. Increase government revenue.
  7. Build international understanding and cooperation.

3.2 International Business vs Domestic Business

Conducting international business is more complex than domestic business because of variations in political, social, cultural and economic environments. The textbook gives eight key areas of difference.

The eight differences in detail

1. Nationality of buyers and sellers. In domestic business, both parties are from the same country, so they understand each other more easily. In international business, they come from different countries; differences in language, attitudes, social customs and business goals make interaction and finalisation of deals harder.

2. Nationality of other stakeholders. Stakeholders include employees, suppliers, shareholders, partners and the general public. In domestic business they belong to one country and share a common value system. In international business, the firm must take into account a wider set of values and aspirations from different nations, so decision-making becomes more complex.

3. Mobility of factors of production. Labour and capital move more freely within a country. Across countries there are legal restrictions, and socio-cultural, geographic and economic differences also restrict movement. Labour finds it especially difficult to adjust to a different climate, economy and culture.

4. Customer heterogeneity across markets. Buyers in different countries differ in tastes, fashions, languages, beliefs, customs, attitudes and product preferences. The textbook examples:

  • People in China prefer bicycles, while the Japanese like to ride bikes.
  • People in India use right-hand-drive cars; Americans drive cars with the steering and brakes on the left.
  • People in the United States change consumer durables within two to three years; Indians use them until they are totally worn out.

These differences make product design and marketing strategy much more complicated across borders.

5. Differences in business systems and practices. Countries differ in socio-economic development, availability and cost of economic infrastructure, market support services, business customs and practices. Firms must adapt their production, finance, human resource and marketing plans to the conditions of the foreign market.

6. Political system and risks. The type of government, party system, political ideology and political risks affect business. A domestic business person knows the home political environment; an international firm must make special efforts to understand the foreign political environment and monitor changes. A common problem is the tendency of nations to favour their own products over foreign products.

7. Business regulations and policies. Each country has its own business laws and regulations. Tariff and taxation policies, import quotas, subsidies and other controls differ across nations and often discriminate against foreign products, services and capital.

8. Currency used in business transactions. International business uses different currencies, and exchange rates keep fluctuating. This makes it difficult to fix prices and to hedge against foreign exchange risk.

Memory hook for the eight bases. N-N-M-C-B-P-R-C — Nationality of buyers and sellers, Nationality of other stakeholders, Mobility, Customer heterogeneity, Business systems, Political system, Regulations, Currency.

Exam tip. The first two bases are about people, the next two are about markets and factors, the next two are about systems and politics, and the last two are about rules and money. That grouping keeps all eight in order.

3.3 Exporting and Importing

The definitions:

Exporting refers to sending goods and services from the home country to a foreign country. Importing is the purchase of foreign products and bringing them into one's home country.

There are two ways of exporting or importing: direct and indirect.

Advantages of Exporting and Importing

  1. Easiest way to enter international markets. It is less complex than setting up and managing joint ventures or wholly owned subsidiaries abroad.
  2. Less involving. The firm does not need to invest as much time and money as it would for a joint venture or a manufacturing plant in a host country.
  3. Low foreign investment risk. Since there is little or no investment in foreign countries, exposure to foreign investment risk is nil or much lower than in other modes.

Limitations of Exporting and Importing

  1. Additional costs. Goods physically move from one country to another, so there are extra packaging, transportation and insurance costs. Heavy items face high transport costs. On arrival, goods are subject to customs duty and other levies. Together, these make the product less competitive.
  2. Not feasible when import restrictions exist. If a foreign country restricts imports, the firm may have to choose licensing, franchising or a joint venture to produce and market locally.
  3. Little contact with the foreign market. Export firms produce at home and ship abroad. Apart from a few visits by executives, they do not have much contact with foreign customers, which puts them at a disadvantage compared with local firms that are close to customers.

The textbook's conclusion. Despite these limitations, exporting and importing is the most preferred way for firms getting initially involved in international business. Firms usually start with exports and imports, gain familiarity with foreign markets, and then switch to other modes.

Exam tip. A question asking "Why is exporting a better way of entering international markets than setting up a wholly owned subsidiary?" wants the three advantages above: easiest, less time and money, and low foreign investment risk. Add the limitations of a wholly owned subsidiary for contrast.

3.4 Contract Manufacturing

The definition:

Contract manufacturing refers to a type of international business where a firm enters into a contract with one or a few local manufacturers in foreign countries to get certain components or goods produced as per its specifications. It is also known as outsourcing.

The three major forms

FormWhat happensExample

Assembly of componentsComponents are assembled into final productsHard disk, motherboard, floppy disk drive and modem chip assembled into computers
Complete manufactureThe entire product is manufactured by the local firmGarments

How it works. The goods are produced or assembled by local manufacturers as per the technology and management guidance provided by the foreign company. The goods are delivered to the international firm for use in its final products, or sold as finished products under the international firm's brand names in the home, host or other countries.

Examples from the textbook. Nike, Reebok, Levis and Wrangler get their products or components produced in developing countries under contract manufacturing. In India, the Godrej group manufactures soaps under contract for many multinationals, including Dettol soap for Reckitt and Colman; this uses its excess soap-making capacity.

Advantages of Contract Manufacturing

  1. Large-scale production without setting up a plant. The international firm gets goods produced on a large scale without investing in production facilities; it uses the facilities already existing in the foreign country.
  2. Little or no investment risk. Since there is no or little investment in the foreign country, there is hardly any foreign investment risk.
  3. Lower production cost. The firm can get products manufactured or assembled at lower cost, especially where local material and labour costs are lower.
  4. Better use of the local producer's capacity. Local producers with idle capacity get a ready market for their output and greater utilisation of their production capacity.
  5. Local producer enters international business. The local manufacturer gets involved with international business and can avail incentives available to export firms if the goods are delivered to the international firm's home country or another foreign country.

Limitations of Contract Manufacturing

  1. Quality and design risk. Local firms might not adhere to production design and quality standards, causing serious product quality problems for the international firm.
  2. Loss of control for the local manufacturer. The local manufacturer loses control over the manufacturing process because goods are produced strictly as per the terms and specifications of the contract.
  3. Restricted sale and lower profit for the local firm. The local firm is not free to sell the contracted output as it wishes; it must sell to the international company at predetermined prices. If open market prices are higher, the local firm earns lower profit.

Exam tip. Contract manufacturing is not the same as licensing. In contract manufacturing, the foreign firm gets goods made to its specifications and usually sells them under its own brand. In licensing, the foreign firm allows another firm to use its patents, technology or trademark for a fee.

3.5 Licensing and Franchising

Licensing

Licensing is a contractual arrangement in which one firm grants access to its patents, trade secrets or technology to another firm in a foreign country for a fee called royalty.

  • The firm that grants permission is the licensor.
  • The firm in the foreign country that acquires the rights is the licensee.
  • It is not only technology that is licensed. In the fashion industry, designers license the use of their names.
  • Sometimes two firms exchange technology or patents; this is called cross-licensing.
  • Example: Pepsi and Coca-Cola are produced and sold all over the world by local bottlers under licensing.

Franchising

Franchising is similar to licensing, but it applies to service businesses.

  • The parent company is the franchiser; the other party is the franchisee.
  • The franchiser can be a restaurant, hotel, travel agency, bank, wholesaler or retailer that has developed a unique technique for creating and marketing services under its own name and trademark.
  • Franchising is more stringent than licensing: the franchiser sets strict rules and regulations on how the franchisee should operate.
  • Examples: McDonald's, Pizza Hut and Wal-Mart.

Advantages of Licensing and Franchising

  1. Little or no investment abroad. The foreign business is set up and run by the licensee or franchisee, so the licensor or franchiser makes virtually no investment abroad. It is a less expensive mode of entry.
  2. No share in losses. Since there is no or very little foreign investment, the licensor or franchiser is not a party to the losses of the foreign business. It receives a fee or royalty fixed in advance as a percentage of production or sales turnover, and this keeps accruing as long as production and sales continue.
  3. Lower political and takeover risk. The business in the foreign country is managed by a local person, so there are lower risks of business takeovers or government interventions.
  4. Local market knowledge. The licensee or franchisee is a local person with greater market knowledge and contacts, which helps the licensor or franchiser conduct marketing operations successfully.
  5. Legal exclusivity. Only the parties to the agreement are legally entitled to use the licensor's or franchiser's copyrights, patents and brand names in the foreign country; other firms cannot use them.

Limitations of Licensing and Franchising

  1. The licensee may become a competitor. Once the licensee or franchisee becomes skilled in manufacture and marketing, it may start marketing an identical product under a slightly different brand name, causing severe competition to the licensor or franchiser.
  2. Trade secrets may leak. If not maintained properly, trade secrets can be divulged to others in the foreign market, causing severe losses.
  3. Conflicts and litigation. Over time, conflicts often develop over maintenance of accounts, payment of royalty and non-adherence to quality norms. These can result in costly litigation and harm both parties.

Exam tip. The two differences that earn marks are: licensing is for goods, franchising is for services; and franchising is more stringent than licensing.

3.6 Joint Ventures

The definition:

A joint venture means establishing a firm that is jointly owned by two or more otherwise independent firms. In the widest sense, it can be described as any form of association which implies collaboration for more than a transitory period.

Three ways of forming a joint venture

  1. A foreign investor buys an interest in a local company.
  2. A local firm acquires an interest in an existing foreign firm.
  3. Both the foreign and local entrepreneurs jointly form a new enterprise.

Advantages of Joint Ventures

  1. Less financial burden. The local partner contributes to the equity capital, so the international firm finds it financially less burdensome to expand globally.
  2. Large projects become possible. Joint ventures make it possible to execute large projects requiring huge capital outlays and manpower.
  3. Local partner's knowledge. The foreign firm benefits from the local partner's knowledge of the host country — competitive conditions, culture, language, political systems and business systems.
  4. Shared costs and risks. Entering a foreign market can be costly and risky; a joint venture allows the partners to share costs and risks.

Limitations of Joint Ventures

  1. Technology and trade secrets may be disclosed. Foreign firms entering joint ventures share technology and trade secrets with local firms, so there is always a risk of disclosure to others.
  2. Dual ownership can cause conflict. The arrangement may lead to conflicts and a battle for control between the investing firms.

Exam tip. A joint venture is not the same as a wholly owned subsidiary. In a joint venture, ownership and control are shared; in a wholly owned subsidiary, the parent owns 100 per cent and controls everything.

3.7 Wholly Owned Subsidiaries

The definition:

Two ways of setting up a wholly owned subsidiary

Advantages of Wholly Owned Subsidiaries

  1. Full control. The parent firm can exercise full control over its operations in foreign countries.
  2. No disclosure of technology. Since the parent company looks after the entire operations of the foreign subsidiary itself, it does not have to disclose its technology or trade secrets to others.

Limitations of Wholly Owned Subsidiaries

  1. Heavy investment. The parent company has to make 100 per cent equity investment in the foreign subsidiary. This makes the mode unsuitable for small and medium-sized firms without enough funds to invest abroad.
  2. The parent bears all losses. Since the parent owns 100 per cent equity, it alone has to bear the entire losses from the failure of foreign operations.
  3. Higher political risk. Some countries are averse to 100 per cent wholly owned subsidiaries by foreigners; this mode therefore faces higher political risk.

Step 1 — Receipt of Enquiry and Sending Quotations

  • The prospective buyer sends an enquiry to different exporters, asking for price, quality, terms and conditions.
  • The exporter replies with a quotation called a proforma invoice.
  • The proforma invoice contains detailed information about price, quality, size, weight, shape, mode of delivery, type of packing and payment terms.
  • The exporter can also learn of an enquiry through an advertisement placed by the importer in the press.

Step 2 — Receipt of Order or Indent

  • If the buyer is satisfied with the proforma invoice, the buyer places an order for the goods to be despatched.
  • This order is called an indent.
  • It contains a description of the goods ordered, the prices to be paid, delivery terms and conditions, packing and marking instructions, and delivery instructions.

Step 3 — Assessing the Importer's Creditworthiness and Securing Guarantee for Payment

  • After receiving the indent and before manufacturing, the exporter enquires about the creditworthiness of the importer.
  • The purpose is to assess the risk of non-payment once the goods reach the import destination.
  • To minimise this risk, most exporters demand a letter of credit.
  • A letter of credit is a guarantee issued by the importer's bank that it will honour payment up to a certain amount of export bills to the bank of the exporter.
  • It is the most appropriate and secure method of payment in international transactions.

Step 4 — Obtaining Export Licence

  • Export of goods is subject to customs law, which requires the export firm to have an export licence before proceeding.
  • The important prerequisites are:
    • Opening a bank account with a bank authorised by the RBI and getting an account number.
    • Obtaining an Import Export Code (IEC) number from the DGFT or the Regional Import Export Licensing Authority.
    • Registering with the appropriate export promotion council.
    • Registering with the Export Credit and Guarantee Corporation (ECGC) to safeguard against non-payment.
  • For the IEC number, the firm applies to the DGFT with the exporter or importer profile, bank receipt for the fee, a certificate from the banker, two attested photographs, details of non-resident interest, and a declaration that the applicant is not associated with caution-listed firms.
  • The exporter must become a member of the appropriate export promotion council and obtain a Registration cum Membership Certificate (RCMC) to avail government benefits.
  • ECGC registration protects overseas payments from political and commercial risks and helps the exporter get financial assistance from banks and financial institutions.

Step 5 — Obtaining Pre-Shipment Finance

  • Once a confirmed order and a letter of credit have been received, the exporter approaches the bank for pre-shipment finance.
  • This finance is needed to:
    • procure raw materials and other components,
    • process and pack the goods,
    • transport the goods to the port of shipment.

Step 6 — Production or Procurement of Goods

  • The exporter gets the goods ready as per the specifications of the importer.
  • If the exporter is a manufacturer, it produces the goods itself.
  • If the exporter is not a manufacturer, it procures the goods from different manufacturers as per the export order.

Step 7 — Pre-Shipment Inspection

  • Exporters must follow international quality standards, and goods are produced strictly to the buyer's quality specifications.
  • The Government of India has made compulsory inspection of certain products by a competent agency designated by the government.
  • The relevant law is the Export (Quality Control and Inspection) Act, 1963.
  • If the product falls in the notified category, the exporter contacts the Export Inspection Agency (EIA) or another designated agency for an inspection certificate.
  • The inspection report is submitted with the other export documents.
  • Inspection is not compulsory for goods exported by star trading houses, trading houses, export houses, industrial units in EPZs or SEZs, and 100 per cent export-oriented units.

Step 8 — Excise Clearance

  • Under the Central Excise Tariff Act, excise duty is payable on materials used in manufacturing goods.
  • The exporter applies to the Excise Commissioner with an invoice; if satisfied, the Commissioner issues excise clearance.
  • The government often exempts excise duty on export goods or refunds it later, to make exports more competitive.
  • The refund of excise duty is called duty drawback.
  • The duty drawback scheme is administered by the Directorate of Drawback under the Ministry of Finance, which fixes the rates. Sanction and payment are handled by the Commissioner of Customs or Central Excise at the port, airport or land customs station.

Source note — excise and GST. The lecture slide uses the phrase "Excise/GST clearance" and explains that exported goods are generally exempt from GST and that paid tax is refunded. In the present GST regime, exports are zero-rated: the exporter can export without payment of integrated tax and claim a refund of unutilised input tax credit, or pay integrated tax and claim a refund. The older excise clearance and duty drawback route still appears in the textbook and in exam questions, so learn both the textbook term and the GST position.

Step 9 — Obtaining Certificate of Origin

  • Some importing countries give tariff concessions or other exemptions to goods coming from a particular country.
  • To claim the benefit, the importer may ask the exporter for a certificate of origin.
  • It is proof that the goods were actually manufactured in the country from which they are being exported.
  • The certificate can be obtained from the trade consulate located in the exporter's country.

Step 10 — Reservation of Shipping Space

  • The exporter applies to the shipping company for shipping space.
  • The application specifies the type of goods, the probable date of shipment and the port of destination.
  • On acceptance, the shipping company issues a shipping order.
  • A shipping order is an instruction to the captain of the ship that the specified goods, after customs clearance at the designated port, be received on board.

Step 11 — Packing and Forwarding

  • The goods are properly packed and marked with:
    • the name and address of the importer,
    • gross and net weight,
    • port of shipment and destination,
    • country of origin.
  • The exporter arranges transport to the port.
  • When goods are loaded into a railway wagon, the railway authorities issue a railway receipt, which serves as a title to the goods.
  • The exporter endorses the railway receipt in favour of its agent so that the agent can take delivery at the port of shipment.

Step 12 — Insurance of Goods

  • The exporter gets the goods insured with an insurance company.
  • The insurance protects against loss or damage due to the perils of the sea during transit.
  • The document is the marine insurance policy.

Step 13 — Customs Clearance

  • Goods must be cleared from customs before they can be loaded on the ship.
  • The exporter prepares the shipping bill, the main document on the basis of which the customs office grants permission for export.
  • The shipping bill contains particulars of the goods, the name of the vessel, the port of discharge, the country of final destination, and the exporter's name and address.
  • Five copies of the shipping bill are submitted to the Customs Appraiser along with:
    • the export contract or export order,
    • the letter of credit,
    • the commercial invoice,
    • the certificate of origin,
    • the certificate of inspection, where necessary,
    • the marine insurance policy.
  • After submission, the Superintendent of the port trust is approached for a carting order.
  • A carting order is an instruction to the staff at the gate of the port to permit the entry of the cargo inside the dock.
  • After the carting order, the cargo is moved into the port area and stored in the appropriate shed.
  • These formalities are usually handled by a Clearing and Forwarding (C&F) agent.

Step 14 — Obtaining Mate's Receipt

  • The goods are loaded on board the ship.
  • The mate or captain of the ship issues a mate's receipt to the port superintendent.
  • It contains the name of the vessel, berth, date of shipment, description of packages, marks and numbers, and the condition of the cargo when received on board.
  • The port superintendent, after receiving port dues, hands the mate's receipt to the C&F agent.

Step 15 — Payment of Freight and Issuance of Bill of Lading

  • The C&F agent surrenders the mate's receipt to the shipping company for computation of freight.
  • After receiving the freight, the shipping company issues a bill of lading.
  • The bill of lading is evidence that the shipping company has accepted the goods for carriage to the designated destination.
  • For goods sent by air, the document is called an airway bill.

Step 16 — Preparation of Invoice

  • After sending the goods, the exporter prepares an invoice of the despatched goods.
  • The invoice states the quantity of goods sent and the amount to be paid by the importer.
  • The C&F agent gets the invoice attested by customs.

Step 17 — Securing Payment

  • The exporter informs the importer that the goods have been shipped.
  • The importer needs documents to claim title to the goods on arrival and to get them customs cleared. These include:
    • a certified copy of the invoice,
    • the bill of lading,
    • the packing list,
    • the insurance policy,
    • the certificate of origin,
    • the letter of credit.
  • The exporter sends the documents through its banker, with the instruction that they be delivered to the importer only when the importer accepts a bill of exchange.
  • Submitting the documents to the bank to get payment is called negotiation of the documents.
  • A bill of exchange is an order to the importer to pay a certain amount to a certain person or to the bearer.
  • There are two types:
    • Sight draft (documents against payment) — documents are handed over only against payment.
    • Usance draft (documents against acceptance) — documents are handed over when the importer accepts the bill and agrees to pay after a specified period, say three months.
  • The exporter need not wait for the importer's payment. It can get immediate payment from its bank by signing a letter of indemnity, under which it undertakes to indemnify the bank if the importer does not pay.
  • After receiving payment, the exporter obtains a bank certificate of payment, which confirms that the documents have been negotiated and payment received in accordance with exchange control regulations.

Exam tip. If the question gives a case and asks for "the next steps", start from the step named in the case and continue in order. Do not restart from the enquiry. For example, if the case says the exporter has obtained the export licence and pre-shipment finance, the next steps are production, inspection, excise/GST, certificate of origin, shipping space, packing, insurance, customs, mate's receipt, bill of lading, invoice and payment.

4.2 Pre-Shipment Inspection and Its Three Methods

Why inspection exists. Exporters must follow international quality standards and produce goods strictly to the buyer's quality specifications. The Government of India has passed the Export (Quality Control and Inspection) Act, 1963 and authorised agencies to inspect certain products before shipment.

The three methods:

#MethodWhen inspection happensWhat the inspector checks
1Consignment-wise inspectionAfter the goods are ready for despatchEach consignment of finished goods
2In-process quality controlDuring the production processQuality of raw material, technology and type of machinery used
3Self-certificationThe exporter maintains quality itselfThe firm's own quality assurance and testing facility

1. Consignment-wise Inspection

  • The exporter calls the inspectors from the Export Council after the goods are ready for despatch.
  • The inspector checks each consignment of finished goods.
  • If satisfied, the inspector issues a certificate of inspection.

2. In-Process Quality Control

  • The inspectors inspect quality during the production process.
  • They can check the quality of the raw material used, the technology and the type of machinery used.
  • If satisfied, the inspector issues an inspection certificate.

3. Self-Certification

  • Reputed firms and large-scale exporters maintain international quality standards themselves to earn goodwill in the international market.
  • They obtain international quality standard marks such as the ISO 9000 series.
  • They acquire a mechanism of quality assurance and a testing facility.

Source note — the accurate position on compulsory inspection. The lecture says "in India, it is compulsory to get a pre-shipment quality inspection" and that "without an inspection certificate export is not permitted". The textbook position is narrower: inspection is compulsory only for certain products notified under the Export (Quality Control and Inspection) Act, 1963, and it is not compulsory for goods exported by star trading houses, trading houses, export houses, units in EPZs or SEZs, and 100 per cent export-oriented units. Write the textbook position in the exam. The lecture also mentions "ISO 2000"; the valid family of quality management standards is the ISO 9000 series, so write ISO 9000.

The import procedure differs from country to country depending on import and customs policies. The lecture teaches ten steps in a typical import transaction.

The flow in one line:

Trade enquiry → Import licence → Foreign exchange → Indent → Letter of credit
→ Finance → Shipment advice → Retirement of documents → Arrival → Customs release

Step 1 — Trade Enquiry

  • The importing firm gathers information about the countries and firms that export the product.
  • Information can be obtained from trade directories and trade associations and organisations.
  • The importer sends a trade enquiry — a written request to the exporter for information about price and the terms and conditions on which the exporter is ready to export.
  • The exporter replies with a proforma invoice, which contains details of quality, grade, design, size, weight, price, and the terms and conditions of export.

Step 2 — Procurement of Import Licence

  • Some goods can be imported freely; others need a licence.
  • The importer consults the Export Import (EXIM) policy in force to check whether the goods are subject to import licensing.
  • In India, every importer and exporter must register with the Directorate General Foreign Trade (DGFT) or the Regional Import Export Licensing Authority and obtain an Import Export Code (IEC) number.
  • The IEC number is required on most import documents.

Step 3 — Obtaining Foreign Exchange

  • The foreign supplier demands payment in a foreign currency.
  • In India, all foreign exchange transactions are regulated by the Exchange Control Department of the RBI.
  • The importer applies to a bank authorised by the RBI to issue foreign exchange.
  • The application is made in the prescribed form along with the import licence, as per the Exchange Control Act.
  • After scrutiny, the bank sanctions the necessary foreign exchange.

Step 4 — Placing Order or Indent

  • After obtaining the import licence, the importer places an import order or indent with the exporter for supply of the specified products.
  • The order contains information about price, quantity, size, grade and quality of goods, and instructions relating to packing, shipping, ports of shipment and destination, delivery schedule, insurance and mode of payment.
  • The order should be drafted carefully to avoid ambiguity and conflict later.

Step 5 — Obtaining Letter of Credit

  • If the payment terms require a letter of credit, the importer obtains it from its bank and forwards it to the overseas supplier.
  • The letter of credit is a guarantee issued by the importer's bank that it will honour payment up to a certain amount of export bills to the exporter's bank.
  • Before issuing it, the bank checks the importer's financial soundness, often by taking advice from other banks.
  • The exporter wants the letter of credit to be sure there is no risk of non-payment.

Step 6 — Arranging for Finance

  • The importer should arrange finance in advance to pay the exporter when the goods arrive at the port.
  • Advance planning is necessary to avoid demurrage — penalties on imported goods lying uncleared at the port for want of payment.

Step 7 — Receipt of Shipment Advice

  • After loading the goods on the vessel, the overseas supplier sends a shipment advice to the importer.
  • It contains the invoice number, bill of lading or airway bill number and date, the name of the vessel with date, the port of export, a description and quantity of goods, and the date of sailing.

Step 8 — Retirement of Import Documents

  • After shipment, the supplier prepares a set of documents as per the contract and the letter of credit and hands them to its banker for transmission and negotiation to the importer.
  • The set normally contains the bill of exchange, commercial invoice, bill of lading or airway bill, packing list, certificate of origin and marine insurance policy.
  • The bill of exchange accompanying these documents is called a documentary bill of exchange.
  • It can be:
    • Documents against payment (sight draft) — the bank hands over the documents only against payment.
    • Documents against acceptance (usance draft) — the bank hands over the documents against the importer's acceptance of the bill, with payment at a later date.
  • The acceptance of the bill of exchange for getting delivery of the documents is called retirement of import documents.
  • After retirement, the bank hands over the import documents to the importer.

Step 9 — Arrival of Goods

  • The carrier informs the officer in charge at the dock or airport about the arrival of the goods.
  • The carrier provides the import general manifest, a document containing details of the imported goods.
  • The import general manifest is the basis on which cargo is unloaded.
  • The importer prepares a bill of entry with details of the goods — number, quantity, price, weight and duty payable — and submits it to customs.
  • The bill of entry is prepared in triplicate.

Step 10 — Customs Clearance and Release of Goods

  • All imported goods must pass through customs clearance after crossing the border.
  • The importer first obtains a delivery order, also called endorsement for delivery. Usually the shipping company endorses the back of the bill of lading; in some cases it issues a separate delivery order. This entitles the importer to take delivery.
  • The importer pays the freight charges if the exporter has not paid them.
  • The importer pays dock dues by submitting two copies of a duly filled form, the "application to import", to the Landing and Shipping Dues Office. One copy comes back as the port trust dues receipt.
  • The importer then files the bill of entry for assessment of customs import duty.
  • An appraiser examines the document and gives the examination order. The importer pays the duty, if any.
  • After payment, the bill of entry is presented to the dock superintendent, who marks it and asks an examiner to physically examine the goods.
  • The examiner reports on the bill of entry.
  • The importer or agent presents the bill of entry to the port authority; after receiving the necessary charges, the port authority issues the release order.

Exam tip. The import procedure is often asked as a case: "Your firm is importing textile machinery from Canada. Describe the procedure." Start with the trade enquiry and end with the release order. The two documents that carry the most marks are the letter of credit and the bill of entry.

The Main Definitions — learn these word for word

Commercial or export invoice. A seller's bill for merchandise. It contains information about the goods — quantity, total value, number of packages, marks on packing, port of destination, name of ship, bill of lading number, terms of delivery and payment.

Packing list. A statement of the number of cases or packs and the details of the goods contained in them. It gives the nature of the goods being exported and the form in which they are sent.

Bill of lading. A document in which the shipping company gives its official receipt of the goods put on board its vessel and undertakes to carry them to the port of destination. It is also a document of title to the goods and is freely transferable by endorsement and delivery.

Airway bill. The air transport equivalent of a bill of lading. The airline gives an official receipt of the goods on board its aircraft, undertakes to carry them to the destination, and the document is a document of title, transferable by endorsement and delivery.

Mate's receipt. A receipt given by the commanding officer of the ship to the exporter after the cargo is loaded. It shows the vessel's name, berth, date of shipment, description of packages, marks and numbers, and the condition of the cargo when received on board. The shipping company does not issue a bill of lading unless it receives the mate's receipt.

Shipping bill. The main document on the basis of which the customs office grants permission for export. It contains particulars of the goods, the name of the vessel, the port of discharge, the country of final destination, and the exporter's name and address.

Certificate of origin. A certificate specifying the country in which the goods were produced. It entitles the importer to tariff concessions or other exemptions, such as non-applicability of quota restrictions, and is also required when there is a ban on imports from certain countries.

Certificate of inspection. A certificate issued by an authorised agency — for example, the Export Inspection Council of India — confirming that the consignment has been inspected under the Export (Quality Control and Inspection) Act, 1963 and is export-worthy.

Marine insurance policy. A certificate of an insurance contract under which the insurance company agrees, in consideration of a premium, to indemnify the insured against loss from perils of the sea.

Cart ticket. Also called a cart chit, vehicle or gate pass. Prepared by the exporter; gives the shipper's name, number of packages, shipping bill number, port of destination and the number of the vehicle carrying the cargo.

Letter of credit. A guarantee issued by the importer's bank that it will honour payment up to a certain amount of export bills to the exporter's bank. It is the most appropriate and secure method of payment in international trade.

Bill of exchange. A written instrument by which the person issuing it directs another party to pay a specified amount to a certain person or the bearer. In an export transaction, the exporter draws it on the importer. The documents giving title to the goods are passed to the importer only when the importer accepts the bill.

Bank certificate of payment. A certificate confirming that the documents relating to a particular export consignment, including the bill of exchange, have been negotiated and that payment has been received in accordance with exchange control regulations.

5.2 Case Studies with Model Answers

Case 1 — Pradeep and Mr. David

Pradeep wants to export auto spare parts to Mr. David in Switzerland. Mr. David has asked for an enquiry to seek information about availability of goods, price, quality and terms and conditions of export. In response, Pradeep sends a reply in the form of a proforma invoice. Mr. David finds the price and terms acceptable and places the order. After verifying Mr. David's creditworthiness, Pradeep obtains an export licence and arranges pre-shipment finance. On the basis of the given case, state the next steps to be followed by Pradeep.

Model answer — the next steps, in order:

  1. Production or procurement of goods — manufacture the spare parts to Mr. David's specifications, or procure them from manufacturers.
  2. Pre-shipment inspection — get the goods inspected by the Export Inspection Agency or another designated agency if the product is in the notified category, and obtain the inspection certificate.
  3. Excise/GST clearance — complete the excise formalities or the GST zero-rating and refund formalities, and claim duty drawback where applicable.
  4. Obtaining certificate of origin — obtain the certificate from the trade consulate so that Mr. David can claim any tariff concession.
  5. Reservation of shipping space — apply to the shipping company and obtain the shipping order.
  6. Packing and forwarding — pack and mark the goods with the importer's name and address, gross and net weight, port of shipment, destination and country of origin; move them to the port.
  7. Insurance of goods — take a marine insurance policy against sea perils.
  8. Customs clearance — prepare the shipping bill, submit five copies with the export order, letter of credit, commercial invoice, certificate of origin, inspection certificate and marine insurance policy; obtain the carting order.
  9. Obtaining mate's receipt — after loading, collect the mate's receipt from the port superintendent through the C&F agent.
  10. Payment of freight and bill of lading — pay the freight, surrender the mate's receipt and obtain the bill of lading.
  11. Preparation of invoice — prepare the commercial invoice and get it attested by customs.
  12. Securing payment — send the documents through the banker, obtain Mr. David's acceptance of the bill of exchange, and receive payment or get the documents negotiated; obtain the bank certificate of payment.

Exam tip. The case already covers steps 1 to 5 of the export procedure — enquiry, proforma invoice, order, creditworthiness and export licence, and pre-shipment finance. The answer must begin at production, not at the enquiry.

Case 2 — Rekha Garments and Swift Imports

Rekha Garments has received an order to export 2,000 men's trousers to Swift Imports Ltd., located in Australia. Discuss the procedure that Rekha Garments would need to go through for executing the export order.

Model answer — the procedure in five stages:

  1. Before production — receive the enquiry and send the proforma invoice; receive the indent; assess Swift Imports' creditworthiness and obtain a letter of credit; obtain the export licence, IEC, RCMC and ECGC registration.
  2. Finance and production — obtain pre-shipment finance; produce or procure 2,000 trousers to the buyer's specifications.
  3. Quality and taxes — complete pre-shipment inspection if applicable; complete excise/GST clearance and duty drawback; obtain the certificate of origin.
  4. Shipping — reserve shipping space and obtain the shipping order; pack and mark the goods; insure them; complete customs clearance with the shipping bill and supporting documents; obtain the carting order and mate's receipt; pay freight and obtain the bill of lading.
  5. Payment — prepare the commercial invoice; send the documents through the bank; secure payment against the bill of exchange; obtain the bank certificate of payment.

Extra credit point. If the question asks for the documents, name the principal documents — commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of inspection and certificate of origin — and the auxiliary documents — shipping order, mate's receipt, proforma invoice and shipping bill.

Case 3 — Mr. Manchanda's entry decision

Mr. Sudhir Manchanda runs a small automobile-components factory in Gurgaon. His domestic sales are facing recession, but he sees demand in South-East Asia and the Middle East. His friend warns him that international business is more complex than domestic business. His son suggests a fully owned factory in Bangkok. Mr. Manchanda is unsure whether to export directly, route products through export houses, or set up a wholly owned subsidiary.

Model answer — a reasoned recommendation:

  • Start with exporting. It is the easiest and least risky mode: low investment, no foreign plant, and a chance to learn the market. Direct exporting gives more control and contact; indirect exporting through export houses reduces the firm's work and risk but gives less contact with customers.
  • Consider a joint venture later. If demand grows and import restrictions or transport costs make direct exporting difficult, a joint venture with a local partner can share investment, risk and market knowledge.
  • Treat the wholly owned factory as a later option. A Bangkok plant would save transport costs and bring the firm closer to customers, but it requires 100 per cent investment, the parent bears all losses, and it carries higher political risk. For a small firm, this is a heavy commitment before the market is proven.
  • Decision rule. Choose the mode that matches the firm's funds, its need for control and its tolerance of risk. For Mr. Manchanda, the sensible sequence is export first, joint venture next, wholly owned subsidiary only after the market is established.

Note. This case is from the NCERT introduction and has no printed answer. The model answer above applies the advantages and limitations of the modes; it is a reasoned application, not a quoted key.

6.3 Common Traps to Avoid

  1. International business vs international trade. International business is the whole; international trade is only the export-import part. Do not write "international business means export and import" as a full definition.
  2. Export vs entrepot. Export is selling home-produced goods abroad. Entrepot is importing goods not for domestic consumption but for re-export. If the goods are used at home, it is an ordinary import, not entrepot.
  3. Nature vs problems. The nature has two features — two countries and foreign currency. The five problems — legal procedures, restrictions, risk, languages, foreign currency — are a different question. Do not merge the lists.
  4. The two comparison tables are different. Internal vs external trade has ten bases; international vs domestic business has eight. The overlap — currency, risk, regulations — is written in different words. Keep the table the question asks for.
  5. Pre-shipment inspection is not universal. The lecture says it is compulsory for all exports; the textbook says only for notified products under the 1963 Act. Star trading houses, trading houses, export houses, EPZ/SEZ units and 100% EOUs are exempt.
  6. ISO 9000, not ISO 2000. The lecture mentions "ISO 2000"; the valid family of quality management standards is the ISO 9000 series.
  7. Excise clearance vs GST. The textbook teaches excise clearance and duty drawback; the present GST regime makes exports zero-rated and provides refunds of input tax credit. The lecture uses "Excise/GST clearance". Write the term the question uses, and add the GST position if the question asks about the current regime.
  8. Bill of lading vs bill of entry. Bill of lading is a shipment document and a document of title. Bill of entry is an import customs document used to assess duty. They are not interchangeable.
  9. Bill of lading copies vs bill of lading definition. The lecture's "triplicate" means three copies — exporter, captain, importer. The definition is a receipt, an undertaking to carry and a document of title. Use the definition for "what is", and the copies for "how many".
  10. Sight draft vs usance draft. Sight = payment before documents; usance = acceptance before documents, payment later. The pair is documents against payment vs documents against acceptance.
  11. Direct vs indirect exporting. Direct means the firm itself approaches overseas buyers and handles formalities; indirect means middlemen such as export houses do most of the work. Do not call indirect exporting "illegal" or "informal".
  12. Licensing vs franchising. Licensing is for goods; franchising is for services; franchising is more stringent. Both involve a fee or royalty, but the subject matter differs.
  13. Joint venture vs wholly owned subsidiary. A joint venture shares ownership, investment and risk. A wholly owned subsidiary has 100 per cent ownership, full control and the parent bears all losses.
  14. WTO vs IMF vs World Bank. The WTO deals with trade rules; the IMF deals with balance of payments and exchange stability; the World Bank deals with development finance. Do not credit the WTO with lending money.
  15. Benefits to nations vs benefits to firms. Foreign exchange, efficient resources, growth and employment, and standard of living are national benefits. Profit, capacity utilisation, growth, competition and vision are firm benefits. The word "growth" appears in both; read the subject.
  16. Export objectives vs import objectives. Export has seven objectives; import has five. "Ensure national defence" belongs to imports; "sell surplus goods" belongs to exports.

  3. Why do labour-abundant developing countries export garments and import textile machinery?

    Show answer

    They can produce garments efficiently because labour is abundant, but they lack the capital and technology needed to produce textile machinery, which developed countries can make more efficiently.

  4. Name the four benefits of international business to nations.

    Show answer

    Earning of foreign exchange, more efficient use of resources, improving growth prospects and employment potential, and increasing the standard of living.

  5. How does international business create employment?

    Show answer

    Export production raises output and the demand for labour; it also creates jobs in forwarding agencies, clearing agencies and other export-related services.

  6. Name the five benefits of international business to firms.

    Show answer

    Prospects for higher profit, increased capacity utilisation, prospects for growth, way out from intense competition in the domestic market, and improved business vision.

  7. How does international business help capacity utilisation?

    Show answer

    The firm gets orders from foreign customers and uses production capacity that was idle or in excess of domestic demand; larger production also brings economies of scale and lower unit cost.

  8. Name the seven objectives of export trade.

    Show answer

    Sell surplus goods; better utilisation of resources; earn foreign exchange; increase national income; generate employment; increase government revenue; create international understanding and cooperation.

  9. How does export trade increase government revenue?

    Show answer

    Through customs duty, corporate tax and excise duty; the revenue can be spent on public welfare.

  10. Name the five objectives of import trade.

    Show answer

    To speed up industrialisation, to meet consumer demand, to improve the standard of living, to overcome natural calamities, and to ensure national defence.

  11. Why do countries import defence equipment?

    Show answer

    To equip the armed forces, navy and air force and to ensure the country's sovereignty and integrity.

  12. A company starts selling abroad because the domestic market is saturated. Which benefit to the firm is this?

    Show answer

    Prospects for growth.

  13. A manufacturer produces 50,000 units but domestic demand is only 35,000 units, so it exports 15,000 units. Which benefit is this?

    Show answer

    Increased capacity utilisation — the firm uses its surplus production capacity.

  14. How are the reasons for international business different from the benefits of international business?

    Show answer

    The reasons explain why trade exists — unequal resources, different factor availability and different productivity and costs. The benefits explain what nations and firms gain from trade — foreign exchange, efficient resources, growth, employment, higher standard of living, profit, capacity utilisation and improved vision.

Exporting and importing

  • Exporting = sending goods and services from the home country to a foreign country.
  • Importing = purchasing foreign products and bringing them into the home country.
  • Direct = the firm itself approaches overseas buyers or suppliers and handles all formalities, shipment and financing.
  • Indirect = middlemen such as export houses, buying offices or wholesale importers handle most of the work; the firm has little direct contact.

Advantages: easiest mode; less time and money than joint ventures or wholly owned subsidiaries; nil or low foreign investment risk.

Limitations: extra packaging, transport, insurance and customs costs; not feasible when import restrictions exist; little contact with foreign customers, which puts the exporter at a disadvantage against local firms.

Contract manufacturing

A firm contracts with one or a few local manufacturers in foreign countries to get components or goods produced as per its specifications. Also called outsourcing.

Three forms: production of components (automobile parts, shoe uppers); assembly of components (hard disk, motherboard, modem chip into computers); complete manufacture (garments).

Examples: Nike, Reebok, Levis and Wrangler; Godrej makes Dettol soap for Reckitt and Colman in India.

Advantages: large-scale production without building a plant; little or no investment risk; lower material and labour costs; local producer uses idle capacity; local producer enters international business and can avail export incentives.

Limitations: local firm may not follow design and quality standards; local manufacturer loses control over the process; local firm must sell at predetermined prices and may earn lower profit than the open market.

Licensing and franchising

  • Licensing = the licensor grants the licensee access to patents, trade secrets or technology for a fee called royalty. It applies to goods.
  • Franchising = the same idea applied to services, with stricter rules on how the franchisee operates. The parties are the franchiser and franchisee.
  • Cross-licensing = mutual exchange of technology or patents between firms.
  • Examples: Pepsi and Coca-Cola local bottlers; McDonald's, Pizza Hut and Wal-Mart.

Advantages: little or no investment abroad; no share in losses; lower political and takeover risk; local partner's market knowledge; legal exclusivity over the brand and technology.

Limitations: licensee may become a competitor with a similar brand; trade secrets may leak; conflicts over accounts, royalty and quality can lead to litigation.

Joint ventures

A firm jointly owned by two or more otherwise independent firms; collaboration for more than a transitory period.

Three ways: a foreign investor buys an interest in a local company; a local firm acquires an interest in an existing foreign firm; both jointly form a new enterprise.

Advantages: local partner shares equity, reducing financial burden; large projects become possible; local partner's knowledge of competition, culture, language, politics and business; costs and risks are shared.

Limitations: technology and trade secrets may be disclosed; dual ownership can cause conflict and a battle for control.

Wholly owned subsidiaries

A foreign company in which the parent makes 100 per cent investment and exercises full control.

Two ways: greenfield venture — set up a new firm; acquisition — buy an established foreign firm.

Advantages: full control; no need to disclose technology or trade secrets.

Limitations: heavy 100 per cent investment, unsuitable for small and medium firms; parent bears all losses; some countries resist foreign wholly owned subsidiaries, so political risk is higher.

Exam line to memorise: Exporting is the easiest and least risky; contract manufacturing avoids a plant; licensing and franchising avoid investment but risk the brand; a joint venture shares control and risk; a wholly owned subsidiary buys control with full investment.

Recall drill — Session 3

  1. Name any five bases of difference between internal and external trade.

    Show answer

    Meaning, countries involved, risk, currency used and procedure involved. The other bases are mode of payment, legal rules, mode of transport, cost and effect on foreign reserves.

  2. Why is the operating cost of external trade higher?

    Show answer

    Because goods travel long distances and the exporter incurs transport, insurance, customs and documentation costs.

  3. Name the eight bases of difference between domestic and international business.

    Show answer

    Nationality of buyers and sellers; nationality of other stakeholders; mobility of factors of production; customer heterogeneity; business systems and practices; political system and risks; business regulations and policies; currency used in business transactions.

    Show answer

    People in China prefer bicycles while the Japanese like to ride bikes; Indians use right-hand-drive cars while Americans drive left-hand-drive cars. Another example: Americans change consumer durables in two to three years, while Indians use them until they are worn out.

  5. What is direct exporting?

    Show answer

    Direct exporting is when the firm itself approaches overseas buyers and looks after all formalities relating to export, including shipment and financing.

  6. State any two advantages and two limitations of exporting.

    Show answer

    Advantages — it is the easiest mode of entry and requires little time, money and foreign investment. Limitations — it adds packaging, transport, insurance and customs costs, and it is not feasible when the foreign country restricts imports; the exporter also has little contact with the foreign market.

  7. Define contract manufacturing and name its three forms.

    Show answer

    Contract manufacturing is a type of international business in which a firm contracts with one or a few local manufacturers in foreign countries to get components or goods produced as per its specifications. Its three forms are production of components, assembly of components into final products, and complete manufacture of the product.

  8. State any two advantages and two limitations of contract manufacturing.

    Show answer

    Advantages — the firm gets large-scale production without building a plant, and there is little or no investment risk; it may also get lower material and labour costs. Limitations — the local firm may not follow design and quality standards, and the local manufacturer loses control over the manufacturing process and must sell at predetermined prices.

  9. What is the difference between licensing and franchising?

    Show answer

    Licensing applies to the production and marketing of goods and is less stringent. Franchising applies to service businesses and is more stringent, with strict rules on how the franchisee operates.

  10. State any two advantages and two limitations of licensing.

    Show answer

    Advantages — the licensor makes little or no investment abroad, and does not share the losses of the foreign business; it receives a royalty or fee. Limitations — the licensee may start selling a similar product under another brand and become a competitor, and trade secrets may be divulged.

  11. Define a joint venture and state its three ways of formation.

    Show answer

    A joint venture is a firm jointly owned by two or more otherwise independent firms, implying collaboration for more than a transitory period. It can be formed by a foreign investor buying an interest in a local company, a local firm acquiring an interest in an existing foreign firm, or both jointly forming a new enterprise.

  12. State any two advantages and two limitations of a joint venture.

    Show answer

    Advantages — the local partner shares the equity capital, reducing the financial burden, and the foreign firm gains the partner's knowledge of the local market, culture, language and political and business systems. Limitations — technology and trade secrets may be disclosed, and dual ownership may lead to conflict and a battle for control.

  13. Define a wholly owned subsidiary and name its two ways of setting up.

    Show answer

    A wholly owned subsidiary is a foreign company in which the parent makes 100 per cent investment in equity capital and exercises full control. It can be set up as a greenfield venture, by starting a new firm, or by acquiring an established foreign firm.

  14. Why is a wholly owned subsidiary not suitable for small and medium firms?

    Show answer

    Because it requires 100 per cent equity investment abroad, and small and medium firms may not have enough funds; the parent also bears all losses and faces higher political risk.

  15. Arrange the five modes from lowest to highest investment abroad.

    Show answer

    Exporting and importing, contract manufacturing, licensing and franchising, joint venture, wholly owned subsidiary. The first three involve nil or very low investment; the joint venture shares investment; the wholly owned subsidiary requires 100 per cent investment.

Definitions that carry marks

  • Bill of lading — the shipping company's official receipt of goods on board, its undertaking to carry them to the destination, and a document of title, freely transferable by endorsement and delivery.
  • Bill of entry — the importer's customs form, prepared in triplicate, giving details of goods and the duty payable.
  • Letter of credit — the importer's bank's guarantee to honour payment up to a stated amount of export bills to the exporter's bank.
  • Bill of exchange — the exporter's written order to the importer to pay a specified amount to a certain person or bearer.
  • Sight draft — documents against payment.
  • Usance draft — documents against acceptance, payment at a later date.
  • Certificate of origin — proof of the country where the goods were produced.
  • Mate's receipt — the ship's acknowledgement that goods are on board.

One-line distinctions:

  • Bill of lading is a shipment document; bill of entry is an import customs document.
  • Shipping bill is for export; bill of entry is for import.
  • Proforma invoice is a quotation; commercial invoice is the final bill.
  • Mate's receipt acknowledges loading; bill of lading is the document of title.

WTO — the exam facts

The WTO is the only global international organisation dealing with the rules and regulations of trade between nations. It was established on 1 January 1995.

Five features:

  1. Deals with trade between nations at the global level.
  2. Contains contracts signed by governments to keep trade policies within agreed limits.
  3. Works to liberalise trade and ensure free flow of goods and services.
  4. Settles disputes through a neutral procedure.
  5. Its main function is to ensure that trade flows as smoothly and freely as possible.

Five objectives:

  1. Promotes international peace.
  2. Settles disputes among member nations.
  3. Makes international trade smooth by framing common rules and regulations.
  4. Helps the economic growth of developing countries through preferential treatment.
  5. Free trade provides quality products and improves the standard of living.

Do not confuse: WTO = trade rules; IMF = balance of payments and exchange stability; World Bank = development finance.

Recall drill — Session 4

  1. Name the first five steps of the export procedure.

    Show answer

    Receipt of enquiry and sending quotations; receipt of order or indent; assessing the importer's creditworthiness and securing a guarantee for payment; obtaining an export licence; obtaining pre-shipment finance.

  2. What is a proforma invoice?

    Show answer

    A proforma invoice is the exporter's reply to an enquiry — a quotation giving details of price, quality, size, weight, mode of delivery, packing and payment terms.

  3. What is a letter of credit, and who issues it?

    Show answer

    A letter of credit is a guarantee issued by the importer's bank that it will honour payment up to a certain amount of export bills to the exporter's bank.

  4. What is the difference between an indent and a proforma invoice?

    Show answer

    A proforma invoice is the exporter's quotation in reply to an enquiry; an indent is the buyer's order placed after accepting the proforma invoice.

  5. What is a shipping order?

    Show answer

    A shipping order is the shipping company's instruction to the captain of the ship to receive the specified goods on board after customs clearance.

  6. What is a carting order?

    Show answer

    A carting order is the port superintendent's instruction to the staff at the port gate to permit the entry of cargo inside the dock.

  7. What is the difference between a mate's receipt and a bill of lading?

    Show answer

    A mate's receipt is issued by the commanding officer of the ship after the cargo is loaded and acknowledges receipt on board. A bill of lading is issued by the shipping company after the mate's receipt is surrendered and freight is paid; it is a receipt, an undertaking to carry and a document of title.

  8. Name the three methods of pre-shipment inspection.

    Show answer

    Consignment-wise inspection, in-process quality control and self-certification.

  9. Is pre-shipment inspection compulsory for every export from India?

    Show answer

    No. It is compulsory only for certain notified products under the Export (Quality Control and Inspection) Act, 1963. It is not compulsory for star trading houses, trading houses, export houses, EPZ or SEZ units and 100 per cent export-oriented units.

  10. Name the first five steps of the import procedure.

    Show answer

    Trade enquiry; procurement of import licence; obtaining foreign exchange; placing order or indent; obtaining letter of credit.

  11. What is an import general manifest?

    Show answer

    The import general manifest is the document containing details of the imported goods; it is the basis on which cargo is unloaded.

  12. What is a bill of entry, and in how many copies is it prepared?

    Show answer

    A bill of entry is the importer's customs form giving details of the imported goods and the duty payable. It is prepared in triplicate.

  13. Name the five principal export documents.

    Show answer

    Commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of inspection and certificate of origin.

  14. Name the four auxiliary export documents.

    Show answer

    Shipping order, mate's receipt, proforma invoice and shipping bill.

  15. What is the difference between a sight draft and a usance draft?

    Show answer

    In a sight draft, documents are handed over only against payment. In a usance draft, documents are handed over against the importer's acceptance of the bill of exchange, with payment at a later date.

  16. What is the WTO, when was it established, and what are its two main objectives?

    Show answer

    The WTO is the only global international organisation dealing with the rules and regulations of trade between nations. It was established on 1 January 1995. Its objectives include promoting international peace, settling disputes among member nations, making trade smooth through common rules, helping developing countries through preferential treatment, and improving product quality and the standard of living through free trade.
