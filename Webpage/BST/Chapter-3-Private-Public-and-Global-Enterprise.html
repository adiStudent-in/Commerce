Section 1

The Public Sector and the Mixed Economy

The idea of this section. India moved from a public-sector-led economy to a mixed one. This part traces that shift and shows how the private and public sectors are classified.

1.1 From Independence to a Mixed Economy

  • After independence in 1947, the emergence of public sector enterprises became a necessity for the all-round development of the country.
  • India was committed to a Socialistic Pattern of Economy.
  • Therefore, it adopted a pattern of Mixed Economy, where both the public sector and the private sector are allotted their respective roles for the economic development of the country.
  • These changes brought tremendous improvement in the economic development of the country.
  • Today there is a more open and competitive environment between the private sector and the public sector.

The Industrial Policy Resolutions of 1948 and 1956

  • These resolutions emphasised a dominant role for the public sector.
  • A few industries were exclusively reserved for the public sector.
  • Entry of the private sector was restricted through licensing.
  • Dominance of the public sector for about 4 decades hampered the process of growth and development.

The 1991 Industrial Policy — the turning point

Policy moveWhat it did
Disinvestment of the public sectorPublic sector holdings were opened up
Enhancing the role of the private sectorMore room was created for private entrepreneurs
Encouraging Foreign Direct InvestmentForeign capital was welcomed into the economy
  • After the reforms of 1991, the Indian economy consists of public sector units, private sector enterprises and global enterprises.

Exam tip. The chapter opens with a timeline. Learn the four dates in one line: 1947 (independence) → 1948 & 1956 (Industrial Policy Resolutions) → 1991 (reforms).

Section 2

The Three Forms of Public Sector Enterprises

The idea of this section. Public sector enterprises work in three forms. This part explains each form, its features, its strengths, its weaknesses, and where each one is used.

2.1 Departmental Undertaking

  • It is the oldest and traditional form of public enterprise.
  • It is considered as one of the departments of government and has no separate existence other than government.
  • It is managed by government officials as one of the government departments.
  • It is under the control of the concerned Minister of the department, who is answerable to government through Parliament.
  • Examples: Railways, Defence, Post & Telegraph, broadcasting, telephone services.

Features

FeatureExplanation
FinanceFinanced by the government through allocation of funds in the Annual General Budget of Parliament. The Government Treasury provides the finance and the revenue earned is also paid into the treasury
Accounting SystemSubject to the same accounting and audit controls as applicable to other government activities
ManagementManaged and controlled by a government department and subject to direct control of the ministry
Appointment of EmployeesWithout a separate legal existence, its employees are Government employees; their terms and conditions of appointment, remuneration and promotion are determined by government
AccountabilityThe employees of the enterprise are accountable to the concerned Minister, who is ultimately responsible to Parliament

Merits

MeritExplanation
Complete Government ControlCompletely owned, managed and controlled by a government ministry; this lets Parliament exercise effective control over their operations
Answerable to ParliamentAccountable to Parliament for their performances, so they have to be careful about their progress and performance
Source of IncomeThe revenue earned is a source of income for the Government as it goes directly to the treasury
Suitable for National SecurityMost suitable when national security is concerned, as it is under the direct control and supervision of the concerned Ministry

Limitations

LimitationExplanation
Lack of FlexibilityLittle scope for exercising initiative and making improvements; lack of autonomy
Delay in Decision-MakingThe employees or heads of the departments take approval of the concerned ministry before taking any decision
Bureaucracy and Conservative ApproachOver-cautious and conservative; business opportunities and risky ventures are missed
Red TapismEven routine matters and day-to-day operations need prior sanction of the relevant authority; bound by bureaucratic procedures and rules
Undue Government InterferenceA lot of political interference through the ministry
Indifferent to Consumers' NeedsSomewhat insensitive to consumer needs and does not provide adequate services to them

Suitability — where it is used

  1. Undertakings which require utmost secrecy — strategic industries like defence and atomic power.
  2. Undertakings which require absolute government control — broadcasting (Ministry of Communication such as India Post, All India Radio and Doordarshan).
  3. Undertakings in which the private sector is unable to enter due to huge capital investment — iron & steel.
  4. Undertakings which are to be used as a source of revenue — Indian Railways.

2.2 Statutory Corporation

The definition — learn this line: Statutory Corporation is a corporate body with a separate legal existence, set up under a special Act of parliament or of the state legislature.

  • The Act defines its powers and functions, rules and regulations governing its employees and its relationship with government departments.
  • It is managed and controlled by a Board of Directors appointed by the government.
  • It is financially independent, with a clear control over a specified area or a particular type of commercial activity.
  • It is a corporate entity having perpetual succession and common seal, with power to acquire, hold and dispose of property and sue and be sued in its own name (subject to the provisions of the governing Act).
  • Examples: Reserve Bank of India (RBI), Food Corporation of India (FCI), Life Insurance Corporation (LIC), Air India, and also ICAI and ICSI.

Features

FeatureExplanation
FormationEstablished under a Special Act of the Parliament that lays down the objects, powers and functions of the Corporation
OwnershipWholly owned by the government. The government has the power to appropriate its profits and also has to bear the losses, if any
Corporate ExistenceA body corporate with a separate legal existence independent of the Government. It can sue and be sued, enter into contract and acquire property in its own name
Financial AutonomyUsually independently financed — funds come from borrowings from the government or through revenues derived from the sale of goods and services to the public
Accounting and Audit ProceduresOutside the government rules and regulations for accounting, budgeting and audit that apply to departmental undertakings
Staffing and Terms of ServiceHas its own rules of recruitment and remuneration for its staffs and employees

Merits

MeritExplanation
Operational FlexibilityIndependence in functioning; sufficient scope for flexibility and initiative
Freedom from InterferenceFunds do not come from the central budget, so day-to-day working stays free from government interference and works efficiently
Autonomous Set-upFrames its own policies and procedures within the powers assigned by the Act; the Act may require prior approval of a particular ministry on certain issues
Facilitates Economic GrowthContributes to economic development with the power of the government along with the initiative of private enterprises

Limitations

LimitationExplanation
Theoretical AutonomyAutonomy and flexibility exist on paper only; in reality it is subject to many rules and regulations
Government InterferenceBureaucrats and politicians interfere, especially in major decisions or where huge funds are involved
Undesirable PracticesOfficials may misuse their autonomy and indulge in unfair practices like corruption, against the objectives of the enterprise
Delay in ActionGovernment often appoints advisors to the Corporation Board, which affects the freedom in entering into contracts and taking other decisions
Rigid StructureObjectives, powers and functions are prescribed by the Act; changing them needs an amendment of the Act, which is time consuming and complicated

Suitability — where it is used

  1. Undertakings which require special powers defined by an Act or a statute.
  2. Undertakings which require a huge amount of capital.
  3. Undertakings which require regular grants from the government.
  4. Undertakings which require a proper balance between public accountability and operational autonomy.

The LIC case in the lecture

  • With the enactment of the LIC Act, 1956, an autonomous body — the Life Insurance Corporation of India — was formed.
  • The Act defines its powers, duties and functions, and it has a separate legal entity.
  • It raises funds by borrowing from the public and government, and it works outside any budgetary, accounting or audit control like Railways.
  • Its annual report is presented in Parliament every year.

2.3 Government Company

The legal definition — Section 2(45) of the Companies Act, 2013: A government company means any company in which not less than 51 percent of the paid up capital is held by the central government or by any state government or partly by central government and partly by one or more state governments, and includes a company which is a subsidiary of a government company.

Key points

  • All provisions of the Act are applicable to government companies unless otherwise specified.
  • It may be formed as a private limited company or a public limited company.
  • Certain provisions apply to the appointment and retirement of directors and other managerial personnel.
  • The shares of the company are purchased in the name of the President of India.
  • Examples: SAIL, BHEL, BEML, BSNL, Indian Oil, Hindustan Petroleum, Bharat Petroleum, BEL.

The 51% rule — worked example from the lecture

PartnerShare
Ram (government)₹24 lakh
Shyam (government)₹26 lakh
Mahesh (private)₹50 lakh
Total company₹1 crore
  • Government share = ₹24 lakh + ₹26 lakh = ₹50 lakh = 50% of the paid-up capital.
  • A government company needs not less than 51%, so this company does not meet the test.

Merits

MeritExplanation
Easy FormationNo need for separate legislation of the parliament for its formation
Operational AutonomyEnjoys autonomy in all management decisions and acts on business prudence

DemeritExplanation

Suitability — where it is used

  1. Where the government wants to control a company in the private sector without its nationalisation.
  2. Where the government wants to launch an enterprise in collaboration with certain private enterprise.
  3. Where a big project requires government planning and funding.

Section 4

Global Enterprises and Partnership Forms

The idea of this section. Large companies now cross borders. This part explains global enterprises, their features, their merits and demerits, and the two partnership forms — joint venture and PPP.

4.1 What Is a Global Enterprise?

The definition — learn this line: Global Enterprise or Multinational Company (MNC) is a company whose business operations extend beyond the country in which it has been incorporated.

  • Global enterprises have their head office in one country — the Home Country.
  • They carry on business operations in other countries, known as Host Countries.
  • MNCs are also known as 'Supernatural Companies' or 'Transnational Companies'.
  • Examples: Coca-Cola, McDonald's, Intel, Sony, Apple, Google.

The McDonald's example from the lecture

  • USA is the home country of McDonald's.
  • India, Pakistan and other countries are host countries.
  • McDonald's makes India-specific products such as McAloo Tikki and Mc Maharaja Mac.

4.4 Joint Venture

The definition: When two or more firms join together to establish a new enterprise, it is known as a joint venture.

  • The two firms contribute capital and participate in management of the enterprise.
  • In simple words, a joint venture is a partnership between two firms.
  • Example: Maruti Company of India and Suzuki Co. of Japan joined together to form Maruti Suzuki India Ltd.
  • Other examples: TATA Coffee, ICICI Prudential Life Insurance, Hero Honda.

4.5 Public Private Partnership (PPP)

The definition: Public Private Partnership is a legally binding contract between the government and a private business firm for the provision of public assets and/or public services for the benefit of the public.

  • In short, PPP is a relationship between public and private entities in the context of infrastructure and other services.

6.3 Common Traps to Avoid

  1. The 51% rule is a floor. A government company needs not less than 51% of the paid-up capital. The lecture example gives the government exactly 50% (₹50 lakh of ₹1 crore), which falls short.
  2. Statutory corporation vs government company. A statutory corporation is created by a Special Act and is wholly government owned; a government company is created under the Companies Act, 2013 with at least 51% government holding.
  3. Departmental undertaking is the oldest form. Statutory corporation and government company came later.
  4. Who appoints the board. A statutory corporation has a board appointed by the government; a government company has a board nominated by the government.
  5. President of India is the name in which the shares of a government company are purchased.
  6. Annual Report of a government company goes to Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha or the State Legislature.
  7. Who the employees are. A departmental undertaking has government employees; a statutory corporation and a government company appoint staff under a contract of service.
  8. LIC is a statutory corporation in the lecture's list of examples.
  9. Home country vs host country. The head office sits in the home country; business operations run in the host countries.
  10. Centralised control in an MNC is exercised by the Head Office, and a joint venture (two firms forming a new enterprise) is different from a PPP (a government–private contract to provide public assets and services).

Statutory corporation — created by a Special Act

Definition: A statutory corporation is a corporate body with a separate legal existence, set up under a Special Act of Parliament or of the State Legislature.

  • The Act defines its powers and functions, the rules and regulations governing its employees, and its relationship with government departments.
  • It is managed and controlled by a Board of Directors appointed by the government.
  • It is financially independent, with clear control over a specified area or a particular type of commercial activity.
  • It is a corporate entity with perpetual succession, meaning continuous existence unaffected by changes in membership, and a common seal, the official stamp used to acquire, hold and dispose of property; it can sue and be sued in its own name, subject to the governing Act.
  • Examples: Reserve Bank of India (RBI), Food Corporation of India (FCI), Life Insurance Corporation (LIC), Air India, ICAI and ICSI.

Government company — the 51% company

Definition — Section 2(45), Companies Act, 2013: A government company means any company in which not less than 51 percent of the paid-up capital is held by the Central Government, or by any State Government, or partly by the Central Government and partly by one or more State Governments, and includes a company which is a subsidiary of a government company.

Key points

  • All provisions of the Companies Act apply to government companies unless otherwise specified.
  • It may be formed as a private limited company or a public limited company.
  • Certain provisions apply to the appointment and retirement of directors and other managerial personnel.
  • The shares of the company are purchased in the name of the President of India.
  • Examples: SAIL, BHEL, BEML, BSNL, Indian Oil, Hindustan Petroleum, Bharat Petroleum and BEL.

Recall drill — Session 2

  1. Which is the oldest form of public enterprise, and what is its legal status?

    Show answer

    The departmental undertaking is the oldest and most traditional form. It is one of the departments of government and has no separate existence other than the government, so it has no separate legal entity.

  2. Where does a departmental undertaking get its finance, and where does its revenue go?

    Show answer

    It is financed by the government through funds allocated in the Annual General Budget of Parliament. The Government Treasury provides the finance, and the revenue earned is paid back into the treasury.

  3. Who manages a departmental undertaking, and to whom is it accountable?

    Show answer

    It is managed by government officials as a government department under the concerned Minister. The employees are accountable to that Minister, who is ultimately responsible to Parliament.

  4. Name any four limitations of a departmental undertaking.

    Show answer

    Lack of flexibility, delay in decision-making, bureaucracy and a conservative approach, red tapism, undue government interference, and indifference to consumers' needs.

  5. When is a departmental undertaking suitable? Give the four uses.

    Show answer

    It suits undertakings needing utmost secrecy such as defence and atomic power, undertakings needing absolute government control such as broadcasting, undertakings where the private sector cannot enter because of huge capital investment such as iron and steel, and undertakings used as a source of revenue such as Indian Railways.

  6. Define a statutory corporation.

    Show answer

    A statutory corporation is a corporate body with a separate legal existence, set up under a Special Act of Parliament or of the State Legislature, which defines its powers, functions and relationship with government departments.

  7. Who appoints the Board of a statutory corporation, and what two corporate features does it have?

    Show answer

    The government appoints its Board of Directors. It has perpetual succession and a common seal, and it can acquire, hold and dispose of property and sue and be sued in its own name.

  8. Name any three merits of a statutory corporation.

    Show answer

    Operational flexibility, freedom from government interference because funds do not come from the central budget, an autonomous set-up that frames its own policies, and facilitation of economic growth by combining government power with private initiative.

  9. Name any three limitations of a statutory corporation.

    Show answer

    Autonomy that is only theoretical, government interference by bureaucrats and politicians, undesirable practices such as corruption, delay because advisors are appointed to the Board, and a rigid structure that needs an amendment of the Act to change.

  10. What did the LIC Act, 1956 create, and where is LIC's annual report presented?

    Show answer

    The LIC Act, 1956 created the Life Insurance Corporation of India as an autonomous body with a separate legal entity. It raises funds by borrowing from the public and government, works outside budgetary, accounting and audit control, and presents its annual report in Parliament every year.

  11. Define a government company under Section 2(45).

    Show answer

    A government company is any company in which not less than 51 percent of the paid-up capital is held by the Central Government, or by any State Government, or partly by the Central Government and partly by one or more State Governments, and it includes a subsidiary of a government company.

  12. Explain the 51% rule with the ₹1 crore example.

    Show answer

    Ram and Shyam, representing the government, hold ₹24 lakh and ₹26 lakh, while Mahesh holds ₹50 lakh in a ₹1 crore company. The government share is ₹50 lakh, which is exactly 50%, but a government company needs not less than 51%, so this company fails the test.

  13. In whose name are government company shares purchased, and where is the Annual Report presented?

    Show answer

    The shares are purchased in the name of the President of India. The Annual Report is presented in Parliament or the State Legislature, in both houses — the Lok Sabha as well as the Rajya Sabha.

  14. Name any three demerits of a government company.

    Show answer

    Freedom is only in name because the government is the majority shareholder, there is a lack of accountability even though the government finances it, and government control of the entire management defeats the purpose of registering it under the Companies Act.

  15. Distinguish the three forms on formation, management and staff.

    Show answer

    A departmental undertaking is created by a government order and attached to a ministry, is managed by government officials, and has government employees. A statutory corporation is created by a Special Act of Parliament, is managed by a Board appointed by the government, and appoints staff under a contract of service. A government company is created under the Companies Act, is managed by a Board nominated by the government, and also appoints staff under a contract of service.

Recall drill — Session 3

  1. What is the main objective of each sector?

    Show answer

    A private sector enterprise works mainly to earn profit, while a public sector enterprise works mainly to provide services to the public.

  2. Who owns each sector?

    Show answer

    The private sector is owned by one or more private individuals or corporate bodies. The public sector is owned by the Central Government and/or one or more State Governments, either singly or jointly.

  3. How does capital contribution differ between the two sectors?

    Show answer

    In the private sector, the entire or majority of capital is contributed by private investors or owners from their own resources. In the public sector, the entire or at least 51% of capital is contributed by the government.

  4. Who manages each sector?

    Show answer

    The private sector is managed by owners and professional managers. The public sector is managed by a Board of Directors who are generally representatives of the government.

  5. How does freedom of management differ?

    Show answer

    Private sector owners are free to manage and control the enterprise with little interference, while the public sector has less freedom because of political interference.

  6. How does the area of operations differ?

    Show answer

    Private enterprises generally operate in areas where steady and reasonable returns are expected. Public enterprises operate mainly in strategic industries, public utility services and other areas of social benefit.

  7. How does flexibility differ?

    Show answer

    Private enterprises can easily modify their policies as per the needs of the situation. Public enterprises must follow rigid policies and procedures that cannot be modified easily and require government approval.

  8. Recite the seven bases in order.

    Show answer

    Objective, ownership, capital, management, freedom of management, area of operations and flexibility.

  9. Which sector operates in strategic industries and public utility services?

    Show answer

    The public sector operates mainly in strategic industries, public utility services and other areas of social benefit.

  10. Give two examples each of private and public sector enterprises.

    Show answer

    Private sector examples are Trump Towers, Reliance Industries, shopping malls and construction of malls. Public sector examples are construction of roads, Indian Railways, Defence and the Post Office.

  11. Why does the public sector have less freedom of management?

    Show answer

    Because of political interference, public sector enterprises have less freedom than private owners, who can manage and control their enterprises with little interference.

  12. Which sector can modify its policies easily, and why?

    Show answer

    The private sector can modify its policies easily as per the needs of the situation. The public sector must follow rigid policies and procedures that need government approval and cannot be changed easily.

Global Enterprises and Partnership Forms

Global enterprise — the definition

Definition: A global enterprise, or Multinational Company (MNC), is a company whose business operations extend beyond the country in which it has been incorporated.

  • The head office is in one country, called the home country.
  • Business operations are carried on in other countries, called the host countries.
  • MNCs are also known as Supernatural Companies or Transnational Companies.
  • Examples: Coca-Cola, McDonald's, Intel, Sony, Apple and Google.
  • Sony's headquarters: Japan, in Tokyo.

Joint venture

Definition: When two or more firms join together to establish a new enterprise, it is known as a joint venture.

  • The firms contribute capital and participate in the management of the new enterprise.
  • In simple words, a joint venture is a partnership between two firms.
  • Main example: Maruti Company of India and Suzuki Co. of Japan joined together to form Maruti Suzuki India Ltd.
  • Other examples: TATA Coffee, ICICI Prudential Life Insurance and Hero Honda.

Public Private Partnership (PPP)

Definition: Public Private Partnership is a legally binding contract between the government and a private business firm for the provision of public assets and/or public services for the benefit of the public.

  • In short, PPP is a relationship between public and private entities in the context of infrastructure and other services.
