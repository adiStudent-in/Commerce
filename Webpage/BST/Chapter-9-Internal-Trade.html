Section 1

Internal Trade: Meaning and Main Features

The idea of this section. Internal trade is the buying and selling of goods and services that happens inside one country. This part defines it, lists its features and shows the two big types it splits into.

1.1 What Is Internal Trade?

The definition — learn this line word for word:

When buying and selling of goods and services takes place within the geographical limits of a country, it is known as Internal Trade.

Two more lines that carry marks:

  • In internal trade, payments for goods and services are made and received in a country's national currency only.
  • The answer printed on the lecture slide: "Buying and selling of goods within the geographical limits of a country is known as Internal Trade."

Three ideas to hold on to:

  • The limits that matter are geographical — the trade stays inside one country.
  • The currency that matters is national — the home currency of that country.
  • The parties can be anyone — a manufacturer, a wholesaler, a retailer or a consumer.

Section 2

Wholesale Trade and the Wholesaler

The idea of this section. Wholesale trade is the big-volume half of internal trade. This part defines it, lists the seven features of a wholesaler, and places him in the chain between the manufacturer and the retailer.

2.1 What Is Wholesale Trade?

The definition — learn this line word for word:

Wholesale Trade refers to the trade in which goods are sold in large quantities.

What the wholesaler actually does:

  • He buys the goods directly from the manufacturer in bulk.
  • He sells them in small lots to the retailer.
  • He acts as a middleman between the manufacturer and the retailer.

Features of Wholesale Trade

#FeatureNote
1Link between producer and retailerThe wholesaler joins the two ends of the trade
2Large quantities of goodsHe deals in large quantities
3One line of productGenerally specialised in one line, e.g. stationery, foodgrains
4Large capital in stockHe invests large amounts of capital in maintaining stock of goods
5Sells in small lots to retailersHe generally does not sell to the ultimate consumer
6Low margin of profitGenerally the margin of profit of a wholesaler is low
7First intermediaryHe is the first intermediary in the distribution chain

Exam tip. Feature 3 and Feature 7 are the most forgotten. Write "one line of product" and "first intermediary" with underline — they are sure marks.

  2. Because he stands right after the manufacturer in the chain and buys in bulk from him and sells in small lots to the retailer, so he is the first link of the distribution chain.

Section 3

Retail Trade and the Retailer

The idea of this section. Retail trade is the end of the chain. This part defines it, shows how retailers are classified, and then walks through every kind of retailer the lecture teaches — from the hawker's cart to the big departmental store.

3.1 What Is Retail Trade?

The definition — learn this line word for word:

Retail trade refers to sale of goods in small lots to the final consumers. A retailer buys goods from a wholesaler and sells them to the consumer. So, he acts as a link between the wholesaler and the ultimate consumer.

Features of Retail Trade

#FeatureNote
1Last linkThe retailer is the last link in the distribution chain
2Sells directly to consumersHe sells the goods or services directly to consumers
3Variety of productsHe deals with a variety of products
4Small quantityHe buys and sells a small quantity of goods
5Residential locationHe is generally located in residential areas
6Personal relationsHe maintains personal relations with the customers
7Keeps in touchHe may contact customers on telephone, Internet, TV or through his retail showroom
8MiddlemanHe acts as a middleman between wholesalers and customers

Exam tip. "Last link" and "middleman between wholesaler and customer" are the two identity lines. Pair them with the wholesaler's line — "first intermediary, link between producer and retailer" — and you have the whole chain.

3.2 How Retailers Are Classified

The lecture classifies retailers on five bases.

BasisWhat the lecture says
SizeRetailers differ in size from small stores to big departmental stores. Some customers prefer large retail houses to get a wide variety under one roof; others prefer small shops in their local area
Product MixStores keeping a wide range of different lines of products are general stores or departmental stores; stores dealing with one line of products are single line or speciality stores
PricingSome retailers sell at a fixed price, some permit bargaining. Stores located in the market charge a high price, as they add a big profit margin to cover the cost of maintaining the showroom; stores in residential areas charge a very nominal price
Service LevelMany stores offer additional services — assistance to customers, free home delivery, credit facility; some go for self-service with no assistance available
Form of OwnershipSome retail shops operate as a sole proprietorship firm, some as a partnership firm. Large departmental stores or multiple shops prefer the company form of business. Another common type of ownership is cooperative

Memory hook. Five bases in order — Size · Product Mix · Pricing · Service Level · Ownership.

3.4 Itinerant Retailers and Their Four Types

What an itinerant is:

Itinerants refer to retailers who have no fixed place of sale. They move from one place to another in search of customers. They are also known as mobile vendors. They deal in low price, daily use items and non-standard items.

Hawkers and Pedlars

  • They move from street to street, in buses, trains, etc. in search of customers.
  • A hawker carries the goods on wheeled carts or on the back of animals.
  • A pedlar carries the goods on his own head or back.
  • They sell a variety of goods such as fruits, vegetables, toys, bangles.
  • They deal with non-branded and local items.
  • They supply the goods at the doorstep of the customer.
  • The prices are fixed according to the bargaining between customer and hawkers or pedlars.

Periodic Market Traders

  • They sell their goods on fixed days in different market places. Their weekly markets are fixed.
  • Examples: Monday Market in Karol Bagh, Thursday Market in Kingsway Camp, Wednesday Market in Rohini in some Delhi areas.
  • They sell in weekly markets, monthly bazaars or annual fairs.
  • Generally they display their goods on temporary structures made outside the shops which are closed for a weekly holiday.
  • They deal in low price and low quality goods.
  • They sell a large variety of goods — plastic products, utensils, ladies bags, shoes.
  • They also set up shops on the occasion of Diwali, Christmas, etc.

Cheap Jacks

  • They display their goods in hired shops or in tents for a temporary period in different localities.
  • They hire small shops or display goods in tents in residential colonies.
  • They shift from locality to locality depending upon the prospects of business.
  • They deal in low-priced, household articles.
  • They remain in one locality for a temporary period only.

Street Traders

  • They display their articles on busy street corners, pavements, bus-stands, etc.
  • They generally operate near public places such as railway stations, cinema halls, busstands and temples.
  • They deal in a variety of goods — towels, handkerchiefs, things of daily use, mirrors.
  • They deal in low-priced products of common use.
  • They are very popular. People find it convenient to pick up items on their way back from work.

Exam tip. The four itinerant types are told apart by where the goods sit. Hawkers and pedlars — on the move. Periodic market traders — on fixed days. Cheap jacks — in hired shops or tents. Street traders — on street corners and pavements.

3.5 Small-Scale Fixed Shops

Who they are. Small-scale retailers are the most common form of retailers. These shops are found in every corner of most cities. They are mostly situated in localities to fulfil the needs of people residing in local areas.

General Stores

  • General stores are small shops located in residential areas.
  • They deal in items of daily use like groceries, confectionery, stationery, soft drinks.
  • They have a large variety in each line of product.
  • They provide free home delivery, credit facility and other services to their regular customers.
  • They create personal relationship with the customers and keep them happy and satisfied.
  • General stores located in posh areas also keep popular brands of products.

Single Line Stores (Speciality Stores)

  • Single line stores are small shops which deal with one line of products.
  • Examples of the line: garments, stationery, textiles, medicines, shoes.
  • They deal in a variety of goods within that line — a garments store has ladies' garments, children's garments, gents' garments, garments of all sizes and brands.
  • They are generally situated in market places.
  • They provide customer services which vary from store to store depending upon the type of product.

Street Shops (Street Stalls)

  • These shops are situated at street crossings, on main roads or on corners of colonies. They are also known as street stalls.
  • These are temporary platforms used to display the goods for sale.
  • These shops have a limited space.
  • They deal in a wide variety of low-priced articles such as hosiery items, toys, magazines.
  • They display their goods on tables, stands, etc.
  • Recently there is a trend of keeping better quality products on these corners — imported cosmetics, garments, watches.

Second-hand Goods Shops

  • These shops deal in second-hand goods or used articles such as books, utensils, furniture.
  • These shops sell used goods, generally priced low because they are used goods.
  • They buy goods from private and public auctions.
  • These shops are mainly for the poor people, who cannot afford to buy new goods.
  • Sometimes, people from the high income group also visit these shops in search of antique items, rare books.

Seconds Shops

  • These are the shops to sell goods which are not produced according to the required specifications. These goods always have some defects in them.
  • These shops deal in products which have some manufacturing defects.
  • Goods sold in these shops are new defective goods — they are not used or second-hand.
  • Goods are sold at a heavily discounted price.
  • The price of the goods depends upon their defect. In some goods, the defects are not easily visible.
  • Many manufacturers have their own seconds shop to clear their defective stocks.
  • Sometimes, these shops are set up temporarily by taking premises, halls or banquets on hire.

3.6 Large-Scale Fixed Shops: Departmental Stores and Chain Stores

What they are. Large-scale retailers deal in a large stock of goods and purchase goods in bulk.

Features of large-scale retailers

  • They require a huge investment.
  • They have large size showrooms to sell goods.
  • They are generally located at a central place or in shopping centres.
  • A large number of customers visit these showrooms.

Their two most common forms are Departmental Stores and Multiple Shops / Chain Stores.

Departmental Stores

A departmental store is a large retail showroom having a number of departments under one roof, each department specialising in one line of product.

#FeatureWhat it means

2Wide rangeDeals with a wide range of products, from low priced to very expensive goods; different varieties are available in different departments
3Departmentally organisedGoods offered for sale are classified and each department specialises in one line of product
4Central purchasesPurchases of all the departments are made centrally — departmental managers give a list of requirements to the general manager, who makes purchases for all the departments

8AdvertisingAdvertises on a large scale to attract customers from far and wide

Examples on the lecture slide: BIG BAZAAR, Reliance.

Multiple Shops / Chain Stores

Multiple shops refer to a number of identical retail shops located in different parts of the city. These shops are owned and controlled by a single organisation. These chain stores may be established by manufacturers or traders.

4.6 Goods and Services Tax (GST)

Introduction — as taught in the lecture:

  • GST was introduced in India with effect from July 2017.
  • Under the earlier taxation system for indirect taxes, various types of taxes were levied — service tax, VAT, excise duty.
  • GST merged these taxes into one.
  • GST is a tax on the supply of goods and services.
  • GST is a single tax at a national level, to be levied at all stages right from manufacture up to final consumption.
  • The lecture calls GST the biggest tax reform and says it removes the cascading effect.
  • GST is a mixture of indirect taxes and works as Dual GST.
  • Income tax and capital gains tax sit in the direct-tax box on the slide, outside GST.

6.3 Common Traps to Avoid

  1. Wholesaler links manufacturer and retailer. The MCQ slide prints the key "(a) Manufacturer and Customer", while the lecture body says the wholesaler acts as a link between the manufacturer and the retailer. Write manufacturer → retailer in the exam.
  2. First versus last. The wholesaler is the first intermediary in the distribution chain; the retailer is the last link. Do not swap them.
  3. Second-hand shop versus seconds shop. A second-hand goods shop sells used goods bought at auctions; a seconds shop sells new defective goods at a heavily discounted price. Never mix the two.
  4. Departmental store versus chain store. Departmental store = many lines, central location, cash and credit, local advertising, more flexibility. Chain store = one line, different localities, cash only, national advertising, uniform decoration and uniform fixed prices, no flexibility.
  5. General store, single line store and street shop. General store = many lines of daily-use items in a residential area. Single line store = one line of products, situated in market places. Street shop = a temporary stall at a street crossing with low-priced articles.
  6. Who serves whom. Wholesalers serve the manufacturer and the retailer; retailers serve the consumer, the wholesaler and the manufacturer. Grant of credit and risk sharing are services to the retailer.
  7. The four itinerant types. Hawkers and Pedlars move street to street. Periodic Market Traders sell on fixed days in different markets. Cheap Jacks use hired shops or tents. Street Traders work on street corners, pavements and near public places.
  8. Itinerants versus fixed shop retailers. Itinerants have no fixed place of sale and they move in search of customers. In fixed shops, customers come to the shop.
  9. GST. Four slabs only — 5, 12, 18, 28 per cent. There is no tax on export and supplies to SEZ. GST began in July 2017.
  10. GST Council. The Finance Minister chairs it, decisions need a 75% majority, and states carry two-thirds weightage while the centre carries one-third.

GST — the basics

  • GST was introduced in India with effect from July 2017.
  • Earlier indirect taxes — service tax, VAT and excise duty — were merged into one GST.
  • GST is a tax on the supply of goods and services.
  • It is a single tax at a national level, levied at all stages from manufacture up to final consumption.
  • The lecture calls GST the biggest tax reform and says it removes the cascading effect.
  • GST is a mixture of indirect taxes, works as Dual GST, and is split into CGST, SGST and IGST.
  • Income tax and capital gains tax sit in the direct-tax box, outside GST.
