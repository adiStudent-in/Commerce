Chapter 4

Organisation of Data

Statistics for Economics — Class 11 NCERT

1. Introduction

Data collected from primary and secondary sources is called raw data or unclassified data — it is unorganised and scattered, making it hard to interpret.

Organisation of data means arranging raw data into a form that facilitates easy understanding and makes it suitable for further processing and interpretation (tabulation, analysis, etc.).

Raw data → Organisation → Tabulation → Analysis → Interpretation

2. Meaning of Classification

Classification is the process of arranging data into sequences and groups according to their common characteristics, or separating them into different but related parts.

3. Objectives of Classification

ObjectivePurpose
Simplify & condense mass of dataMakes large datasets manageable
Explain similarities & dissimilaritiesHighlights patterns and differences
Facilitate comparisonsEnables meaningful comparison between groups
Study relationshipsHelps identify associations between variables
Prepare data for tabulationStructures data for table construction
Present a mental pictureGives a clear overall view of the data

4. Requisites of a Good Classification

A good classification should be:

  • Suitable — appropriate for the purpose of investigation
  • Unambiguous & Clear — each item should belong to only one class
  • Exhaustive — every observation must be accounted for
  • Mutually Exclusive — classes must not overlap
  • Stable — classification basis should be consistent
  • Flexible — should allow for future adjustments

5.3 Qualitative Classification

Data is classified on the basis of descriptive characteristics / attributes that cannot be quantified — such as sex, literacy, region, caste, education, etc.

  • Simple Classification: Facts are divided into two classes based on one attribute only (e.g., population divided into Male / Female).
  • Manifold Classification: Facts are classified according to more than one attribute, or each class is sub-divided into more than two sub-classes.

A variable is a quantity or characteristic whose value varies from one observation to another. Each value within the range is called a variate.

7. Frequency and Frequency Distribution

Frequency

Frequency refers to the number of times a given value appears in a distribution.

Example: Out of 20 students — 9 scored 70 marks, 6 scored 85 marks, 5 scored 92 marks. Frequencies are 9, 6, and 5 respectively.

💡 Key Idea: Frequency = count of occurrences of a particular value in a dataset.

Frequency Distribution

A frequency distribution is a table in which the frequencies and the associated values of a variable are written side by side. It shows how different values of a variable are distributed along with their corresponding class frequencies.

8. Statistical Series

A statistical series is the arrangement of classified data in some logical order — according to size, time of occurrence, or other measurable/non-measurable characteristics.

10.1 Exclusive Series

Classes are formed such that the upper limit of one class equals the lower limit of the next class (e.g., 10–20, 20–30, 30–40).

  • The upper class limit is excluded from that class.
  • The lower class limit is included in that class.
  • A value equal to the upper limit goes to the next class.
  • Maintains continuity of data.

Example:

Class (Marks)Frequency
10–206
20–305
30–409
40–5010
Total30

A student scoring 20 would be included in 20–30, not 10–20.

10.2 Inclusive Series

Both lower and upper limits of a class-interval are included in that interval itself (e.g., 10–19, 20–29, 30–39). Overlapping of intervals is avoided.

  • The upper limit of one class does not repeat as the lower limit of the next.
  • For further statistical calculation, inclusive classes are often converted to exclusive form by using true class boundaries.

Example:

Class (Marks)FrequencyTrue Boundaries
10–1969.5–19.5
20–29519.5–29.5
30–39929.5–39.5
40–491039.5–49.5
Total30

Frequencies are presented in cumulative form, either in increasing or decreasing order.

10.6 Mid-Value Series

When only the mid-values (class marks) of class intervals are given instead of class limits.

Steps to convert to continuous series:

  1. Calculate the difference between two consecutive mid-values.
  2. Half of this difference is subtracted from each mid-value (to get lower limit) and added to each mid-value (to get upper limit).

Example:

Mid-ValueCalculationClass Interval
55 − 5 = 0, 5 + 5 = 100–10
1515 − 5 = 10, 15 + 5 = 2010–20
2525 − 5 = 20, 25 + 5 = 3020–30

11. Bivariate Frequency Distribution

When data is classified on the basis of two variables simultaneously (e.g., height and weight, marks in Maths and Statistics), the distribution is called a bivariate frequency distribution or two-way frequency distribution.

12. Important Points to Remember

  1. Loss of information: When raw data is grouped into classes, individual observations lose their identity. All values in a class are assumed to be equal to the class mid-point for calculations, which causes some loss of information.
  2. Class boundaries (true limits) are used when converting inclusive to exclusive series. Gap between upper limit of one class and lower limit of next is divided by 2 and adjusted.
  3. Frequency Array is another name for Discrete Series (ungrouped frequency distribution).
  4. Relative Frequency helps compare distributions of different total sizes.
⚠️ Important: Classification of data summarises raw data making it concise, but it does not show the details found in raw data. An individual observation has no significance in further statistical calculations once grouped.

Assertion-Reason Questions

  1. Assertion (A): In case of Inclusive Series, the value of upper limit of a class never equals the value of lower limit of the next class.
    Reason (R): Class frequencies are same in both exclusive and inclusive series, although the class-intervals are apparently different.

    • (a) Both (A) & (R) are True & (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    • (b) Both (A) & (R) are True & (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    • (c) (A) is True but (R) is False
    • (d) (A) is False but (R) is True

    Answer: (b) — (A) is true. (R) is also true but class frequencies are not necessarily the same in both — the reason is not the correct explanation.

  2. Assertion (A): Exclusive Series ensures continuity of data.
    Reason (R): In case of exclusive series, upper limit of one class is the lower limit of succeeding class.

    • (a) Both (A) & (R) are True & (R) is the correct explanation of (A) ✓
    • (b) Both (A) & (R) are True & (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    • (c) (A) is True but (R) is False
    • (d) (A) is False but (R) is True

  3. Assertion (A): Population of different states of India as per Census 2011 is an example of Temporal Classification.
    Reason (R): In case of Temporal Classification, data is classified with respect to different periods of time.

    • (a) Both (A) & (R) are True & (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    • (b) Both (A) & (R) are True & (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    • (c) (A) is True but (R) is False
    • (d) (A) is False but (R) is True ✓

    Explanation: Population of different states is Geographical (Spatial) Classification, not Temporal. (R) is true but (A) is false.
