Elasticity of Demand

The law of demand says price and quantity move in opposite directions. Elasticity says how far. That one number decides whether a price cut raises a shop's revenue, how much of a tax buyers end up paying, and why a cinema can charge students less than business travellers.

The one-line idea: elasticity measures how strongly quantity demanded responds to a price change; the law of demand gives the direction, elasticity gives the size.

The core question: if price changes by 1%, by what percentage does quantity demanded change — and what does that mean for total spending?

Section 1

Why Elasticity? Meaning and Definition

The idea of this section. The law of demand tells us the direction of the price–quantity relationship. It is silent on the size. Elasticity fills that gap with a single unit-free number.

1.1 The gap in the law of demand

The law of demand says that, other things constant, a rise in price reduces quantity demanded and a fall in price raises it. That is a statement about direction, not size.

Two goods can obey the law and still behave very differently. A 10% rise in the price of salt may cut quantity by 1%. The same 10% rise in the price of a cold drink may cut quantity by 25%. Both curves slope downward; one is almost vertical, the other much flatter.

In simple words. The law of demand tells you which way the seesaw tips; elasticity tells you how far.

1.2 Definition

Definition. Price elasticity of demand is the degree of responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price, other things remaining constant. It is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.

The concept is associated with Alfred Marshall's Principles of Economics (1890). In this chapter, “elasticity” means price elasticity unless stated otherwise.

Demand is elastic when the percentage change in quantity exceeds the percentage change in price. It is inelastic when the quantity change is proportionately smaller. When the two are equal, demand is unitary elastic.

Quick example. Price rises 10%. Quantity falls 20%: elastic. Quantity falls 4%: inelastic. Quantity falls 10%: unitary.

1.3 A pure number

Elasticity compares two percentage changes, so the units cancel. A value of 2 means quantity responds twice as strongly, in percentage terms, as price. It is not 2 kilograms or ₹2. That is why we can compare the price sensitivity of salt and gold with the same number.

Because price and quantity move in opposite directions, the calculated coefficient is usually negative. Economists report the absolute value, |Ed|. The sign only records the inverse relationship; the size tells you the responsiveness.

What to notice. The direction is the same in both panels; only the size differs. Elasticity is the number that captures that size.

1.4 Elastic, inelastic, unitary

LabelCoefficientMeaningEveryday example
Elastic|Ed| > 1Quantity moves proportionately more than priceCold drinks, branded clothing
Unitary|Ed| = 1The two percentage changes are equalA good with constant total spending
Inelastic0 < |Ed| < 1Quantity moves proportionately less than priceSalt, matchsticks, insulin

“Elastic” does not mean “large quantity”. A market can sell millions of units and still be inelastic if buyers barely respond to price. Elasticity is about responsiveness, not size.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Define price elasticity of demand. Why is it needed when the law of demand already exists?
  2. Q2. Price of X rises 10% and quantity falls 20%. Price of Y rises 10% and quantity falls 5%. Which demand is more elastic?
  3. Q3. Why might a 10% rise in the price of salt and a 10% rise in the price of a luxury watch produce different percentage falls in quantity?
Show answers
  1. It is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in the good's own price, other things constant. The law gives only the direction; elasticity measures the size of the response.
  2. X: Ed = 20/10 = 2. Y: Ed = 5/10 = 0.5. Demand for X is more elastic.
  3. Salt is a necessity with few substitutes and a small budget share, so its demand is inelastic. A watch is a luxury with substitutes and a large budget share, so its demand is more elastic.
Section 2

The Percentage Method

The idea of this section. Elasticity is a ratio of two percentage changes. The only two decisions are which base to divide by and what to do with the negative sign.

2.1 Formula and terms

The percentage method is also called the proportionate, flux or mathematical method.

Ed = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP = (ΔQ/Q) ÷ (ΔP/P) = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q)

Q and P are the initial quantity and price; Q1 and P1 are the new values; ΔQ = Q1 − Q and ΔP = P1 − P. The 100 in each percentage cancels, which gives the ratio form.

Pointer. The formula uses the initial P and Q. If the question gives only the new values, find the changes first.

2.2 Why percentages, and which base?

Absolute changes are not comparable. A ₹1 fall means something different for a pen and a car. Ten extra units is large for a shop selling 50 and small for one selling 10,000. Percentages scale the change to the initial value, cancel units and produce a pure number.

Use the initial value as the base, as in the standard CBSE formula. If price falls, ΔP is negative and ΔQ is positive; if price rises, ΔP is positive and ΔQ is negative. The ratio is negative either way, so take the absolute value. Do not reverse the formula when the direction changes.

Warning. The same absolute change can give different coefficients from different starting points. A ₹2 fall from ₹10 is 20%; a ₹2 rise from ₹8 is 25%. Always check the base before comparing two elasticities.

2.3 A worked price cut

Price falls from ₹10 to ₹8; quantity rises from 100 to 140 units. The price change is −20%, the quantity change is +40%, so Ed = 40 ÷ (−20) = −2. The absolute value is 2: elastic.

What to notice. The vertical step is the price change; the horizontal step is the quantity change. Each is divided by its own initial value, not by the new one.

2.4 Six worked examples

#Change%ΔQ%ΔPEdResult
1Price ₹10 → ₹8; quantity 100 → 140+40%−20%−2Elastic
2Price ₹5 → ₹4; quantity 20 → 24+20%−20%−1Unitary
3Price ₹8 → ₹10; quantity 40 → 32−20%+25%−0.8Inelastic
4Price ₹20 → ₹16; quantity 40 → 50+25%−20%−1.25Elastic
5Price falls 5%; quantity rises 12%+12%−5%−2.4Elastic
6Price ₹10 → ₹5; Ed = 3; initial Q = 40+150%−50%−3New Q = 100

Example 6 in steps. The price fall is 50%. Taking absolute values, 3 = %ΔQ ÷ 50%, so %ΔQ = 150%. New quantity = 40 + 1.5 × 40 = 100 units.

2.5 The base matters

The same ₹2 price change and 1-unit quantity change can give different coefficients:

  • ₹10 → ₹8, quantity 4 → 5: Ed = 25% ÷ 20% = 1.25.
  • ₹8 → ₹10, quantity 5 → 4: Ed = 20% ÷ 25% = 0.8.

The absolute changes are identical; the bases are not. Elasticity is a local measure around a starting point, not a fixed property of a good for all price changes.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Why use percentage changes instead of absolute changes?
  2. Q2. Price falls from ₹12 to ₹9; quantity rises from 60 to 75. Calculate Ed and classify demand.
  3. Q3. A shopkeeper says, “Price fell by ₹2 and sales rose by 10 units, so demand is elastic.” What is missing?
Show answers
  1. Absolute changes are not comparable across goods or market sizes. Percentages use the initial values, cancel units and give a pure number.
  2. %ΔQ = 15/60 × 100 = 25%; %ΔP = −3/12 × 100 = −25%; Ed = 25 ÷ (−25) = −1. |Ed| = 1: unitary elastic.
  3. He needs percentage changes, not absolute changes. Without the initial price and quantity, ₹2 and 10 units could imply any elasticity.
Section 3

The Five Degrees of Elasticity

The idea of this section. Elasticity runs on a scale from zero to infinity. Five benchmark cases cover the scale, and each has a characteristic shape.

3.1 The five cases

DegreeValueShapeExample
Perfectly inelasticEd = 0Vertical lineLife-saving medicine with no substitute (extreme)
Relatively inelastic0 < Ed < 1Steep curveSalt, matchsticks
Unitary elasticEd = 1Rectangular hyperbolaA good with constant total expenditure
Relatively elasticEd > 1Flat curveBranded clothing, cold drinks
Perfectly elasticEd = ∞Horizontal lineA firm under perfect competition
  • Ed = 0: quantity does not change when price changes.
  • 0 < Ed < 1: quantity changes proportionately less than price.
  • Ed = 1: the two percentage changes are equal; P × Q is constant along a rectangular hyperbola.
  • Ed > 1: quantity changes proportionately more than price.
  • Ed = ∞: at the ruling price buyers will take any quantity; a tiny price rise reduces demand to zero. A perfectly competitive firm, a price taker, faces this horizontal curve.

3.2 The shapes

What to notice. Vertical means quantity does not respond; horizontal means buyers will take any quantity at the going price. Between them, steep is less responsive and flat is more responsive. The unitary curve is a curve, not a straight line, because only a rectangular hyperbola keeps P × Q constant at every point.

3.3 Reading the curves correctly

Warning. Do not read elasticity from steepness alone. On a single straight-line demand curve the coefficient changes from infinity at the price axis to zero at the quantity axis. The five panels above are drawn so that the labelled elasticity holds along the whole curve; Section 5 shows the straight-line case.

Two demand curves can be compared by steepness only when they pass through the same point. Even then, the comparison is about the responsiveness at that point, not about the slope of the whole line.

ShapeWhat a price rise doesElasticity
VerticalQuantity unchanged0
SteepQuantity falls a littleBetween 0 and 1
Rectangular hyperbolaQuantity falls just enough to keep P × Q constant1
FlatQuantity falls a lotGreater than 1
HorizontalQuantity falls to zero at any higher priceInfinity
Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Match each degree to its shape: 0, 0 < Ed < 1, 1, > 1, ∞.
  2. Q2. A demand curve is a rectangular hyperbola. Price rises from ₹10 to ₹12. What happens to total expenditure?
  3. Q3. Why is a perfectly competitive firm's demand curve horizontal, and what is its elasticity?
Show answers
  1. 0: vertical; 0 < Ed < 1: steep; 1: rectangular hyperbola; > 1: flat; ∞: horizontal.
  2. It stays unchanged. Ed = 1 everywhere on a rectangular hyperbola, so the price rise is exactly offset by the fall in quantity.
  3. The firm is a price taker: it can sell any quantity at the market price but nothing above it. Its demand curve is horizontal, so Ed = ∞.
Section 4

The Total Expenditure Method

The idea of this section. When price changes, total spending is pulled in two directions. Elasticity decides which pull wins, so the direction of the change in spending reveals the elasticity.

4.1 The test

Total expenditure is price times quantity. The method based on it is also called the outlay method. From the seller's side, total expenditure is total revenue.

TE = P × Q

When price falls, two forces act on expenditure: each unit is cheaper (pulls TE down), but more units are bought (pushes TE up). Elasticity decides which force wins.

  • If demand is elastic, the quantity effect dominates and TE rises.
  • If demand is inelastic, the price effect dominates and TE falls.
  • If demand is unitary, the two effects offset and TE is unchanged.

4.2 The three cases

ElasticityPrice fallsPrice risesDirection of price and TE
Ed > 1TE risesTE fallsOpposite
Ed = 1TE unchangedTE unchangedNo change
Ed < 1TE fallsTE risesSame

Start at ₹10 and 100 units, so TE = ₹1,000. Price falls to ₹8. Three alternative quantity responses give three outcomes:

ResponseNew quantityNew TE (₹)TE change
Elastic1401,120Rose
Unitary (TE test)1251,000Unchanged
Inelastic105840Fell

These are alternatives, not a sequence. For a price rise, read the table in reverse: elastic → TE falls; unitary → unchanged; inelastic → TE rises.

4.3 Seeing the test on a demand curve

What to notice. Start at A (price ₹16, quantity 40), where TE is ₹640. Cut price towards M: TE rises to ₹1,000. At M, Ed = 1 and TE is maximum. Cut price further towards B: TE falls back to ₹640 even though quantity is much larger.

4.4 Why it is only a rough test

  • It gives a direction, not a coefficient. It can say “elastic”, not “Ed = 2.3”.
  • It cannot separate Ed = 0 from 0 < Ed < 1: both make TE fall when price falls. It cannot separate Ed = ∞ from Ed > 1: both make TE rise when price falls.
  • It assumes other factors are constant. If income or tastes change too, the TE change may not be due to price.
  • Discrete arithmetic can blur the boundary. For ₹8 → ₹10 and 40 → 32 units, the percentage method gives Ed = 0.8, while TE happens to stay at ₹320. The expenditure test reads direction; the percentage method gives the coefficient.

In simple words. The expenditure test is a thermometer, not a precision instrument.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. State the relation between price and TE for Ed > 1, Ed = 1 and Ed < 1.
  2. Q2. Price rises from ₹20 to ₹25 and TE falls from ₹2,000 to ₹1,800. What is the elasticity range?
  3. Q3. Why can the TE method not distinguish Ed = 0 from Ed = 0.5?
Show answers
  1. Ed > 1: opposite directions. Ed = 1: TE unchanged. Ed < 1: same direction.
  2. Price rose and TE fell, so they moved oppositely: demand is elastic (Ed > 1).
  3. In both cases a price fall reduces TE and a price rise raises it. The direction is the same; only the size differs, and the method does not measure size.
Section 5

The Geometric Method

The idea of this section. On a straight-line demand curve, a point divides the line into two segments. The ratio of those segments is the elasticity at that point — no price or quantity table needed.

5.1 The segment formula

The geometric or point method measures elasticity at one point on a straight-line demand curve using the two segments cut by that point.

Ed = lower segment ÷ upper segment = EB ÷ AE

A is the price-axis intercept, B the quantity-axis intercept, and E the point. AE is the upper segment; EB is the lower segment. Because the line is straight, the ratio of the segments equals the ratio of their horizontal projections, so the quantities can be read off the quantity axis.

Memory line. Lower over upper. The lower segment is the one nearer the quantity axis.

5.2 Midpoint and halves

What to notice. Above M, the lower segment is longer, so Ed > 1. Below M, the lower segment is shorter, so Ed < 1. At M the two are equal, so Ed = 1. At A the upper segment is zero, so Ed → ∞; at B the lower segment is zero, so Ed = 0.

At E, price is ₹15 and quantity is 25 units. The horizontal projections are 25 and 75:

Ed = EB ÷ AE = 75 ÷ 25 = 3

The point formula gives the same result:

Ed = (P ÷ Q) × (1 ÷ |slope|) = (15 ÷ 25) × (1 ÷ 0.2) = 3

where the slope is |ΔP/ΔQ| = 20/100 = 0.2 for this line.

5.3 Intercept cases

PositionUpper AELower EBEd
At A (price axis)0AB∞
Above MSmallerLarger> 1
At MEqualEqual1
Below MLargerSmaller< 1
At B (quantity axis)AB00

Two lessons: slope is not elasticity, because a straight line has one slope but elasticity changes from infinity to zero; and position matters, so compare two curves at the same price and quantity, not by steepness alone.

Exam line. On a straight-line demand curve, the upper half is elastic, the midpoint is unit elastic, and the lower half is inelastic.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. State the geometric formula and the elasticity at the midpoint.
  2. Q2. A straight-line demand curve cuts the price axis at ₹20 and the quantity axis at 100 units. Find Ed at Q = 25.
  3. Q3. Why is elasticity infinite at the price-axis intercept and zero at the quantity-axis intercept?
Show answers
  1. Ed = EB/AE, lower ÷ upper. At the midpoint the segments are equal, so Ed = 1.
  2. At Q = 25, the price is ₹15. Lower horizontal segment = 75, upper = 25, so Ed = 75/25 = 3.
  3. At A the upper segment is zero, so division by zero makes Ed → ∞. At B the lower segment is zero, so Ed = 0/AB = 0.
Section 6

Determinants of Price Elasticity

The idea of this section. Elasticity is not a fixed property of a good. It changes with the buyer's options: substitutes, time, uses, habits and how much of the budget the good takes.

6.1 The determinants

DeterminantEffectExample
Availability of substitutesMore substitutes → more elasticA soap brand vs soap in general
Nature of the goodNecessity → inelastic; luxury → elasticSalt vs a foreign holiday
Number of usesMore uses → more elasticMilk vs a single-use medicine
Postponement of consumptionCan postpone → more elasticCar vs urgent medicine
Share of incomeSmall share → inelastic; large → elasticMatchbox vs refrigerator
HabitsStrong habit or addiction → inelasticCigarettes for a regular smoker
Time periodLonger period → more elasticPetrol in the short vs long run
ComplementarityUsed with another good → often less elasticPetrol for a car; printer cartridge
Price levelHigher price → generally more elasticA ₹50,000 watch vs a ₹500 watch

The strongest factor is substitutes. If buyers can switch easily, a price rise sends them away quickly. This is why demand for “salt” is inelastic but demand for a particular salt brand is more elastic: the brand has rivals.

6.2 Options versus lock-in

Necessities, habits and essential complements all reduce the buyer's options. Luxuries, postponable purchases and goods with many uses increase them. Time matters because adjustment takes time: petrol demand is less elastic in the short run, when drivers still have the same cars, than in the long run, when they can buy efficient vehicles or use public transport.

A useful summary. Elasticity is high when buyers have options — substitutes, time, other uses, budget room. It is low when they are locked in — necessity, habit, addiction, or an essential complement.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Why is the demand for salt usually inelastic?
  2. Q2. A new soap brand has many close substitutes; a life-saving insulin brand has few. Which demand is more elastic?
  3. Q3. Why does demand become more elastic over a longer time period?
Show answers
  1. Salt is a necessity with few substitutes, a small budget share and habitual purchase, so quantity responds little to price.
  2. The soap brand. Buyers can switch to rivals; insulin buyers have few alternatives and the good is essential.
  3. Consumers have more time to change habits, find substitutes or buy efficient equipment, so the quantity response can work itself out.
Section 7

Elasticity and Total Revenue

The idea of this section. Every seller is really asking an elasticity question: if I cut price, will the extra units make up for the lower price per unit?

7.1 The seller's question

From the seller's side, total expenditure is total revenue:

TR = P × Q

P is price per unit and Q is quantity sold. Before changing price, a seller asks whether extra units will compensate for the lower price per unit. Elasticity answers that.

ElasticityPrice risesPrice fallsSeller's reading
Ed > 1TR fallsTR risesA price cut raises revenue
Ed = 1TR unchangedTR unchangedPrice changes do not raise revenue
Ed < 1TR risesTR fallsA price rise raises revenue

Read it with Figure 4.4. The TE curve is the revenue curve seen from the buyer's side. It rises while demand is elastic, peaks at the midpoint where Ed = 1, and falls when demand is inelastic.

7.2 A café example

A café sells 100 cups at ₹50, so its total revenue is ₹5,000. It cuts price to ₹45 and sells 130 cups, so revenue is ₹5,850. The changes are −10% in price and +30% in quantity, so Ed = 30/10 = 3. Revenue rises by ₹850.

Whether profit rises depends on the cost of the extra 30 cups. If each extra cup costs ₹40 to make, the extra revenue of ₹850 is partly eaten by ₹1,200 of extra cost, and profit falls even though revenue rises.

Warning. Revenue maximisation is not profit maximisation. A price cut can raise TR and still lower profit if extra output costs more than the extra revenue.

7.3 The marginal revenue link

Chapter 7 gives the exact relationship:

MR = AR × (1 − 1/Ed)

MR is marginal revenue, AR is average revenue (price), and Ed is the absolute value of elasticity.

  • If Ed > 1, the bracket is positive, so MR > 0 and TR is still rising.
  • If Ed = 1, MR = 0 and TR is at its maximum.
  • If Ed < 1, MR < 0 and TR is falling.

This is why a seller facing a straight-line demand curve maximises revenue at the midpoint, exactly where Ed = 1 in Figure 4.4.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. If Ed = 0.6, what happens to TR when price rises?
  2. Q2. Price falls from ₹50 to ₹45 and quantity rises from 100 to 130. Find Ed and the change in TR.
  3. Q3. Why does a price cut raise revenue only when demand is elastic?
Show answers
  1. TR rises. Demand is inelastic, so the price effect dominates.
  2. %ΔP = −10%, %ΔQ = 30%, so Ed = 3. TR rises from ₹5,000 to ₹5,850, an increase of ₹850.
  3. The price cut lowers revenue per unit but raises units sold. With elastic demand, the percentage rise in quantity exceeds the percentage fall in price, so the quantity effect wins.
Section 8

Elasticity in Policy and Business

The idea of this section. Elasticity is not just an exam calculation. It shapes tax policy, monopoly pricing, export earnings, price discrimination and wage bargaining.

8.1 Five applications

UseHow elasticity mattersExample
TaxationTax inelastic goods to raise revenue with little loss of quantity; consumers bear more of the tax when demand is inelastic relative to supplySalt, fuel, tobacco
Monopoly pricingA price-maker studies elasticity before changing price; profit-maximising output lies on the elastic range, where MR is positivePatented medicine
DevaluationExports become cheaper abroad; elastic export demand raises earnings, inelastic demand lowers themTextiles vs specialised machinery
Price discriminationCharge more where demand is inelastic, less where it is elasticStudent discounts, airline fares
Wage bargainingInelastic labour demand means a wage rise causes a smaller employment fall, giving workers more powerDoctors, specialised engineers

8.2 The qualifications

Taxation is not only about revenue. A tax on salt may be revenue-safe but regressive, hitting poor households harder; elasticity predicts the revenue effect, not the fairness.

Devaluation works through export demand. A cheaper currency lowers the foreign price of exports. If foreign buyers respond strongly, quantity rises enough to raise earnings; if they respond weakly, the price fall dominates and earnings fall.

Price discrimination requires different elasticities and separate markets. Students are more price-sensitive than business travellers, so cinemas and airlines discount to students while charging business travellers more. If resale is easy, the low-price buyer undercuts the high-price market and the scheme collapses.

A final warning. Elasticity predicts direction, not the whole decision. Governments also care about fairness, firms about costs, and unions about the total wage bill.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Why do governments often tax goods with inelastic demand?
  2. Q2. A country devalues and export demand is inelastic. What happens to export earnings?
  3. Q3. Why can a seller charge different prices to two groups?
Show answers
  1. A tax raises price without a large fall in quantity, so revenue is relatively stable and more of the tax is passed to consumers when demand is inelastic relative to supply.
  2. Earnings fall: the foreign-currency price falls, but quantity rises less than proportionately.
  3. The groups have different elasticities and the markets can be kept separate. The seller charges more to the inelastic group and less to the elastic group.
Section 9

Common Mistakes, Recap and Exam Practice

The idea of this section. Most lost marks in this chapter come from three places: the wrong base, the wrong direction in the geometric formula, and treating slope as elasticity. Fix those and the rest is practice.

9.1 Common mistakes

MistakeCorrect idea
Reporting elasticity as negative and treating the sign as strengthReport the absolute value; the sign only shows the inverse relationship.
Using the new price or quantity as the baseUse the initial values in the standard CBSE formula.
Thinking elastic demand means a large quantity demandedElasticity is responsiveness, not market size.
Treating a steep curve as inelastic at every pointOn a straight line, elasticity changes from infinity to zero; slope is not elasticity.
Thinking Ed = 1 means quantity is constantIt means the percentage changes are equal; quantity does change.
Treating unchanged TE as an exact proof of Ed = 1 in every exampleThe expenditure test is rough; discrete arithmetic can blur the boundary.
Using the TE test to separate Ed = 0 from Ed < 1It cannot; both move TE in the same direction.
Writing upper ÷ lower in the geometric methodIt is lower ÷ upper.
Confusing a movement along the curve with a shiftOwn-price changes move along the curve; income or taste changes shift it.

9.2 Chapter recap

  • Elasticity measures the size of the quantity response; the law of demand gives only the direction.
  • Ed = (ΔQ/Q) ÷ (ΔP/P), using initial values and reporting the absolute value.
  • The five degrees: 0 vertical, less than 1 steep, 1 rectangular hyperbola, more than 1 flat, infinity horizontal.
  • TE method: Ed > 1 → opposite; Ed < 1 → same; Ed = 1 → unchanged. It is only a rough test.
  • Geometric method: Ed = lower ÷ upper; midpoint 1; upper half elastic, lower half inelastic; intercepts infinity and zero.
  • Elasticity is higher with substitutes, luxuries, many uses, postponement, a large budget share, a long time period and a high price level; lower for necessities, habits and essential complements.
  • A price cut raises TR when demand is elastic, lowers it when inelastic, and leaves it unchanged when unitary. Revenue maximisation is not profit maximisation.
  • Elasticity informs taxation, monopoly pricing, devaluation, price discrimination and wage bargaining.

Answer pointers

Very short answer.

  1. Percentage change in quantity demanded divided by percentage change in own price, other things constant.
  2. Ed = 1.
  3. A vertical straight line.
  4. Inelastic (Ed = 0.5).
  5. Total expenditure rises.

Short answer.

  1. Absolute changes are not comparable across goods or market sizes; percentages use initial values, cancel units and give a pure number.
  2. %ΔQ = 10/40 × 100 = 25%; %ΔP = −4/20 × 100 = −20%; Ed = 1.25. Elastic.

Session 1

Meaning, method and degrees

The chapter in 12 lines

  • The law of demand gives the direction; elasticity gives the size.
  • Ed = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP, other things constant.
  • Report the absolute value; the negative sign only records the inverse relation.
  • Use the initial price and quantity as the base.
  • Elasticity is a pure number, so salt and gold can be compared.
  • |Ed| > 1 elastic; = 1 unitary; < 1 inelastic.
  • Ed = 0: vertical. Quantity does not respond.
  • Ed = ∞: horizontal. Any quantity at the ruling price.
  • Ed = 1: rectangular hyperbola, so P × Q is constant.
  • Steep usually means less elastic; flat usually means more elastic — at the same point.
  • Slope is not elasticity: one straight line has one slope but many elasticities.
  • Perfectly competitive firms face a horizontal demand curve: Ed = ∞.

Why elasticity exists

A 10% price rise may cut salt purchases by 1% and cold-drink purchases by 25%. Both obey the law of demand; the law cannot tell them apart. Elasticity measures the difference.

LabelCoefficientMeaning
Elastic|Ed| > 1%ΔQ is larger than %ΔP
Unitary|Ed| = 1%ΔQ equals %ΔP
Inelastic0 < |Ed| < 1%ΔQ is smaller than %ΔP

Not size: “elastic” does not mean “large quantity”. It means buyers are responsive to price.

The percentage method

Ed = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP = (ΔQ/Q) ÷ (ΔP/P) = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q)

Use the initial Q and P. If price falls ₹10 → ₹8 and quantity rises 100 → 140:

StepCalculationResult
%ΔQ40 ÷ 100 × 100+40%
%ΔP−2 ÷ 10 × 100−20%
Ed+40 ÷ (−20)−2
|Ed|size only2 → elastic

Base warning: a ₹2 fall from ₹10 is 20%; a ₹2 rise from ₹8 is 25%. The same absolute change can give different coefficients.

The five degrees

DegreeValueShapeExample
Perfectly inelastic0VerticalEssential medicine with no substitute
Relatively inelastic0 to 1SteepSalt, matchsticks

Relatively elasticAbove 1FlatCold drinks, branded clothing
Perfectly elastic∞HorizontalFirm under perfect competition

Two warnings: a steep curve is not automatically inelastic at every point, and Ed = 1 does not mean quantity is constant — it means the percentage changes match.

Recall drill — Session 1

  1. Define price elasticity of demand and explain why the law of demand is incomplete without it.

    Show answer

    It is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in the good's own price, other things constant. The law gives only the direction of the response; elasticity gives its size.

  2. Price rises 10% and quantity falls 20%. Price rises 10% and quantity falls 5%. Calculate both elasticities and classify them.

    Show answer

    First: 20 ÷ 10 = 2, elastic. Second: 5 ÷ 10 = 0.5, inelastic. The first good is twice as responsive.

  3. Why use percentage changes, and which values go in the base?

    Show answer

    Percentages cancel units and make changes comparable across goods and market sizes. Use the initial price and quantity as the base, as in the standard CBSE formula.

  4. Price falls from ₹10 to ₹8; quantity rises from 100 to 140. Find Ed.

    Show answer

    %ΔQ = 40 ÷ 100 × 100 = 40%; %ΔP = −2 ÷ 10 × 100 = −20%; Ed = 40 ÷ (−20) = −2. |Ed| = 2, elastic.

  5. Price rises from ₹8 to ₹10; quantity falls from 40 to 32. Find Ed.

    Show answer

    %ΔQ = −8 ÷ 40 × 100 = −20%; %ΔP = 2 ÷ 8 × 100 = 25%; Ed = −20 ÷ 25 = −0.8. |Ed| = 0.8, inelastic.

  6. A 5% fall in price raises quantity by 12%. Find Ed.

    Show answer

    Ed = 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4. Elastic.

  7. Ed = 3; price falls from ₹10 to ₹5; initial quantity is 40. Find the new quantity.

    Show answer

    The price fall is 50%. %ΔQ = 3 × 50% = 150%. New quantity = 40 + 1.5 × 40 = 100 units.

  8. Match each degree to its shape: 0, between 0 and 1, 1, above 1, infinity.

    Show answer

    0: vertical. Between 0 and 1: steep. 1: rectangular hyperbola. Above 1: flat. Infinity: horizontal.

Session 2

Total expenditure and geometric method

The total expenditure test

TE = P × Q. When price falls, each unit earns less but more units are sold. Elasticity decides which effect wins.

TE = P × Q Ed > 1 → TE moves opposite to price

One-line test: price and TE moving in opposite directions means elastic; moving in the same direction means inelastic; no change means unitary.

The three cases

ElasticityPrice fallsPrice risesReading
Ed > 1TE risesTE fallsOpposite directions
Ed = 1TE unchangedTE unchangedNo change
Ed < 1TE fallsTE risesSame direction

Example: at ₹10 and 100 units, TE = ₹1,000. A cut to ₹8 gives TE = ₹1,120 if quantity rises to 140 (elastic), ₹1,000 at 125 (unitary), or ₹840 at 105 (inelastic).

Rough test only: TE cannot separate 0 from 0.5, or infinity from 2. It gives a direction, not a coefficient.

The geometric method

Ed = lower segment ÷ upper segment = EB ÷ AE

On a straight-line demand curve from A (price axis) to B (quantity axis), a point E divides the line. Above the midpoint, EB > AE, so Ed > 1. At the midpoint, EB = AE, so Ed = 1. Below it, EB < AE, so Ed < 1.

PositionEdReason
At A (price axis)∞Upper segment is zero
Upper halfAbove 1Lower segment is longer
Midpoint1Segments are equal
Lower halfBelow 1Upper segment is longer
At B (quantity axis)0Lower segment is zero

Worked check: a line cuts the axes at ₹20 and 100 units. At Q = 25, P = ₹15. Lower projection = 75, upper = 25, so Ed = 75 ÷ 25 = 3.

Limits and comparison

MethodWhat it givesMain limit
PercentageAn exact coefficientNeeds initial and new P and Q
Total expenditureA direction: elastic, unitary or inelasticCannot measure size or separate the extremes
GeometricElasticity at a point on a straight lineOnly for a straight-line demand curve

Slope is not elasticity: a straight line has one slope, but elasticity falls from infinity at A to zero at B.

Recall drill — Session 2

  1. State the TE test for Ed > 1, Ed = 1 and Ed < 1.

    Show answer

    Ed > 1: price and TE move oppositely. Ed = 1: TE unchanged. Ed < 1: price and TE move in the same direction.

  2. Price falls from ₹20 to ₹16 and TE rises. What can you say about elasticity?

    Show answer

    Price and TE moved in opposite directions, so demand is elastic (Ed > 1). The quantity effect dominated.

  3. Why can the TE method not distinguish Ed = 0 from Ed = 0.5?

    Show answer

    Both are inelastic, so both make TE fall when price falls and rise when price rises. The direction is identical; the method does not measure the size of the response.

  4. State the geometric formula and the elasticity at the midpoint.

    Show answer

    Ed = EB ÷ AE, lower ÷ upper. At the midpoint the segments are equal, so Ed = 1.

  5. A straight line cuts the price axis at ₹20 and the quantity axis at 100 units. Find Ed at Q = 25.

    Show answer

    At Q = 25, P = ₹15. The lower horizontal projection is 75 and the upper is 25, so Ed = 75 ÷ 25 = 3.

  6. Why is Ed infinite at A and zero at B?

    Show answer

    At A the upper segment is zero, so the ratio EB/AE divides by zero and tends to infinity. At B the lower segment is zero, so EB/AE = 0.

  7. Why is slope not elasticity?

    Show answer

    A straight line has a constant slope, but its elasticity changes from infinity at the price axis to zero at the quantity axis. Elasticity depends on the ratio P/Q as well as the slope.

  8. A point on a straight-line demand curve has Ed = 2. Is it above or below the midpoint?

    Show answer

    Above the midpoint. In the upper half the lower segment is longer than the upper segment, so Ed > 1.

Determinants, revenue and uses

Determinants of elasticity

The master idea: elasticity is high when buyers have options and low when they are locked in.

Elasticity and revenue

ElasticityPrice risePrice fall
Ed > 1TR fallsTR rises
Ed = 1TR unchangedTR unchanged
Ed < 1TR risesTR falls
MR = AR × (1 − 1/Ed)

With Ed as the absolute value: elastic → MR positive; unitary → MR = 0 and TR maximum; inelastic → MR negative.

Café check: 100 cups at ₹50 gives TR ₹5,000. At ₹45 and 130 cups, TR is ₹5,850. Ed = 30 ÷ 10 = 3, so the price cut raises revenue. Profit still depends on the cost of the extra cups.

Uses in policy and business

UseElasticity logic
TaxationTax inelastic goods: revenue is stable and buyers bear more of the tax
Monopoly pricingChoose output on the elastic range, where MR is positive
DevaluationExport earnings rise only if export demand is elastic
Price discriminationCharge more to the inelastic group, less to the elastic group; keep markets separate
Wage bargainingInelastic labour demand means a wage rise costs few jobs

Exam caution: elasticity predicts the direction of a revenue or earnings change, not the whole policy decision. Fairness and costs also matter.

Recall drill — Session 3

  1. Why is the demand for salt inelastic but the demand for one salt brand more elastic?

    Show answer

    Salt as a whole is a necessity with few substitutes and a small budget share. A single brand has rival brands that buyers can switch to, so its demand is more elastic.

  2. Why does petrol demand become more elastic in the long run?

    Show answer

    In the long run drivers can change cars, buy efficient vehicles, move closer to work or use public transport. More adjustment options make quantity more responsive.

  3. Ed = 0.6 and price rises. What happens to TR?

    Show answer

    TR rises. Demand is inelastic, so the price effect dominates the fall in quantity.

  4. Price falls from ₹50 to ₹45 and quantity rises from 100 to 130. Find Ed and the TR change.

    Show answer

    %ΔP = −10%, %ΔQ = +30%, so Ed = 3. TR rises from ₹5,000 to ₹5,850, an increase of ₹850.

  5. State the MR formula. What is MR when Ed = 1?

    Show answer

    MR = AR × (1 − 1/Ed). When Ed = 1, the bracket is zero, so MR = 0 and TR is at its maximum.

  6. Why does a profit-maximising monopolist choose output on the elastic range?

    Show answer

    On the elastic range MR is positive, so extra output adds to revenue. A profit-maximiser never chooses the inelastic range, where MR is negative and cutting output would raise revenue.

  7. Why can a cinema charge students less than business travellers?

    Show answer

    Students have more elastic demand and can be kept in a separate market with ID checks. Business travellers have inelastic demand, so the cinema can charge them more.

  8. A country devalues and export demand is inelastic. What happens to export earnings?

    Show answer

    Export earnings fall. The foreign-currency price falls, but quantity rises less than proportionately, so the price effect dominates.
