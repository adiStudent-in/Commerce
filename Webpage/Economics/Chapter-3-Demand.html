Demand

Every market story starts here. Demand is the quantity a buyer is willing and able to buy at each price — and the strange, reliable fact that this quantity usually moves opposite to price. Learn it properly once and supply, elasticity and equilibrium all become easier.

The one-line idea: demand is the quantity a buyer is willing and able to buy at each price in a given period, and almost every result in microeconomics begins with the fact that this quantity usually moves opposite to price.

The core question: why does quantity demanded normally fall when price rises — and when does it fail to fall?

Section 1

The Meaning of Demand

The idea of this section. In everyday speech, “demand” means wanting something. In economics it means wanting it, being able to pay for it, at a particular price, in a particular period. All four parts must be present.

1.1 Desire, want and demand

A desire is a mere wish to possess a commodity. A want is a desire backed by the ability and willingness to satisfy it. Demand is a want backed by willingness and ability to pay a particular price, for a particular quantity, during a given period.

TermWhat backs itExample
DesireThe wish aloneA student wishes for an ₹80,000 laptop
WantAbility and willingnessShe has ₹60,000 saved and will spend it
DemandAbility, willingness, price and timeAt ₹55,000, she buys one laptop this month

The ladder runs one way: every demand is a want, every want is a desire, but not the reverse. A wish without money is not demand; money without willingness is not demand either.

1.2 The four elements of demand

Definition. Demand is the quantity of a commodity that a consumer is willing and able to buy at each possible price during a given period of time.

  • Quantity: a definite number of units, not “some” or “a lot”.
  • Willingness and ability: both are needed. Wanting without money, or money without willingness, is not demand.
  • Price: demand exists at a price. The same buyer takes more at a lower price.
  • Time period: per day, week or month. Without it, the number has no meaning.

Exam pointer. If a question says “demand”, check all four elements. Most one-mark errors come from quoting the definition without the price and the time period.

1.3 A flow, and two levels of demand

Definition. A flow is measured over a period; a stock is measured at a point of time. Demand is a flow: a shop may hold 500 packets in stock today but demand 40 packets per day.

Individual demand is one consumer's quantity at each price. Market demand is the total all consumers will buy at each price in a given period. At ₹40, Asha buys 2 bottles and Bharat 3, so market demand is 5 bottles.

Memory line. Stock is a photograph; flow is a video. Demand is a video, because it is always “per period”.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Why is a poor person's wish to own a car not demand?
  2. Q2. Ravi has ₹6,000, will spend up to ₹5,500, and a bicycle costs ₹5,200 this month. Is his want a demand?
  3. Q3. “Demand is a flow, not a stock.” Explain with an example.
Show answers
  1. It lacks ability to pay. Demand needs willingness, ability, a price and a time period.
  2. Yes: ability, willingness, the price of ₹5,200 and the period are all present.
  3. Demand is per unit of time, such as 40 cups per day. Stock is at a point of time, such as 500 cups in the shop today.
Section 2

The Demand Schedule, the Curve and the Law

The idea of this section. A demand schedule turns the definition into a table; a demand curve turns the table into a picture. The law of demand is the sentence that both are expressing.

2.1 The demand schedule

Definition. A demand schedule is a table showing the quantities of a commodity a consumer will buy at various prices during a given period.

Price of X (₹)Quantity demanded (units)
1010
820
630
440
250

As price falls from ₹10 to ₹2, quantity rises from 10 to 50 units. Read the table downwards and the inverse relationship is already visible.

Pointer. A schedule is a list, not a graph. The examiner may ask you to “prepare” one, so keep the price column first and the quantity column second.

2.2 The demand curve and its slope

Definition. A demand curve is the graphical representation of a demand schedule. Price is on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal; each row becomes a point, and joining the points gives the curve.

What to notice. The curve slopes downward; the dashed lines read off A (₹10, 10 units) and B (₹4, 40 units).

Slope = ΔP / ΔQd = (P2 − P1) / (Q2 − Q1)

Between A and B, slope = (4 − 10) / (40 − 10) = −6 / 30 = −0.20. A ₹1 fall in price is associated with 5 more units.

Warning. With price on the vertical axis, slope is ΔP/ΔQ, not ΔQ/ΔP. The negative sign records the inverse relation; the size says how steep the line is.

2.3 The law of demand and ceteris paribus

Law of demand. Other things being equal (ceteris paribus), quantity demanded is inversely related to price: price rises → quantity demanded falls; price falls → quantity demanded rises.

Ceteris paribus means “other things remaining the same”. It isolates the price effect. The law assumes that the prices of substitutes and complements, income, tastes and future-price expectations do not change; for market demand, population and income distribution are also constant.

In simple words. The law is a controlled experiment. Change price, freeze everything else, and watch quantity. If three things move at once, you cannot tell which one caused the change.

2.4 What kind of law it is

  • Qualitative, not quantitative: it gives the direction of the response, not its size. That size is Chapter 4's job.
  • Not proportional: a 10% price fall need not raise quantity by 10%.
  • A tendency, not a universal rule: it holds normally, but Section 8 lists the exceptions.
  • Demand is the whole curve; quantity demanded is one point on it. Keep the two words apart from the first day.
Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. State the law of demand and explain why it uses ceteris paribus.
  2. Q2. Using the schedule above, calculate the slope between ₹8 and ₹4, and interpret it.
  3. Q3. Why would the law become untestable if tastes changed along with price?
Show answers
  1. Other things equal, quantity demanded falls when price rises and rises when price falls. Ceteris paribus isolates the price effect from income, tastes and other determinants.
  2. ΔP = −4 and ΔQ = 20, so slope = −4/20 = −0.20. A ₹1 fall in price brings 5 more units.
  3. A fall in quantity could be caused by the higher price or by the loss of taste; the two could not be separated.
Section 3

Why the Law of Demand Holds

The idea of this section. The downward slope is not an assumption; it has reasons. The first three work through the individual consumer; the last two work through wider choices and the market.

3.1 Diminishing marginal utility

Law of diminishing marginal utility. As a consumer takes more units of a commodity in a period, the additional satisfaction from each successive unit falls. Since each extra unit is worth less, it is bought only at a lower price.

The first samosa at a stall is worth a lot; the fourth is worth much less. A stall selling at ₹15 can sell one or two; to sell the fourth, it must lower the price.

3.2 The substitution effect

Substitution effect. When a good becomes cheaper relative to other goods, consumers substitute it for those goods. If tea becomes cheaper while coffee does not, some coffee drinkers switch to tea, so the quantity of tea demanded rises.

This reason works through relative price: the good has not changed in quality, but it has become a better deal compared with its rivals.

3.3 The income effect

Income effect. A fall in price raises the consumer's real income — the purchasing power of the same money income — and this raises demand for normal goods. With ₹40, a student buys 4 notebooks at ₹10 but 5 at ₹8, so real income rises.

For normal goods both effects raise quantity when price falls; for inferior goods the income effect works the other way (Section 5).

Do not confuse the two. Substitution compares the good with its rivals. Income compares the buyer's purchasing power with what it was before.

3.4 New buyers enter the market

At a lower price, people who could not afford the good enter the market. A smartphone at ₹30,000 sells to higher-income buyers; at ₹15,000, students also buy it, so demand rises.

This reason is strongest for goods with a wide range of incomes among potential buyers, such as scooters, air coolers and coaching courses.

3.5 Alternative uses

Goods such as milk and electricity have uses of differing importance: at a high price only priority uses are worth paying for; at a lower price, less important uses become worthwhile.

Milk can become curd, paneer or kheer. At ₹70 a litre a household may buy only for tea; at ₹50 it may also buy for paneer. More uses become affordable at a lower price, so more units are bought.

3.6 Where the law weakens

The law is a tendency. It can fail for Giffen goods (real income falls sharply), Veblen goods (high price is the attraction), speculation (expected shortage), ignorance (unknown price), and bare necessities. Each is a case where something held constant moves with price.

Bridge to Section 8. An exception is not a refutation. It is a situation where ceteris paribus breaks down, so a factor that should have stayed still moves along with price.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Distinguish the income effect from the substitution effect of a price fall.
  2. Q2. Use the “alternative uses” reason to explain why a fall in the electricity tariff raises quantity demanded.
  3. Q3. Money income is unchanged when price falls. Why has real income risen?
Show answers
  1. Substitution works through relative price, as buyers switch toward the cheaper good; income works through real income, as the same money buys more.
  2. At a high tariff only priority uses such as lighting are worth it; at a lower tariff, less important uses also become affordable.
  3. Real income is what money income can buy. ₹40 buys 4 notebooks at ₹10 but 5 at ₹8, so purchasing power has risen.
Section 4

Determinants of Demand and the Demand Function

The idea of this section. Price is not the only thing that changes quantity demanded. Every other determinant works by shifting the whole curve, and the demand function is the compact way to write them all down.

4.1 Non-price determinants

A determinant of demand is any factor that can change quantity demanded at a given price. Own price moves the buyer along the curve; every other determinant shifts it.

DeterminantWhat happensShift
IncomeHigher income raises demand for normal goods and lowers it for inferior goodsRight for normal; left for inferior
Price of a substituteA rise in the substitute's price makes this good relatively cheaperRight
Price of a complementA rise in the complement's price makes the pair costlierLeft
Tastes and preferencesFashion, habits, health awareness and advertising change the desire for the goodRight if favourable; left if not
Expected future priceExpect a rise → buy now; expect a fall → postponeRight if a rise is expected; left if a fall

Test. Ask one question: “Did the good's own price change?” If yes, move along the curve. If no, the curve shifts.

4.2 The demand function

Definition. A demand function shows the functional relationship between quantity demanded and the factors that influence it.

Qd = f(Px, Y, Pr, T, E)

Qd is quantity demanded of X per period, Px its own price, Y consumer income, Pr the price of related goods, T tastes and E the expected future price.

A simple numerical form, holding all determinants except price constant, is Qd = 60 − 5Px.

Price (₹)CalculationQuantity demanded
1060 − 5(10)10
860 − 5(8)20
660 − 5(6)30
460 − 5(4)40
260 − 5(2)50

4.3 Reading the coefficient

The coefficient −5 says each ₹1 rise in price reduces quantity demanded by 5 units. The constant 60 is the quantity that would be demanded at a zero price — useful for the algebra, not for the market.

The same relationship can be written backwards as P = 12 − 0.2Q. Then the slope is −0.20, matching Figure 3.1. Both forms describe one line; the function form puts quantity on the left, the curve form puts price on the vertical axis.

Exam pointer. If income rises and X is normal, the number 60 changes — the whole line shifts right. The −5 slope stays the same only if the responsiveness to price is unchanged.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Name any four determinants of individual demand other than own price.
  2. Q2. For Qd = 60 − 5Px, find quantity at ₹10 and ₹6. If income rises and X is normal, what happens to the curve?
  3. Q3. Own price is a determinant, yet a change in it does not shift the demand curve. Why not?
Show answers
  1. Income, prices of related goods, tastes and preferences, and expectations of future prices.
  2. At ₹10, Qd = 10; at ₹6, Qd = 30. If income rises and X is normal, the curve shifts right.
  3. The curve already records quantity at every price; a change in own price selects another point on the same relationship.
Section 5

Normal and Inferior Goods; Substitutes and Complements

The idea of this section. Two relationships do most of the work in this chapter. Income changes demand in one direction for normal goods and the other for inferior goods. A related good's price changes demand in one direction for substitutes and the other for complements.

5.1 Normal and inferior goods

Definition. Normal goods are goods whose demand increases when income increases and decreases when income decreases; the income effect is positive. Examples are full-cream milk, branded clothing and cars.

Definition. Inferior goods are goods whose demand decreases when income increases and increases when income decreases; the income effect is negative. As income rises, toned milk gives way to full-cream milk and bus travel to taxis.

“Inferior” describes the direction of the income effect, not quality. Toned milk is not bad; it is simply the choice a household leaves behind when income rises.

BasisNormal goodInferior good
Demand when income risesRisesFalls
Income effectPositiveNegative
ExampleFull-cream milkToned milk

5.2 Substitute and complementary goods

Definition. Substitute goods can be used in place of one another to satisfy the same want. They have competitive demand, and the cross-price relation is positive: a rise in the price of coffee raises demand for tea.

Definition. Complementary goods are used together to satisfy a want. They have joint demand, and the cross-price relation is negative: a rise in the price of petrol reduces demand for cars. They need not be used in fixed proportions.

5.3 Cross demand and the direction of the effect

Cross demand is the relationship between the demand for a good and the price of a related good, other things equal: Dx = f(Py).

Change in the marketEffect on demand for XShift of the curve
Price of a substitute risesRises ↑Right
Price of a substitute fallsFalls ↓Left
Price of a complement risesFalls ↓Left
Price of a complement fallsRises ↑Right
Price of an unrelated good changesNo changeNo shift

Sign rule. Substitutes: cross-price effect positive. Complements: cross-price effect negative. If the goods are unrelated, the effect is zero.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Define an inferior good. Does “inferior” mean poor quality?
  2. Q2. A and B are complements, while A and C are substitutes. The price of A rises. What happens to demand for A, B and C?
  3. Q3. Why does demand for tea rise when coffee becomes more expensive?
Show answers
  1. An inferior good is one whose demand falls when income rises and rises when income falls. “Inferior” refers to this negative income effect, not quality.
  2. Quantity demanded of A falls because its own price rose (a movement); demand for B falls because it is a complement; demand for C rises because it is a substitute.
  3. Coffee and tea are substitutes; a higher coffee price makes tea relatively cheaper, so buyers switch and the tea curve shifts right.
Section 6

Change in Quantity Demanded vs Change in Demand

The idea of this section. This is the most examined distinction in the chapter. The test is simple: did the good's own price change? If yes, the buyer moves along the curve. If no, the whole curve shifts.

6.1 Change in quantity demanded

Definition. A change in quantity demanded is a change in the amount purchased due to a change in the good's own price, other factors constant.

It is a movement along the same curve. Expansion means price falls and quantity rises, a movement down-right. Contraction means price rises and quantity falls, a movement up-left.

Memory line. Expansion is good for the buyer's quantity: price down, quantity up. Contraction is the reverse.

6.2 Change in demand

Definition. A change in demand is a change in the amount purchased due to a non-price factor, the price itself constant.

It is a shift of the whole curve. An increase in demand means buyers want more at the same price, so the curve shifts right; a decrease in demand means they want less, so it shifts left.

The shifters are the non-price determinants from Section 4: income, related-good prices, tastes, expectations and, for market demand, population and income distribution.

6.3 The graph

What to notice. In panel (a) there is only one curve; price is the cause and the movement is the effect. In panel (b) price stays at ₹8 while quantity changes, so the whole relationship has moved.

6.4 The distinction

BasisChange in quantity demandedChange in demand
CauseOwn priceA non-price factor
PriceChangesRemains constant
Curve effectMovement along the same curveShift of the whole curve
DirectionExpansion (down-right) or contraction (up-left)Increase (right) or decrease (left)
ExampleTea price falls, more tea is boughtIncome rises, more tea is bought at the same price

Expansion and contraction are movements; increase and decrease are shifts. Mixing these four words is the single most common way to lose marks in this chapter.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Distinguish a change in quantity demanded from a change in demand.
  2. Q2. Classify each event and give the direction: (a) rice price falls, households buy more; (b) income rises, they buy more rice; (c) wheat price rises, rice demand rises; (d) rice price rises, they buy less.
  3. Q3. Why does a change in own price not shift the demand curve?
Show answers
  1. A change in quantity demanded is caused by own price and is a movement along the curve; a change in demand is caused by a non-price factor and is a shift.
  2. (a) Movement, down-right (expansion). (b) Rightward shift. (c) Rightward shift, since wheat is a substitute. (d) Movement, up-left (contraction).
  3. The curve already shows quantity at every price; a new own price selects another point on the same relationship.
Section 7

Market Demand

The idea of this section. A market curve is built by adding buyers, not by adding curves end to end. At every price, add the quantities that each buyer wants.

7.1 Meaning and schedule

Market demand is the total quantity all consumers in a market are willing and able to buy at each price during a given period, other things equal.

Price (₹)A's quantity (units)B's quantity (units)Market demand (units)
1010515
8201535
6302555
4403575
2504595

Every row holds price constant and adds quantities: at ₹6, 30 + 25 = 55 units.

7.2 Deriving the market demand curve

What to notice. At ₹6, A, B and M lie on the same horizontal line at 30, 25 and 55 units, and 30 + 25 = 55. The market curve adds quantities sideways at every price.

QM(P) = qA(P) + qB(P) + ⋯ + qN(P)

QM(P) is market quantity and qA(P) to qN(P) are individual quantities at price P.

Warning. Do not add prices. If A buys 30 at ₹6 and B buys 25 at ₹6, the market does not demand 55 at ₹12. There is one market price, and quantities are added at that price.

When both buyers are active, the market curve is flatter: a price fall raises market quantity by the sum of both responses.

7.3 Determinants of market demand

The shifters are related-good prices, income, tastes, expectations, population size and composition, income distribution, and season and weather.

QM = f(Px, Pr, Y, T, E, N, Yd, S)

QM is market quantity, Px own price, Pr related-good prices, Y average income, T tastes, E expected future price, N population, Yd income distribution, and S season and weather.

Example. A rise in population raises market demand even if every individual buys the same quantity at the same price; the curve shifts right because there are more buyers.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Why is market demand a horizontal sum and not a vertical sum?
  2. Q2. At ₹10, ₹8 and ₹6, A demands 10, 20 and 30 units while B demands 5, 10 and 15. Construct the market schedule.
  3. Q3. Why is the market curve flatter than each individual curve when both buyers are active?
Show answers
  1. All buyers face the same price, so we add the quantities each chooses at that price; a vertical sum would add prices, not quantities.
  2. Market demand is 15 units at ₹10, 30 at ₹8 and 45 at ₹6.
  3. Each buyer's quantity rises, so market quantity rises by the sum of those increases.
Section 8

Exceptions to the Law of Demand

The idea of this section. An exception is a case where a price change moves a factor that was meant to stay constant — usually real income, expectations or knowledge — so quantity demanded fails to fall as the law predicts, and may rise.

8.1 What an exception means

An exception is not proof that the law is wrong. It is a situation in which the ceteris paribus condition fails: the price change itself changes real income, expectations or knowledge. Sometimes price itself is what the buyer values.

8.2 Giffen goods

Definition. A Giffen good is a special kind of inferior good, taking a large share of a poor household's budget, whose demand rises when its price rises and falls when its price falls.

The mechanism has three steps:

  1. The staple's price rises, so the household's real income falls.
  2. The substitution effect says buy less of the dearer staple.
  3. The income effect says buy more, because the household is poorer and the staple is the cheapest food.

When the income effect outweighs the substitution effect, quantity demanded rises with price and the curve slopes upward. A poor family facing a 20% rise in the price of coarse grain may buy more of it and cut back on milk and vegetables.

Two conditions. The good must be inferior and take a large share of the budget. Most inferior goods are not Giffen goods.

8.3 Veblen goods and status goods

Definition. Veblen goods are bought mainly for display of status, and their demand rises when their price rises because the high price is itself part of the attraction.

This is Thorstein Veblen's conspicuous consumption: spending meant to be seen. Price signals exclusivity, so a cheaper version would lose the very thing the buyer wants; designer watches and premium cars are examples.

8.4 Speculation, ignorance and bare necessities

Speculation. If buyers expect a shortage or a further price rise, they buy more now, even at a price that has already risen: rumours of an onion shortage make households stock up at ₹60 per kg.

Ignorance. A buyer who does not know the market price may pay more for the same quantity: a tourist pays ₹40 for water worth ₹20.

Bare necessities. For salt, fuel, insulin and life-saving medicines the required quantity is nearly fixed, so demand is steep, almost vertical: a family needing 1 kg of salt buys it at ₹20 or ₹30. Strictly, quantity is unchanged, not increased.

Exam caution. Bare necessities are sometimes listed as an exception, but they are better described as perfectly inelastic demand. The law predicts a fall; here the fall is simply close to zero.

8.5 Fashion and weather: shifts, not true exceptions

Both often appear on lists of exceptions. A change in fashion changes tastes; a change in season changes the conditions of demand. Both change demand at the same price, so they shift the curve: sweater demand rises in December although price has not fallen.

The law is not refuted: its ceteris paribus condition has been broken. Call these shifts in demand, not exceptions to the law.

8.6 The exception in a graph

What to notice. The curve rises from left to right, the opposite of Figure 3.1; one assumption of the law fails. A single upward-sloping curve cannot tell you which exception it is; the reason must come from the market story.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. What is a Giffen good? Why is it a special kind of inferior good?
  2. Q2. Name the exception: (a) a luxury watch becomes dearer and sells more; (b) a poor family buys more coarse grain when its price rises; (c) buyers rush to buy onions after a shortage rumour; (d) a tourist pays ₹40 for water worth ₹20.
  3. Q3. Explain in three steps why demand for a Giffen good can rise when its price rises.
Show answers
  1. A Giffen good is an inferior good with a large budget share whose demand rises when its price rises; only here does the negative income effect outweigh the substitution effect.
  2. (a) Veblen good. (b) Giffen good. (c) Speculation. (d) Ignorance.
  3. The price rise lowers real income; the substitution effect says buy less of the dearer staple; the income effect says buy more because the household is poorer and it is the cheapest food.
Section 9

Common Mistakes, Recap and Exam Practice

The idea of this section. Most lost marks in this chapter come from four places: calling a want a demand, shifting the curve when only price changed, adding market demand vertically, and calling every inferior good Giffen. Fix those and the rest is practice.

9.1 Common mistakes

MistakeCorrect idea
“Desire is demand.”Demand needs willingness, ability, a price and a time period.
“A price change shifts the demand curve.”A change in own price is a movement along the curve.
“Expansion means the curve shifted right.”Expansion is a movement down-right along the same curve; a rightward shift is an increase in demand.
“Market demand is the vertical sum.”It is the horizontal sum: add quantities at each price.
“All inferior goods are Giffen goods.”Only a special inferior good with a large budget share is Giffen.
“A fall in a substitute's price raises demand for X.”It lowers demand for X; buyers switch to the cheaper substitute.
“Slope of demand is ΔQ/ΔP.”With price on the vertical axis, slope is ΔP/ΔQ and is negative.
“An exception proves the law is false.”An exception means a ceteris paribus condition failed; the law still describes the normal case.

9.2 Chapter recap

  • Demand is the quantity a consumer is willing and able to buy at each price in a given period; it is a flow.
  • The law of demand says price and quantity demanded are inversely related, ceteris paribus, so the curve slopes downward.
  • Diminishing marginal utility, substitution, income, new buyers and alternative uses explain the slope.
  • Own price moves buyers along the curve; income, related-good prices, tastes and expectations shift it.
  • Normal goods have a positive income effect, inferior goods a negative one; substitutes a positive cross-price effect, complements a negative one.
  • Expansion and contraction are movements; increase and decrease are shifts.
  • Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual curves: add quantities at each price, never prices.
  • Giffen goods, Veblen goods, speculation, ignorance and bare necessities are the exceptions; fashion and weather are shifts.

Session 1

Meaning, schedule and the law

The chapter in 12 lines

  • Demand = willingness + ability + price + time period.
  • A desire is a wish; a want adds ability and willingness; demand adds price and time.
  • Demand is a flow, measured per period; a stock is measured at a point of time.
  • Individual demand is one buyer; market demand is all buyers added at each price.
  • A schedule is a table; a curve is its graph.
  • Price goes on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal.
  • Law of demand: price ↑ → quantity demanded ↓, ceteris paribus.
  • Ceteris paribus = other things remaining the same.
  • The law gives direction, not size.
  • It is a tendency, not a universal rule.
  • Slope = ΔP/ΔQ; it is negative.
  • Demand is the whole curve; quantity demanded is one point.

The law and its assumptions

Law of demand: other things being equal, quantity demanded is inversely related to price.

Held constantWhy it matters

Nature of the law: qualitative, not quantitative; not proportional; a tendency, not a universal rule.

Recall drill — Session 1

  1. Define demand and name its four elements.

    Show answer

    The quantity a consumer is willing and able to buy at each possible price during a given period. The four elements are quantity, willingness and ability, price, and time period.

  2. Distinguish desire, want and demand with one example.

    Show answer

    Desire is the wish alone; want adds ability and willingness; demand adds a price and a time period. A student wishing for a laptop, with ₹60,000 saved, who buys one at ₹55,000 this month, passes through all three.

  3. Why is demand a flow and not a stock?

    Show answer

    Demand is measured over a period, such as 40 cups per day. A stock is measured at a point of time, such as 500 cups in the shop today.

  4. Distinguish individual demand from market demand.

    Show answer

    Individual demand is one consumer's quantity at each price. Market demand is the total quantity all consumers buy at each price in a given period.

  5. State the law of demand. What happens to quantity demanded when price falls?

    Show answer

    Other things being equal, quantity demanded is inversely related to price. When price falls, quantity demanded rises.

  6. What does ceteris paribus mean, and why is it needed?

    Show answer

    “Other things remaining the same.” It isolates the price effect so that a change in quantity can be attributed to price rather than to income, tastes or related prices.

  7. Using the schedule, calculate the slope between ₹8 and ₹2 and interpret it.

    Show answer

    ΔP = −6 and ΔQ = 30, so slope = −6/30 = −0.20. A ₹1 fall in price brings 5 more units.

  8. Why is the law of demand called qualitative rather than quantitative?

    Show answer

    It states the direction of the relationship, not its size. It says quantity falls when price rises, not by how much; elasticity measures the size.

Reasons, determinants and related goods

Five reasons for the downward slope

ReasonThe mechanismExample
Diminishing marginal utilityEach extra unit gives less satisfaction, so it is bought only at a lower priceSecond and third samosa

Recall drill — Session 2

  1. Name the five reasons for the downward slope of the demand curve.

    Show answer

    Diminishing marginal utility, the substitution effect, the income effect, new buyers entering the market, and alternative uses of the good.

  2. Explain diminishing marginal utility with an example.

    Show answer

    As a consumer takes more units in a period, each extra unit gives less satisfaction. The first samosa is worth a lot, the fourth much less, so the fourth is bought only at a lower price.

  3. Distinguish the substitution effect from the income effect.

    Show answer

    Substitution works through relative price: buyers switch toward the cheaper good. Income works through real income: the same money buys more, so normal-good demand rises.

  4. For Qd = 60 − 5P, find quantity at ₹7 and at ₹4.

    Show answer

    At ₹7: 60 − 35 = 25 units. At ₹4: 60 − 20 = 40 units.

  5. Income rises. What happens to demand for a normal good and an inferior good?

    Show answer

    Normal good: demand rises, curve shifts right. Inferior good: demand falls, curve shifts left.

  6. Coffee and tea are substitutes; tea and milk are complements. The price of coffee rises. What happens to tea and milk demand?

    Show answer

    Tea demand rises (substitute). Milk demand rises too, because more tea is drunk and milk is used with it. The tea curve shifts right; the milk curve also shifts right.

  7. State the sign of the cross-price effect for substitutes and for complements.

    Show answer

    Substitutes: positive. A rise in Y's price raises demand for X. Complements: negative. A rise in Y's price lowers demand for X.

  8. Why does a change in own price not shift the demand curve?

    Show answer

    The curve already records quantity at every price. A new own price simply selects another point on the same relationship, so the buyer moves along the curve.

Horizontal, not vertical: all buyers face one market price, so quantities add sideways. The market curve is flatter than each individual curve.

Exceptions

ExceptionWhy demand can rise with priceExample
Giffen goodIncome effect outweighs substitution for a poor household's stapleCoarse grain

Recall drill — Session 3

  1. Distinguish a change in quantity demanded from a change in demand.

    Show answer

    A change in quantity demanded is caused by own price and is a movement along the curve. A change in demand is caused by a non-price factor and is a shift of the whole curve.

  2. Classify: (a) rice price falls, households buy more; (b) income rises, they buy more rice; (c) wheat price rises, rice demand rises; (d) rice price rises, they buy less.

    Show answer

    (a) Movement, expansion. (b) Rightward shift. (c) Rightward shift, since wheat is a substitute. (d) Movement, contraction.

  3. Why is expansion not the same as an increase in demand?

    Show answer

    Expansion is a movement down-right along the same curve after a price fall. An increase in demand is a rightward shift of the whole curve after a non-price change.

  4. At ₹6, A demands 30 units and B demands 25. What is market demand?

    Show answer

    30 + 25 = 55 units at ₹6.

  5. Why is market demand a horizontal sum, not a vertical sum?

    Show answer

    Every buyer faces the same price, so we add the quantities each chooses at that price. Adding prices would make no sense because there is one market price.

  6. Explain in three steps why a Giffen good's demand can rise when its price rises.

    Show answer

    The price rise lowers real income; the substitution effect says buy less of the dearer staple; the income effect says buy more because the household is poorer and the staple is the cheapest food. When income dominates, quantity rises with price.

  7. Distinguish a Giffen good from a Veblen good.

    Show answer

    A Giffen good is an inferior staple with a large budget share; its demand rises with price because of the income effect. A Veblen good is a status good; its demand rises with price because the price itself signals exclusivity.

  8. Are fashion and weather exceptions to the law of demand?

    Show answer

    No. They change tastes or market conditions at the same price, so they shift the demand curve. They break ceteris paribus, not the inverse relationship itself.
