Production Function

A production function is the firm's technical recipe: it turns inputs into the maximum output possible. Add one variable factor to a fixed one and output passes through three stages; scale every factor together and the question becomes returns to scale.

The one-line idea: a production function is the technical recipe that turns inputs into output; in the short run one factor is fixed, and in the long run all factors can change.

The core question: how does output change when a firm adds more of one input, or scales up every input?

Section 1

Production and the Inputs Used to Produce

The idea of this section. Production is not only making goods. It is any activity that creates utility, and the inputs used are classified by whether the firm can change them in the short run.

1.1 Meaning of production

Definition. Production is the process of creating utility by transforming inputs into output. It includes goods and services.

Utility is the ability to satisfy a want. A doctor treating a patient, a bus carrying passengers and a baker making bread are all producing, because each creates utility.

Form utility, place utility and time utility all count as production. Moving mangoes from a farm to a city market creates place utility; storing grain for the lean season creates time utility.

Bakery example. A bakery uses flour, eggs and sugar (raw material), an oven and a building (capital), a baker (labour), and the owner's organising effort (entrepreneurship) to produce 500 cakes a day. The cakes are output; everything used to make them is input.

1.2 Factors of production

Inputs are grouped into four factors of production:

  • Land — all natural resources; the reward is rent.
  • Labour — human effort, physical or mental; the reward is wages.
  • Capital — produced means of production such as machines, tools and buildings; the reward is interest.
  • Entrepreneurship — the owner who organises the factors, takes decisions and bears risk; the reward is profit.

Money used to buy a machine is financial capital; the machine itself is physical capital. In economics, capital means the produced input, not the money.

1.3 Fixed and variable factors

A fixed factor is one whose quantity cannot be varied in the short run. A variable factor is one whose quantity can be varied in the short run.

Fixed factorsVariable factors
Factory building, plant and machinery, permanent staff, landRaw material, casual labour, electricity, fuel, packing material

“Fixed” does not mean “not used” or “zero”. It means the quantity is fixed for the period. A machine may be half-used and still be a fixed factor, and fixed factors can be underutilised or overutilised.

1.4 Short run and long run

The short run is a period in which at least one factor is fixed, so output can be changed only by changing variable factors. The long run is a period in which all factors are variable, so output can be changed by changing any factor, including plant size.

BasisShort runLong run
FactorsAt least one fixed; others variableAll variable
Output changes byChanging variable factors onlyChanging all factors
FunctionQ = f(L, K̄)Q = f(L, K)

These are functional categories, not calendar periods. A tea stall may adjust all its factors in a few weeks; a steel plant may need years.

Pointer. If a question says “in the short run”, look for a fixed factor. If it says “in the long run”, every factor can change.

1.5 Production is a flow

Production and inputs are flows: “500 cakes a day” is a flow, while “one oven” is a stock. TP, AP and MP are flows, so a schedule always refers to a stated period.

  3. The short run is defined by the inability to vary all factors, not by the calendar. A tea stall may reach its long run in weeks; a steel plant may take years.
Section 2

The Production Function

The idea of this section. The production function is the technical recipe of the firm. It gives the maximum output possible from given inputs and technology, and its form changes between the two runs.

2.1 Meaning and equation

Definition. The production function is the technical relationship between physical inputs and the maximum output that can be produced in a given period with a given technology.

Q = f(L, K)

Q is maximum output per period, L is labour, K is capital, and f represents the available technology.

“Technical” means physical: the function contains no prices, costs or revenue. “Maximum” means it is a benchmark of technical efficiency; a firm may actually produce less because of poor organisation.

Example. If Q = 5L + 2K, then with L = 2 and K = 10, maximum output is 5(2) + 2(10) = 30 units.

2.2 Short-run and long-run production functions

In the short run, capital is fixed at K̄, so:

Q = f(L, K̄)

This is the short-run production function: one variable factor, used for the law of variable proportions.

In the long run, every factor can change:

Q = f(L, K)

This is the long-run production function: all factors variable, used for returns to scale.

2.3 Assumptions

  1. Technology is constant. A new technique creates a new function.
  2. Inputs are measured in physical units and output is measured per period.
  3. The firm is technically efficient, and inputs are divisible enough to vary by one unit, so the function shows maximum output and smooth curves.
Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. What does “technical relationship” mean?
  2. Q2. For Q = 4L + 3K, find output when L = 5 and K = 2.
  3. Q3. Why does the production function show maximum output rather than actual output?
Show answers
  1. It describes the physical relationship between inputs and output for a given technology, without prices, costs or profit.
  2. Q = 4(5) + 3(2) = 26 units.
  3. It states what is technically possible when inputs are used efficiently. Actual output may be lower because of poor management, absenteeism or wastage.
Section 3

Total, Average and Marginal Product

The idea of this section. TP, AP and MP are three views of the same product data: the total, the per-unit average and the addition from the next unit.

3.1 The three product concepts

Total product (TP) is the total output produced with a given number of inputs in a given period.

Average product (AP) is output per unit of the variable factor.

Marginal product (MP) is the addition to TP when one more unit of the variable factor is employed, fixed factors unchanged.

TP = AP × L AP = TP ÷ L MP = ΔTP ÷ ΔL = TPn − TPn−1

L is units of the variable factor, ΔTP is the change in TP, and ΔL is the change in L (usually one unit). At L = 0, AP and MP are undefined and shown as “—”.

Shirts example. If 4 workers produce 44 shirts and 5 produce 48, the 5th worker's MP is 48 − 44 = 4 shirts, and AP at 5 workers is 48 ÷ 5 = 9.6 shirts per worker.

MP is the difference between consecutive TP values, for example 24 − 10 = 14 and 48 − 50 = −2. AP is TP divided by L: 10, 12, 12, 11, 9.60, 8.33, 6.86.

3.3 Relationships between TP, AP and MP

(a) MP is the slope of TP. MP is the change in TP per unit change in L. When MP is positive, TP rises; when MP is zero, TP is maximum; when MP is negative, TP falls. Rising MP means TP rises at an increasing rate; falling but positive MP means TP rises at a decreasing rate.

(b) AP is the slope of the ray from the origin. For any point on TP, AP = TP ÷ L, which is the slope of a straight line from the origin to that point. The ray is steepest when AP is maximum.

(c) MP cuts AP at AP's maximum. If the new unit's product is above the average, it pulls the average up; if below, it pulls it down; if equal, the average is unchanged.

ConditionEffect on AP
MP > APAP rises
MP = APAP is maximum
MP < APAP falls

This is like a batsman's average: a score above the average raises it, below lowers it, and equal leaves it unchanged.

(d) MP can be zero or negative while AP stays positive. AP equals TP ÷ L, so AP stays positive as long as TP is positive. MP can be zero at TP's maximum or negative when TP falls; after MP drops below AP, both fall, but MP falls faster.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Define AP and MP. Why is MP the slope of TP?
  2. Q2. Find the MP of the 4th unit and AP at 6 units.
  3. Q3. Can AP rise while MP is falling?
Show answers
  1. AP = TP ÷ L; MP = ΔTP ÷ ΔL. The change in output divided by the change in labour is the slope of the TP curve.
  2. MP of the 4th unit = 44 − 36 = 8. AP at 6 units = 50 ÷ 6 = 8.33.
  3. Yes, if MP is falling but still above AP. The new unit pulls the average up even though its own contribution is smaller than the previous unit's.
Section 4

The Law of Variable Proportions

The idea of this section. Keep at least one factor fixed, keep adding units of the variable factor, and output passes through three predictable stages: increasing returns, diminishing returns and negative returns.

4.1 Statement and assumptions

Definition. The law of variable proportions, also called the law of returns to a factor, states that when more units of one variable factor are combined with fixed quantities of other factors, total product first increases at an increasing rate, then increases at a decreasing rate, and finally declines.

In terms of MP: MP first rises, then falls but stays positive, and finally becomes negative.

In simple words. Keep the shop or the acre fixed and keep adding workers. At first extra workers help a lot because the fixed factor was underused. Then each extra worker adds less. Eventually too many workers get in one another's way and output falls.

Assumptions:

  1. Technology is constant.
  2. At least one factor is fixed.
  3. Only one factor is variable.
  4. Units of the variable factor are homogeneous.
  5. Factors are imperfect substitutes for one another.
  6. The variable factor can be divided into small units.

One acre and a fixed tubewell. With one worker many tasks wait; with three, workers can specialise; with ten, they crowd one another and may damage the crop.

A one-line summary says “Stage I: TP rises at an increasing rate.” Strictly, the increasing-rate part ends at the point of inflexion, where MP is maximum; Stage I itself ends later, where AP is maximum. The stage is still called “increasing returns” because MP is above AP and AP is rising.

Exam line. Stage I ends where AP is maximum (MP = AP). Stage II ends where MP = 0 (TP is maximum). Stage III is where MP < 0.

4.3 Where the stages begin and end

The zero of MP lies between 6 and 7 units: the 6th unit adds 2, and the 7th subtracts 2. So Stage III begins after 6 units, and the first unit with a negative MP is the 7th.

4.4 Why a rational producer operates in Stage II

In Stage I, the fixed factor is underutilised and MP is above AP, so AP is still rising. Adding another variable unit raises output per unit — the firm is not making the best use of the fixed factor.

In Stage III, MP is negative: an extra unit reduces total output while still costing money. Reducing the variable factor would actually raise output.

Stage II is the sensible range. MP is positive but falling, TP is still rising, and the fixed factor is fully utilised. The exact point inside Stage II depends on input and output prices; the law only narrows the choice to Stage II.

What to notice. TP peaks at 50 units with 6 units of labour, and the point of inflexion is at 2 units where MP peaks. AP peaks at 12 at 2–3 units, and MP cuts it at 3 units; after that MP is below AP and AP falls. MP crosses zero at the peak of TP, where Stage III begins.

Show answers
  1. As one variable factor increases with other factors fixed, TP first rises at an increasing rate, then at a decreasing rate, then falls; MP rises, then falls but stays positive, then becomes negative. It needs a fixed factor, so it is a short-run law.
  2. L = 4: Stage II, TP 44, MP 8, AP 11. L = 7: Stage III, TP 48, MP −2, AP 6.86.
  3. MP is negative, so an extra unit reduces output while adding cost; reducing the variable factor would raise output. A producer avoiding waste moves back to Stage II.
Section 5

Why the Three Stages Occur

The idea of this section. Every stage has a cause. The fixed factor is the engine: at first it is underused, then it becomes the bottleneck, and finally it is overrun.

5.1 Increasing returns (Stage I)

  • Indivisibility of the fixed factor. Machines, tractors and buildings come in large units that cannot be split. With too few workers part of the fixed factor is idle; adding workers uses more of its capacity, so MP rises.
  • Better utilisation and improved proportion. Additional workers keep the machine busy, and the ratio of variable to fixed factors moves closer to the efficient proportion.
  • Specialisation and division of labour. More workers allow tasks to be split, reducing time lost switching jobs and raising efficiency.

5.2 Diminishing returns (Stage II)

  • Imperfect substitutability. Labour cannot perfectly replace land or machines. As more labour is added, each worker has less fixed factor to work with.
  • The fixed factor becomes a constraint. After the optimum combination, extra workers crowd the fixed capacity; the fixed factor is the bottleneck.
  • Over-utilisation. Too many workers per machine cause waiting, congestion and duplicated effort.

Diminishing returns does not mean negative returns. TP is still rising, only more slowly.

5.3 Negative returns (Stage III)

  • Excessive pressure on the fixed factor. Too many workers on one acre trample the crop, block one another and reduce output.
  • Poor coordination and management. Supervision becomes difficult; workers idle, instructions are duplicated and mistakes increase.
  • Falling efficiency of the variable factor. Each extra unit has less fixed factor to work with, and the physical limit of the fixed factor is eventually reached.

5.4 Why the law applies at all

The fixed factor is the driving force. In the short run the firm cannot change it, so increasing the variable factor changes the proportion between the two. There is a limited range of efficient proportions: before it the variable factor is too scarce, after it too abundant.

The law applies to agriculture, industry and services; only the length of each stage differs. In the long run the firm can expand the fixed factor too, so the constraint disappears and the relevant concept becomes returns to scale.

One-line answer. The law operates because the factor proportions change while at least one factor is fixed — underuse, then optimum, then overcrowding.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Why does indivisibility of the fixed factor cause increasing returns?
  2. Q2. A restaurant has one kitchen and hires more cooks. Explain why MP may rise, then fall, then become negative.
  3. Q3. Why does the law of variable proportions not apply in the long run?
Show answers
  1. A machine comes in a large unit. With few workers part of it is idle; adding workers uses more of its capacity, so each extra worker can add more output than the previous one.
  2. With one or two cooks the kitchen is underused and specialisation raises MP. Beyond capacity, cooks wait for stove space and interfere, so MP falls. With too many, they block one another and spoil dishes, so MP becomes negative.
  3. In the long run all factors are variable. The firm can expand the kitchen and machines along with the workers, so the fixed-factor constraint disappears; the relevant concept is returns to scale.
Section 6

Returns to Scale

The idea of this section. Now every factor changes together. If the firm doubles all inputs, does output more than double, exactly double, or less than double? That is returns to scale — a long-run idea.

6.1 Meaning

Definition. Returns to scale describe how output responds in the long run when all factors are changed in the same proportion.

“Scale” means the size of the operation. If a firm doubles every input and output more than doubles, there are increasing returns to scale. If output exactly doubles, there are constant returns to scale. If output less than doubles, there are diminishing returns to scale.

Double every input → compare output with 2 × old output

Returns to scale requires every factor to be variable, so it is a long-run concept. It is not the law of variable proportions, which holds some factors fixed.

6.2 The three patterns

PatternWhen all inputs are doubledExampleMain reason
Increasing returnsOutput more than doubles1,000 → 2,500Indivisibilities, specialisation, economies of scale
Constant returnsOutput exactly doubles1,000 → 2,000Factors divisible; economies exhausted
Diminishing returnsOutput less than doubles1,000 → 1,700Managerial diseconomies, coordination problems, scarce factors

Diminishing returns to scale does not mean output falls. Output still rises when all inputs increase; it rises less than proportionately.

What to notice. At 2× inputs, IRS output is above the benchmark and DRS output below it. The DRS curve still goes up; it does not turn down.

6.3 Causes

Increasing returns: indivisibilities (large machines and specialised managers used more fully at larger scale); specialisation and division of labour; economies of scale (technical, managerial, marketing and financial); better use of by-products.

Constant returns: factors are divisible and can be replicated exactly; economies of scale are exhausted.

Diminishing returns: managerial diseconomies (coordination and supervision become harder); scarcity of entrepreneurial talent; external diseconomies (input prices rise, transport is congested).

6.4 Difference from the law of variable proportions

BasisLaw of variable proportionsReturns to scale
Time periodShort runLong run
Factors changedOne variable factor; others fixedAll factors
ProportionBetween factors changesAll factors change in the same proportion
StagesIncreasing, diminishing, negativeIncreasing, constant, diminishing
Output at the endCan fall when MP is negativeDoes not fall; rises less than proportionately

Memory line. One factor versus all factors; short run versus long run; changing proportion versus constant proportion.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. Distinguish returns to a factor from returns to scale.
  2. Q2. All inputs double and output rises from 1,000 to 1,800, then to 2,000, then to 2,400. Identify each case.
  3. Q3. Why can diminishing returns to scale occur when every input is doubled?
Show answers
  1. Returns to a factor is the short-run response when one variable factor changes while others are fixed. Returns to scale is the long-run response when all factors change in the same proportion.
  2. 1,800 is less than double: diminishing. 2,000 is exactly double: constant. 2,400 is more than double: increasing.
  3. Management and organisation cannot be doubled perfectly. Coordination, supervision and decision-making become harder as the firm grows, so output can rise less than proportionately even though measurable inputs have doubled.
Section 7

Production Function and Technology

The idea of this section. A production function is a snapshot of one technology. Improve the technology and the whole function changes — the curve shifts, rather than the firm moving along it.

7.1 A new function for a new technology

A production function is a snapshot of the available technology. If technology improves, the same inputs produce more output, so the old function is replaced by a new one: Q = f(L, K) becomes Q′ = g(L, K), with g(L, K) > f(L, K) for the same inputs.

Old: Q = f(L, K) New: Q′ = g(L, K), g(L, K) > f(L, K)

The old function does not disappear because the inputs changed; it is replaced because the technical recipe changed.

7.2 Movement along versus a shift

On a graph, better technology shifts the TP curve upward. A movement along TP comes from changing the variable input with technology unchanged; a shift comes from a change in technology or in the quantity or quality of the fixed factors.

ChangeWhat causes itWhat the graph shows
Movement alongMore or less of the variable factor, technology unchangedDifferent point on the same TP curve
ShiftBetter technology, or more or better fixed factorsWhole TP curve moves up or down

7.3 Short-run and long-run forms

The short-run function Q = f(L, K̄) holds capital fixed and gives returns to a factor. The long-run function Q = f(L, K) allows every factor to change and gives returns to scale.

The long run is not just a longer short run: the firm can choose a different plant size, so the whole set of possible techniques changes.

Pointer. “Technology improved” → shift the curve. “The firm hired two more workers” → move along the curve.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. What happens to a production function when technology improves?
  2. Q2. A factory's function changes from Q = 10L to Q = 14L. Find output at L = 5 before and after.
  3. Q3. Why does a change in the fixed factor change the short-run production function?
Show answers
  1. It is replaced by a new function giving more output for the same inputs; the TP curve shifts upward.
  2. Before: 10(5) = 50 units. After: 14(5) = 70 units. Output rises by 20 units.
  3. The fixed factor is part of Q = f(L, K̄). A bigger machine or more land changes K̄, so the whole relationship between L and Q shifts.

The idea of this section. Every exam table asks the same three things: fill the blanks, mark the stages, and explain the turning points. The arithmetic is short; the labels are where marks are won.

At L = 5: 48 = 44 + 4, and 48 = 9.60 × 5. Check every row with these two identities before you touch the stage labels.

8.2 Locating the stages

Point of inflexion: L = 2, MP = 14 (maximum), TP = 24. Before it TP rises at an increasing rate; after it, at a decreasing rate.

Maximum of AP: AP is 12 at L = 2 and also 12 at L = 3; it falls to 11 at L = 4. The maximum is the plateau at 2–3 units, and the MP = AP crossing is at L = 3, where both equal 12. AP is not maximum at L = 4; by then it has already fallen.

Maximum of TP: TP is 50 at L = 6. The 6th unit adds 2; the 7th subtracts 2. The zero of MP lies between 6 and 7; the first negative MP is at L = 7.

Stage summary:

  • Stage I: 0 to 3 units; MP above AP; AP rises to 12.
  • Stage II: 3 to 6 units; MP positive but below AP; AP falls; TP reaches its maximum.
  • Stage III: after 6 units; MP negative (first at the 7th unit); TP falls.

8.3 The point of inflexion

The point of inflexion is where TP changes from convex (slope increasing) to concave (slope decreasing). MP is maximum there. In the table it is at L = 2, TP = 24, MP = 14.

It is not the Stage I–II boundary. That boundary is at AP's maximum, which occurs later. Between the two points, MP is falling but still above AP, so AP is still rising.

Two turning points, two different curves. MP peaks at the point of inflexion; AP peaks where MP = AP. Never merge the two.

Check yourself3 questions
  1. Q1. What is the point of inflexion, and which curve peaks there?
  2. Q2. Find MP of the 6th unit and AP at 7 units. What do they tell you about the stages?
  3. Q3. Why is AP's maximum not at the point of inflexion?
Show answers
  1. It is where TP changes from increasing at an increasing rate to increasing at a decreasing rate; MP is maximum there.
  2. MP = 50 − 48 = 2; AP = 48 ÷ 7 = 6.86. The 6th unit is the last with positive MP, so TP is maximum after 6 units. At 7 units MP is negative, so Stage III has begun.
  3. AP is maximum where MP = AP, not where MP is maximum. After the point of inflexion, MP falls but stays above AP for a while, so AP keeps rising until MP meets it.
Section 9

Common Mistakes, Recap and Exam Practice

The idea of this section. Most lost marks in this chapter are label errors, not calculation errors. Read the traps once, then test yourself with the exam questions.

9.1 Common mistakes to avoid

MistakeCorrect idea
“The law of variable proportions is a long-run law.”It is short-run: one variable factor, at least one fixed. Returns to scale is long-run.
“AP is maximum where MP is maximum.”MP peaks at the point of inflexion; AP peaks where MP = AP.
“TP falls as soon as MP starts falling.”TP rises while MP is positive; it falls only when MP is negative.
“MP = 0 means output is zero.”MP = 0 means TP is maximum; output is positive.
“AP can become negative.”AP = TP ÷ L; it stays positive as long as TP is positive.
“Short run means less than one year.”Short and long run depend on which factors can be varied, not calendar time.

9.2 Chapter recap

  • Production is creation of utility by transforming inputs into output; it includes services, and inputs and output are flows.
  • Land, labour, capital and the entrepreneur are the factors of production; in the short run they are fixed or variable.
  • The production function Q = f(L, K) is the technical relationship between inputs and maximum output for a given technology. Short run: Q = f(L, K̄), returns to a factor; long run: Q = f(L, K), returns to scale.
  • AP = TP ÷ L and MP = ΔTP ÷ ΔL = TPn − TPn−1; MP is the slope of TP, AP is the slope of the ray from the origin.
  • MP cuts AP at AP's maximum; TP is maximum when MP = 0; TP falls when MP is negative.
  • The law of variable proportions has three stages: increasing, diminishing and negative returns. Stage I ends at AP's maximum; Stage II ends at MP = 0; Stage III has MP < 0.
  • A rational producer operates in Stage II: Stage I underutilises the fixed factor, and Stage III has negative MP.
  • Returns to scale is long-run: increasing, constant or diminishing. A change in technology creates a new production function and shifts TP upward.

Show answer pointers

Very short answer

  1. The technical relationship between physical inputs and maximum output in a given period with a given technology.
  2. A factor whose quantity cannot be varied in the short run, such as a machine or factory building.
  3. AP = 60 ÷ 5 = 12 units.
  4. MP is zero at the maximum of TP; positive before, negative after.
  5. Stage II, diminishing returns to a factor.

Short answer

  1. Short run: at least one factor fixed, Q = f(L, K̄), returns to a factor. Long run: all factors variable, Q = f(L, K), returns to scale.
  2. From L = 1 to 3, MP is above AP, so AP rises; at L = 3, MP = AP = 12 and AP is maximum; after L = 3, MP is below AP, so AP falls.
  3. The law needs a fixed factor. In the long run all factors can be varied, so the long-run counterpart is returns to scale.
  4. Returns to a factor: short run, one variable factor, changing factor proportion. Returns to scale: long run, all factors in the same proportion.

Long answer

  1. Stage I: AP rises, MP is above AP; TP rises, first at an increasing rate, then at a decreasing rate; ends at AP's maximum where MP = AP. Stage II: AP falls, MP is below AP but positive; TP rises at a decreasing rate and peaks where MP = 0. Stage III: MP negative, TP falls. A rational producer stays in Stage II because Stage I leaves the fixed factor underused and Stage III reduces output while adding cost.
  2. Increasing: indivisibility, better utilisation, specialisation, improved factor proportion. Diminishing: imperfect substitutability, fixed-factor constraint, over-utilisation. Negative: excessive pressure, poor coordination, falling efficiency of the variable factor.
  3. Increasing: output more than doubles; indivisibilities and economies of scale. Constant: output exactly doubles; factors divisible, economies exhausted. Diminishing: output less than doubles; managerial diseconomies and coordination problems.

Recall drill — Session 1

  1. Define production and explain why a doctor's consultation is production.

    Show answer

    Production is creating utility by transforming inputs into output. The doctor combines knowledge, time, equipment and medicines to create a service that satisfies a want, so utility is created even though no good is manufactured.

  2. Classify for a bakery in the short run: oven, flour, daily-wage baker, shop building, electricity.

    Show answer

    Fixed: oven and shop building. Variable: flour, daily-wage baker and electricity.

  3. Why is the short run not “any period less than one year”?

    Show answer

    The short run is defined by the inability to vary all factors, not by the calendar. It ends when the firm can change its plant size, which differs across industries.

  4. State the production function. For Q = 5L + 2K, find output when L = 4 and K = 6.

    Show answer

    Q = f(L, K) is the technical relationship between inputs and maximum output for a given technology. Q = 5(4) + 2(6) = 20 + 12 = 32 units.

  5. Why does the production function show maximum output rather than actual output?

    Show answer

    It is a technical benchmark: what is possible when inputs are used efficiently. Actual output may be lower because of poor management, absenteeism or wastage.

  6. TP is 48 with 5 workers. Find AP. Then find the MP of the 5th worker if TP was 44 at 4 workers.

    Show answer

    AP = 48 ÷ 5 = 9.6 units per worker. MP = 48 − 44 = 4 units.

  7. State the average–marginal rule. What does MP = 0 mean for TP?

    Show answer

    MP above AP pulls AP up, MP below AP pulls AP down, and MP = AP at AP's maximum. MP = 0 means TP is at its maximum, not that output is zero.

  8. Can AP rise while MP is falling? Explain in one line.

    Show answer

    Yes — as long as MP is falling but still above AP, the new unit pulls the average up.

Session 2

Law of variable proportions

Statement and assumptions

As more units of one variable factor are combined with fixed quantities of other factors, TP first rises at an increasing rate, then at a decreasing rate, then falls. In MP terms: MP rises, then falls but stays positive, then becomes negative.

  • Technology is constant.
  • At least one factor is fixed.
  • Only one factor is variable; its units are homogeneous.
  • Factors are imperfect substitutes, and the variable factor is divisible.

Short-run law: it exists because factor proportions change while at least one factor is fixed. In the long run all factors can vary, so the counterpart is returns to scale.

The three stages

StageTPMPAPEnds where
I · IncreasingRises, first faster then slowerRises, then falls but stays above APRises to maximumAP maximum, so MP = AP
II · DiminishingRises to maximumFalls, positive and below APFallsMP = 0, so TP maximum
III · NegativeFallsNegativeFalls but stays positiveNo upper limit shown

With the schedule: point of inflexion L = 2 (MP = 14); end of Stage I at L = 3 (MP = AP = 12); end of Stage II at L = 6 (TP = 50, MP about to hit zero); first negative MP at L = 7 (−2).

Do not merge the turns: MP peaks at the point of inflexion; AP peaks where MP = AP; TP peaks where MP = 0.

Why each stage happens

StageMain causes
Increasing returnsIndivisibility of the fixed factor; better utilisation; specialisation and division of labour
Diminishing returnsImperfect substitutability; the fixed factor becomes a constraint; over-utilisation
Negative returnsExcessive pressure on the fixed factor; poor coordination; falling efficiency of the variable factor

Diminishing returns does not mean falling output: TP is still rising, only more slowly. Output falls only in Stage III, where MP is negative.

The rational producer

StageWhy not operate there?
Stage IThe fixed factor is underutilised; adding a variable unit raises output per unit, so the firm should expand
Stage IIThe sensible range: MP positive, TP rising, fixed factor fully used
Stage IIIMP negative: an extra unit reduces output while adding cost, so cutting the variable factor raises output

Exam line: the law narrows the choice to Stage II; the exact point inside it depends on input and output prices.

Recall drill — Session 2

  1. State the law of variable proportions in TP terms and in MP terms.

    Show answer

    TP first rises at an increasing rate, then at a decreasing rate, then falls. MP first rises, then falls but stays positive, then becomes negative.

  2. Why is it a short-run law?

    Show answer

    It requires at least one fixed factor, so that adding the variable factor changes the proportion between factors. That condition exists only in the short run.

  3. Give three assumptions of the law.

    Show answer

    Constant technology, at least one fixed factor, one variable factor with homogeneous units, and factors that are imperfect substitutes. Any three.

  4. Identify the stage: L = 2, L = 3, L = 6, L = 7.

    Show answer

    L = 2: Stage I, at the point of inflexion. L = 3: boundary — AP maximum, Stage I ends and Stage II begins. L = 6: last point of Stage II, TP maximum. L = 7: Stage III, MP negative.

  5. Why does Stage I end at AP's maximum and not at MP's maximum?

    Show answer

    MP peaks at the point of inflexion, but it stays above AP after that, so AP keeps rising. Stage I is defined by rising AP, so it ends only where MP = AP at AP's maximum.

  6. One kitchen, more cooks. Explain rising, then falling, then negative MP.

    Show answer

    With one or two cooks the kitchen is underused and specialisation raises MP. Beyond capacity, cooks wait for stove space and interfere, so MP falls. With too many they block one another and spoil dishes, so MP becomes negative.

  7. Why would a rational producer never stay in Stage III?

    Show answer

    MP is negative, so the last units add cost and reduce output. Cutting the variable factor would raise output and lower cost at the same time.

  8. Can TP rise while MP falls? Explain.

    Show answer

    Yes. As long as MP is positive, each extra unit still adds something to TP, even though it adds less than the previous unit.

Returns to scale + technology + numbers

The doubling test

Returns to scale describe the long-run response when all factors change in the same proportion. Double every input and compare output with 2× the old output.

PatternDoubling all inputsExampleMain reason
Increasing (IRS)Output more than doubles1,000 → 2,500Indivisibilities, specialisation, economies of scale
Constant (CRS)Output exactly doubles1,000 → 2,000Divisible factors; economies exhausted
Diminishing (DRS)Output less than doubles1,000 → 1,700Managerial diseconomies, coordination problems

Careful: diminishing returns to scale still means output rises. It rises less than proportionately; it does not fall.

Recall drill — Session 3

  1. Define returns to scale and explain why it is a long-run concept.

    Show answer

    The response of output when all factors change in the same proportion. Every factor must be variable, which is true only in the long run.

  2. All inputs double; output goes 1,000 → 1,800, then → 2,000, then → 2,400. Identify each.

    Show answer

    1,800 is less than double: diminishing. 2,000 is exactly double: constant. 2,400 is more than double: increasing.

  3. Give two causes of increasing returns to scale.

    Show answer

    Indivisibilities — large machines and specialised managers used more fully; specialisation and division of labour. Economies of scale also count.

  4. Give two causes of diminishing returns to scale.

    Show answer

    Managerial diseconomies — coordination and supervision become harder; scarcity of entrepreneurial talent, or external diseconomies such as rising input prices.

  5. Distinguish returns to a factor from returns to scale on three bases.

    Show answer

    Run: short vs long. Factors: one variable with others fixed vs all factors. Proportion: changing vs constant. Output can fall in the short-run law, but not under diminishing returns to scale.

  6. Q = 10L becomes Q = 14L. Find output at L = 6 before and after, and the change.

    Show answer

    Before: 10(6) = 60 units. After: 14(6) = 84 units. Output rises by 24 units at the same input.

  7. Movement along versus shift: which is which?

    Show answer

    More labour with the same technology is a movement along TP. Better technology, or a bigger or better fixed factor, shifts the whole TP curve.

  8. From the table, find AP's maximum and TP's maximum with values.

    Show answer

    AP is maximum at 12 on the plateau at L = 2–3, with MP = AP at L = 3. TP is maximum at 50 with L = 6, and MP is zero between 6 and 7 units.
